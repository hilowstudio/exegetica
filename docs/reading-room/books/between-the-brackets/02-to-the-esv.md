# Part Two: From the King James to the ESV

## Collating the Manuscripts, 1627–1881

In 1627 Cyril Lucaris, Patriarch of Constantinople, sent Codex Alexandrinus to Charles I, the first manuscript of such age English scholars could study at leisure. Brian Walton's London Polyglot Bible (1654–57) gathered variant readings from it and other sources in its sixth volume. The Puritan theologian John Owen answered in 1659 with *Of the Integrity and Purity of the Hebrew and Greek Text of the Scripture*. Owen granted that copyists had erred in particular copies, but he feared that massed lists of variants would unsettle confidence in Scripture, and he maintained that God had preserved the text in the copies the church possessed. Walton replied that collating the variants served the defense of the text. Both were Protestants committed to the authority of Scripture, and the question they disputed, what the variants imply and what should be done with them, has recurred ever since.

John Mill of Oxford spent some thirty years collating manuscripts, versions, and patristic quotations, and in 1707 reprinted Estienne's 1550 text with an apparatus of the variants he had found. Readers counted about thirty thousand. In 1713 the deist Anthony Collins used the number to argue that Scripture could not be trusted. Richard Bentley of Trinity College, Cambridge, answered that the number of variants grew with the number of manuscripts collated, that most were trivial, and that a text with many witnesses could be established better than one with few.

Scholars began to state rules for choosing among readings. Johann Albrecht Bengel of Tübingen, a Lutheran pietist, kept the received text in his edition of 1734 but graded alternative readings in its margin, grouped manuscripts into families, and stated a principle used ever since: the harder reading is to be preferred, since scribes were likelier to smooth a difficulty than to create one. Johann Jakob Griesbach, whose editions began in 1774, sorted the witnesses into Alexandrian, Western, and Byzantine groups, and in 1796 set out rules including a preference for the shorter reading, on the ground that scribes more often added than omitted.

Karl Lachmann of Berlin published in 1831 the first edition to set the received text aside entirely and build a text directly from ancient witnesses, aiming at the text current in the late fourth century. Constantin von Tischendorf of Leipzig devoted his life to finding manuscripts. At St. Catherine's Monastery at Mount Sinai he obtained forty-three leaves from the Old Testament portion of a very old Greek Bible in 1844, and in 1859 he was shown the rest, including the complete New Testament. Codex Sinaiticus was presented to the Russian tsar and published in 1862; the terms on which it left the monastery remain disputed, and most of it is now in the British Library. Codex Vaticanus, in the Vatican Library for four centuries, became available by stages: an unreliable edition in 1857, Tischendorf's edition of 1867, and a photographic facsimile in 1889–90.

In England, Samuel Prideaux Tregelles published a critical text (1857–72) on principles close to Lachmann's, while Frederick Scrivener, the most thorough English collator of his generation, gave more weight to later manuscripts and departed from the received text more slowly. By 1870 most of the leading editors held that the oldest manuscripts often differed from the Textus Receptus, and that in those places the oldest were usually closer to the original.

## Westcott, Hort, and the Revised Version

On 10 February 1870 the Convocation of Canterbury voted to revise the Authorized Version. Its New Testament company, chaired by Bishop Charles Ellicott, included Brooke Foss Westcott and Fenton John Anthony Hort of Cambridge, and Scrivener. The rules directed the revisers "to introduce as few alterations as possible into the Text of the Authorized Version consistently with faithfulness," to adopt the Greek text "for which the evidence is decidedly preponderating," and to note in the margin where it differed from the text behind the KJV. A change required a two-thirds majority at the final review. The Revised New Testament appeared on 17 May 1881 and sold in enormous numbers; the Old Testament followed in 1885, and an edition reflecting the American committee's preferences, the American Standard Version, in 1901.

Westcott and Hort had worked on a Greek text since 1853 and supplied it in proof to the revisers. Their *New Testament in the Original Greek* appeared in May 1881, days before the Revised Version, and Hort's Introduction followed in 1882.

Hort began from a principle of method: "Knowledge of documents should precede final judgement upon readings." A reading's value depends on the character of its witnesses, and that character is learned by studying each witness's readings as a whole. Genealogy followed. Manuscripts copied from a common ancestor bear that ancestor's testimony once, however many survive, so the number of manuscripts supporting a reading does not by itself establish it. Hort grouped the witnesses into kinds of text: Syrian (his name for the Byzantine), Western, Alexandrian, and a "Neutral" text, best represented by Vaticanus and Sinaiticus.

He then argued that the Syrian text was the latest of these. It contains conflate readings, which combine two earlier ones: at Luke 24:53 some early witnesses say the disciples were in the temple "blessing God," others "praising God," and the Byzantine text, which the KJV translates, has "praising and blessing God." He argued that readings distinctive of the Syrian text do not appear in Christian writers before about 350, and he proposed that it arose from a revision made near Antioch in the fourth century.

Hort formed these views early. In a private letter of 1851, at twenty-three, he called the received text "that vile Textus Receptus leaning entirely on late MSS." Defenders of the received text often quote the letter as evidence of prejudice; Hort's defenders answer that his published arguments stand or fall on their evidence.

### Burgon's reply

The most forceful opponent of the new text was John William Burgon, Dean of Chichester, who had already defended Mark 16:9–20 in *The Last Twelve Verses of the Gospel According to S. Mark* (1871). His articles against the Revised Version, collected in 1883 as *The Revision Revised*, argued that the true text is attested by the agreement of witnesses across time and place: the mass of Greek manuscripts, the ancient versions, and the Fathers taken together. He held that Vaticanus and Sinaiticus were among the most corrupt copies in existence and survived because they had been set aside as faulty; that a text read by the Greek church for fifteen centuries could not be a late corruption; and that God's providence made it unlikely the true text had lain hidden in a few manuscripts until the nineteenth century. Inside the revision company, Scrivener often voted against Westcott and Hort's proposals and was often outvoted.

The Revised Version never replaced the KJV in English pulpits. Its Greek text had a longer life: through the American Standard Version and its successors, the judgments of 1881, adjusted by later evidence, passed into most English translations of the twentieth century.

## The Twentieth-Century Text

The century after Westcott and Hort brought evidence they never saw. Excavations at Oxyrhynchus in Egypt from 1896 recovered thousands of papyri, among them New Testament fragments. In the early 1930s Alfred Chester Beatty acquired three substantial codices: P45 (Gospels and Acts) and P47 (Revelation), both of the third century, and P46 (Paul's letters, around 200). Between 1956 and 1961 the Bodmer Library near Geneva published P66 (John, around 200), P72 (1 and 2 Peter and Jude), and P75 (Luke and John).

P75 changed the debate over Hort's theory. Usually dated around 200, though some recent scholars place it later, it agrees so closely with Vaticanus that the text of Vaticanus must already have existed, and been copied with care, long before Vaticanus was made. Other parts of Hort's theory did not hold. Scholars dropped the label "Neutral," since the Alexandrian text shows editorial traits of its own. Hort had granted that the Western text was very old but judged it corrupt; later scholars gave its early readings more weight. No evidence for a formal fourth-century revision of the Byzantine text came to light. And some readings once considered distinctively Byzantine turned up in early papyri, a point pressed by Harry Sturz in 1984. A reading's presence in the Byzantine text is no evidence that it is late, and modern critics weigh such readings one at a time.

In 1898 Eberhard Nestle published a compact Greek New Testament whose text followed the majority of three nineteenth-century editions; Kurt Aland later carried it on. In 1966 the United Bible Societies published *The Greek New Testament* for translators, edited by Aland, Matthew Black, Bruce Metzger, and Allen Wikgren. Its apparatus reports variants that matter for translation and rates the committee's confidence in each decision from A to D, and Metzger's *Textual Commentary* (1971; second edition 1994) records the committee's reasoning. Since the 1970s the Nestle-Aland and UBS editions have printed the same text; as of 2026 the current editions are the twenty-eighth Nestle-Aland (2012) and the fifth UBS (2014), and a twenty-ninth Nestle-Aland in preparation will adopt the *Editio Critica Maior*'s changes in Mark, Acts, and Revelation. The twenty-eighth edition changed the text of James through Jude in some three dozen places, following the *Editio Critica Maior*, a large edition prepared at Münster with a computer-assisted method of comparing manuscripts. One change bears on this book: at Jude 5 it reads "Jesus," where the previous edition read "the Lord." The ESV had read "Jesus" since 2001, eleven years earlier, a reminder that its translators sometimes depart from their Greek base text.

Other critical editions stand alongside these. The SBL Greek New Testament (2010) and the Tyndale House Greek New Testament (2017) differ from Nestle-Aland in hundreds of places, most of them small. The Tyndale House edition prints "and fasting" at Mark 9:29, and the SBL edition prints Romans 16:24, though Nestle-Aland omits both. Critical editors disagree among themselves.

### The method

The method of these editions is called *reasoned eclecticism*: the editor chooses reading by reading, following no single manuscript or family throughout. External evidence concerns the witnesses, their age, geographical spread, and general character; a reading found in early witnesses from several regions is preferred to one found in a single region or only late. Internal evidence concerns the readings. Transcriptional probability asks what a scribe was likely to do: harmonize parallels, smooth a hard expression, add a familiar phrase. The reading that best explains how the others arose is preferred. Intrinsic probability asks what the author was likely to write, given his vocabulary, style, and argument.

These rules are applied with judgment, and their application is debated. The preference for the shorter reading, for example, has been qualified by James Royse's study of the earliest papyri (2008), which found that their scribes left words out more often than they added them. The UBS edition rates its decisions from A to D because its editors did not regard every decision as equally certain.

## From the Revised Standard Version to the ESV

In 1937 the International Council of Religious Education, which held the copyright of the American Standard Version, authorized a revision. A committee chaired by Luther Weigle of Yale published the Revised Standard Version New Testament in 1946 and the whole Bible in 1952; the copyright passed to the National Council of Churches. The RSV's first New Testament placed Mark 16:9–20 and John 7:53–8:11 in footnotes and omitted the second half of Luke 22:19 and verse 20, following Westcott and Hort. Its second edition, in 1971, restored all three passages to the text.

Conservative Protestants criticized the RSV sharply, above all its rendering "young woman" in Isaiah 7:14, where the KJV had "virgin." The quarrel concerned translation more than the Greek text, and it sent evangelical publishers in search of other Bibles: the New American Standard Bible (1971) and the New International Version (1978). The New King James Version (1982) modernized the KJV's English while keeping the Textus Receptus, and its footnotes report where the critical text (marked NU) and the Majority Text (marked M) differ. The NKJV shows that a modern translation can render the received text. Most of the differences this book describes arise from the Greek behind each translation.

In the 1990s Lane T. Dennis, president of the evangelical publisher Crossway, and a group of scholars planned a translation in the line of the RSV, and of the KJV before it, that would reflect evangelical convictions. In 1998 Crossway obtained permission to use the 1971 RSV as its starting point. A fourteen-member Translation Oversight Committee directed the work, with J. I. Packer as general editor and Leland Ryken as literary stylist, and more than fifty scholars reviewed the translation. The ESV was published in 2001 and lightly revised in 2007, 2011, 2016, and 2025; the 2025 update changed 68 words and 57 footnotes. In August 2016 Crossway announced that the 2016 text would remain unchanged permanently, and in September it withdrew the announcement.

The current preface states that the Old Testament is based on the Masoretic text of *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* (1997) and the New Testament on the 2014 UBS edition and the twenty-eighth Nestle-Aland (2012); the 2001 edition used the editions then current. In exceptional cases the translators consulted the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Septuagint, and other ancient sources. The preface calls the footnotes "an integral part of the ESV translation," reporting textual variations and how they were resolved.

From the critical editions the ESV received its Greek text, with the omissions described in Chapter 1. From the 1971 RSV it received the practice of printing the long passages of Mark and John in the text while setting them apart.

### How the ESV marks its judgments

The ESV marks its choices in a small number of ways, and a reader who knows them can tell what the translators decided in each place:

1. Double brackets with a notice, for the two long passages judged later than their books yet ancient and long read in the church, Mark 16:9–20 and John 7:53–8:11. The Nestle-Aland text also prints both in double brackets.
2. A vacant verse number with a footnote, where a whole verse is judged an addition: "Some manuscripts insert verse 21: *But this kind never comes out except by prayer and fasting*" (Matthew 17:20).
3. "Some manuscripts add," for phrases judged additions: "Or *the evil one*; some manuscripts add *For yours is the kingdom and the power and the glory, forever. Amen*" (Matthew 6:13).
4. "Some manuscripts omit," where the translators print words some witnesses lack: "Some manuscripts omit verses 43 and 44" (Luke 22:44).
5. "Some manuscripts" with an alternative, where they chose between readings: "Some manuscripts *although you fully knew it, that the Lord who once saved*" (Jude 5).
6. No note at all, at 1 John 5:7–8.

The phrase "some manuscripts" covers very different situations. At Matthew 6:13 the manuscripts that add the doxology are most of the Greek manuscripts in existence; at Acts 8:37 they are a small minority. A footnote records that a choice was made. The evidence behind the main choices follows.
