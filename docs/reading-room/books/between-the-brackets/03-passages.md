# Part Three: The Passages

## The Ending of Mark

Mark 16:8 ends, "And they went out quickly, and fled from the sepulchre; for they trembled and were amazed: neither said they any thing to any man; for they were afraid." In the KJV twelve more verses follow. Jesus appears to Mary Magdalene, to two disciples walking into the country, and to the eleven at table. He commissions them to preach the gospel to every creature, promises signs that will follow those who believe, and is received up into heaven.

These verses stand in the great majority of Greek manuscripts that contain the end of Mark, including Codices Alexandrinus, Ephraemi, and Washingtonianus, all of the fourth or fifth century, and Codex Bezae, whose original hand breaks off at verse 15. They stand in most manuscripts of the Latin, Syriac, and Coptic versions as well, though some of the oldest copies in those languages lack them. Irenaeus, writing around 180, quotes Mark 16:19 and names Mark as its author (*Against Heresies*, book 3). Tatian's harmony of the Gospels, the *Diatessaron* (around 170), survives only in later forms, and those include the passage; Justin Martyr, a generation earlier, may echo verse 20. The passage was read in the churches of East and West through most of Christian history.

Against this stand the two oldest Greek manuscripts that contain the end of Mark, Vaticanus and Sinaiticus, which stop at verse 8. In Vaticanus the scribe left the rest of the column and the whole next column blank, the only blank column in its New Testament, which some take as a sign that the scribe knew of further text. The oldest Syriac manuscript, many Armenian manuscripts, and the oldest Georgian manuscripts also end at verse 8. Eusebius of Caesarea, early in the fourth century, wrote that the accurate copies ended with "for they were afraid" and that the verses following were missing from almost all copies (*To Marinus*). Jerome repeated the observation.

A third form complicates the picture. A shorter ending of a sentence or two, reporting that the women told Peter's companions what they had been commanded and that Jesus afterward sent out the proclamation of salvation "from east to west," follows verse 8 in several manuscripts. One Old Latin manuscript has it alone; several Greek manuscripts of the seventh to ninth centuries give the shorter ending followed by the longer, some with notes about the endings. Codex Washingtonianus adds a further passage after verse 14. Some manuscripts that contain the longer ending mark it with notes or symbols indicating that some copies lack it.

Scholars who regard the longer ending as an addition point to vocabulary and style that differ from the rest of Mark, and to its opening: verse 9 introduces Mary Magdalene as if for the first time, though she is named in 16:1, and does not continue the story of the women. They read the passage as a summary of appearances known from the other Gospels and Acts. Its defenders answer that vocabulary counts in twelve verses prove little, that a Gospel ending on the women's frightened silence is improbable, and that Irenaeus places the passage in Mark within about a century of its writing. Burgon made these arguments at length in 1871, and later scholars, among them Maurice Robinson and Nicholas Lunn (*The Original Ending of Mark*, 2014), have restated them. Among those who think the passage was added, some hold that Mark meant to end at verse 8 and others that his original ending was lost.

The KJV prints the passage without remark. The ESV prints it in double brackets under the notice "Some of the earliest manuscripts do not include 16:9–20," with a footnote describing the ending at verse 8, the shorter ending, and the addition after verse 14. The word "some" probably registers that the earliest evidence is divided: the two oldest Greek copies end at verse 8, while Irenaeus, earlier than both, knew the longer ending.

## The Woman Taken in Adultery

John 7:53–8:11 tells how scribes and Pharisees brought to Jesus a woman taken in adultery, how he wrote on the ground and said, "He that is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone at her," and how, when her accusers had gone out, he said, "Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more."

The passage is absent from the two oldest manuscripts that contain this part of John, the papyri P66 and P75, both from around 200, and from Vaticanus and Sinaiticus. Alexandrinus and Ephraemi have lost their leaves at this point, but measurement of the missing leaves indicates that neither contained it. Two later majuscules that lack it leave a blank space where it would stand. It is absent from the oldest Syriac and Coptic versions and from several Old Latin manuscripts, and no Greek commentator on John discusses it before the twelfth century.

It is present in Codex Bezae (around 400), in most later Greek manuscripts, and in the Vulgate. Jerome, who kept it in his revision, wrote that it was found "in many Greek and Latin manuscripts" (*Against the Pelagians* 2.17). Augustine suggested that some readers had removed it, fearing it would seem to give wives license to sin. A story of a sinful woman whom Jesus did not condemn appears in a third-century church order, the *Didascalia Apostolorum*, and Eusebius reports that Papias, early in the second century, told of a woman accused before the Lord of many sins (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.17).

Where the passage appears, its position varies. Most manuscripts that have it place it after John 7:52. Others place it after 7:36, after 7:44, or after 21:25; one family of Greek manuscripts places it in Luke, after Luke 21:38. Many manuscripts that contain it mark it with symbols of doubt, and its wording varies more than the text around it.

Most critical scholars conclude that the account is an early and widely known tradition about Jesus that circulated outside the Gospels and entered John's Gospel in some copies by the late fourth century. Many regard it as a true account of something Jesus did. Its vocabulary and style are closer to Luke's than to John's. Defenders of its place in John, from Burgon to Edward Hills and Maurice Robinson, argue that it was removed. The Greek church's reading for Pentecost ran from John 7:37 to 7:52 and resumed at 8:12, passing over the account, and they argue that this practice could have led copyists to drop it. They also cite Augustine's report.

The KJV prints the passage without remark. The ESV prints it in double brackets under the notice "The earliest manuscripts do not include 7:53–8:11," with the footnote, "Some manuscripts do not include 7:53–8:11; others add the passage here or after 7:36 or after 21:25 or after Luke 21:38, with variations in the text." The notice says "the earliest manuscripts" here and "some of the earliest" at Mark 16, probably because every Greek manuscript older than Codex Bezae lacks the passage.

## The Three Heavenly Witnesses

The words in 1 John 5:7–8 that the KJV prints and the ESV lacks are called the *Comma Johanneum*, the "Johannine clause." In the KJV the passage reads, with the clause in bold:

> For there are three that bear record **in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one. And there are three that bear witness in earth,** the Spirit, and the water, and the blood: and these three agree in one.

The clause appears in fewer than a dozen known Greek manuscripts, all from the late Middle Ages or later, and in several it was added in the margin by a later hand. The earliest that carry it in the text, such as minuscule 629 of the fourteenth century, are bilingual or show the influence of the Latin. It is absent from the Byzantine majority and quoted by no Greek Father. Its absence from the Greek writers of the fourth century, who argued over the Trinity at length and cited every text they thought relevant, is taken by most scholars as evidence that they did not know it.

The clause's history lies in the Latin church. The earliest clear quotation is in the *Liber Apologeticus* of Priscillian, a Spanish bishop, around 380. Cyprian of Carthage, around 250, wrote that it is written of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, "and these three are one"; whether he was quoting the clause or reading verse 8's Spirit, water, and blood as a figure of the Trinity, as some later Latin writers did, is disputed. The words spread through manuscripts of the Vulgate from the early Middle Ages, though the oldest, Codex Fuldensis (546) and Codex Amiatinus (around 700), lack them. Fuldensis contains a prologue to the Catholic Letters, falsely ascribed to Jerome, complaining that unfaithful translators had left out the testimony of the Father, the Word, and the Spirit. The clause appears in varying forms and positions in Latin manuscripts. By the later Middle Ages it was standard in the Latin Bible, and the Fourth Lateran Council of 1215 quoted it.

The Complutensian Polyglot printed a Greek form of the clause matching the Latin. Erasmus added it in 1522, Estienne and Beza kept it, and it passed into the KJV. Luther's German New Testament, made from Erasmus's 1519 edition, lacked it in his lifetime. The Revised Version of 1881 removed it, as have most English translations since. The NKJV keeps it and notes the other texts; the NIV gives the words in a footnote, stating that they are found in no Greek manuscript before the fourteenth century.

The clause has had defenders in every century since Erasmus. In the eighteenth century the Greek scholar Eugenios Voulgaris argued from grammar that the masculine "three" of verse 8, applied to the neuter nouns Spirit, water, and blood, requires the preceding masculine nouns the clause supplies; the American Presbyterian Robert L. Dabney made a similar argument in 1871. Defenders also cite Cyprian and the Latin tradition as evidence that the words are early, and argue that they could have been lost from the Greek by accident or removed during the Arian controversy. Most textual scholars, including many who hold the doctrine of the Trinity with full conviction, judge the clause a Latin gloss that entered the text. The ESV prints no footnote here, and its preface does not explain the absence of one.

## The Vacant Verses

Twelve of the sixteen stand in the Byzantine majority and four do not. In the twelve, the oldest manuscripts and the majority disagree, and an editor's choice follows from the method the editor holds. In the four, the Textus Receptus departs from the majority as well.

The manuscripts that contain Matthew 23:14 place it either before verse 13 or after it, and some give Luke 23:17 after verse 19. Critics judge material that moves to have been added, since a copyist inserting a marginal note had to decide where it went; defenders answer that displacement does not settle origin.

John 5:3b–4, the angel troubling the pool, is present in Alexandrinus and the Byzantine majority and was known to Tertullian around 200 (*On Baptism* 5). It is absent from P66, P75, Vaticanus, Sinaiticus, and other early witnesses, and many manuscripts that contain it mark it with symbols of doubt. Its defenders note that the sick man's complaint in verse 7, "I have no man, when the water is troubled, to put me into the pool," presupposes the explanation verse 4 gives; critics reply that verse 4 was written to explain verse 7.

Acts 8:37 appears in Codex Laudianus of the sixth century, in a minority of later Greek manuscripts, and in Latin witnesses, and Irenaeus knew the confession. It is absent from the earliest Greek manuscripts and from the Byzantine majority. Critics suggest it entered as a baptismal confession, supplied where the narrative passes directly from the eunuch's question, "what doth hinder me to be baptized?", to his baptism.

Romans 16:24 belongs to a larger puzzle. The doxology now numbered 16:25–27 stands after 14:23 in many Byzantine manuscripts, after 15:33 in P46, at the end of chapter 16 in the oldest majuscules, in two places in some, and nowhere in others, and the grace benediction of verse 24 moves with it. The SBL Greek New Testament prints the verse; the Nestle-Aland text does not.

## Shorter Readings and Longer Ones

Below the level of whole verses the same pattern repeats. Many differences between the KJV and the ESV are a phrase or a single word, and they fall into the same two groups as the vacant verses: places where the oldest manuscripts and the Byzantine majority disagree, and places where the Textus Receptus stands apart from both.

The close of the Lord's Prayer in Matthew 6:13, "For thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, for ever. Amen," is absent from Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Bezae, and most early Latin copies, and present in most later Greek manuscripts. The Sahidic Coptic version and one Old Latin copy have shorter forms of it. The *Didache*, a church manual of the late first or early second century, closes its form of the prayer with "for thine is the power and the glory for ever," which shows that the prayer was ended with a doxology in worship very early. Critics hold that the close entered Matthew's text from worship; defenders hold that it belongs to Matthew and was dropped in some copies. In Luke's version of the prayer (Luke 11:2–4) the KJV adds clauses that match Matthew, and the ESV gives Luke's shorter form.

When James and John want to call down fire on a Samaritan village, the KJV has Jesus say, "Ye know not what manner of spirit ye are of. For the Son of man is not come to destroy men's lives, but to save them" (Luke 9:55–56). The ESV, with the oldest manuscripts, says only that he turned and rebuked them, and gives the longer words in a footnote. Many later Greek manuscripts have them, though those manuscripts are divided.

Mark 9:29 shows a smaller addition. The KJV has "by prayer and fasting," the ESV "by prayer." The Byzantine text and the Tyndale House edition have "and fasting"; Sinaiticus and Vaticanus lack it. Romans 8:1 in the KJV ends with words that every text has at Romans 8:4, "who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit." The Byzantine text has them at 8:1 as well, and the oldest manuscripts lack them there. In Colossians 1:14 the KJV reads "redemption through his blood" and the ESV "redemption." The phrase stands at Ephesians 1:7 in every text, but at Colossians 1:14 it is found in the Textus Receptus and a few late manuscripts and is absent from the Byzantine majority.

The difference at 1 Timothy 3:16 began with two letters. The KJV reads "God was manifest in the flesh," the ESV "He was manifested in the flesh." In majuscule script "God" was abbreviated ΘΣ with a line above, and the pronoun "who" was written ΟΣ; the two differ by a stroke inside the first letter and the line above. Most later manuscripts read "God," and the oldest majuscules whose reading is clear, among them Sinaiticus, read "who." The ESV's footnote reports both.

The critical text is sometimes the longer one, and some of its readings state a doctrine more directly. Where the KJV has "the only begotten Son, which is in the bosom of the Father" at John 1:18, the 2025 ESV has "God the only Son, who is at the Father's side." The reading "God" (*theos*) stands in P66, P75, Vaticanus, and Sinaiticus, and "Son" (*huios*) in Alexandrinus and the Byzantine majority. In the ESV's rendering, "only Son" translates the single word *monogenēs*, and printings from 2001 to 2024 read "the only God." At Jude 5 the KJV says that the Lord saved the people out of Egypt, and the ESV that Jesus did. The ESV adds "and so we are" to 1 John 3:1, "that we should be called children of God; and so we are," and "into salvation" to 1 Peter 2:2, where the KJV has "that ye may grow thereby." Where the KJV opens Mark 1:2 with "As it is written in the prophets," the ESV has "As it is written in Isaiah the prophet." The quotation that follows joins Malachi 3:1 to Isaiah 40:3. The oldest manuscripts name only Isaiah, the Byzantine "in the prophets" removes the difficulty, and critics take it as a harder reading changed to an easier one.

### The Old Testament

Both Old Testaments rest on the Masoretic text, the Hebrew text transmitted by the Jewish scholars called Masoretes, who between the sixth and tenth centuries added vowel signs, accents, and notes to a consonantal text fixed long before. The KJV translators used printed editions based on late medieval manuscripts. The ESV uses *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia*, which reproduces the Leningrad Codex of 1008 or 1009, the oldest complete manuscript of the Hebrew Bible. These two forms of the Masoretic text differ little. The Dead Sea Scrolls, found from 1947 onward, supplied manuscripts a thousand years older. The Great Isaiah Scroll agrees closely with the Masoretic Isaiah; elsewhere the scrolls sometimes agree with the Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation, against the Masoretic text.

The ESV departs from the Masoretic text only in exceptional cases, and in some it prints more than the KJV. Psalm 145 is an acrostic, each verse beginning with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, and the Masoretic text lacks the verse for the letter *nun*. The ESV supplies it at the end of verse 13, "The LORD is faithful in all his words and kind in all his works," from one Hebrew manuscript, the Septuagint, and the Syriac, with support from a Dead Sea scroll. At Deuteronomy 32:8, where the KJV says God set the bounds of the peoples "according to the number of the children of Israel," the ESV reads "according to the number of the sons of God," following a Dead Sea scroll and the Septuagint.
