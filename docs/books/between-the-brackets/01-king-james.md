## To the Reader

Open an English Standard Version to Matthew 17 and read from verse 20 to verse 22. There is no verse 21. Turn to the last chapter of Mark and you will find twelve verses inside double brackets beneath a notice that some of the earliest manuscripts do not include them. Open a King James Version to the same pages and the text runs on without a break.

This book explains why. It tells how the King James Version came to be in the first decade of the seventeenth century, how the English Standard Version came to be at the turn of the twenty-first, and what happened in the four centuries between. It describes the evidence, the people who weighed it, and the reasons they gave. It does not argue for one Bible against the other, and where the evidence leaves a question open, the book leaves it open.

The subject is the text: which Greek and Hebrew words stand behind each translation. Differences of English style belong to another book. Christians who read either Bible confess that God has spoken and that his word is found in the writings of the prophets and apostles. They differ over where the exact words of the New Testament are to be found and how a reader can know them. That difference has a history, and this is an account of it.

# Part One: The King James Version

## What the Reader Sees

When the English Standard Version (ESV) prints less than the King James Version (KJV), or prints something different, the change can be as long as a dozen verses or as short as a single word.

The longest changes concern two passages. Mark 16:9–20 closes Mark in the KJV with the risen Lord's appearances, his commission to the eleven, and his ascension, and John 7:53–8:11 tells of the woman taken in adultery. The ESV prints both in full but encloses each in double brackets beneath a notice. Above Mark 16:9 the notice reads, "Some of the earliest manuscripts do not include 16:9–20"; above John 7:53 it reads, "The earliest manuscripts do not include 7:53–8:11." The wording probably differs because the evidence differs, and Chapters 9 and 10 set that evidence out.

In sixteen places the ESV skips a verse number altogether and gives the verse, in its own translation, in a footnote: Matthew 17:21; 18:11; 23:14; Mark 7:16; 9:44; 9:46; 11:26; 15:28; Luke 17:36; 23:17; John 5:4; Acts 8:37; 15:34; 24:7; 28:29; and Romans 16:24. More often the difference is a phrase. Many ESV verses lack a clause the KJV has, usually with a footnote that begins "Some manuscripts add," and the best known is the close of the Lord's Prayer in Matthew 6:13, "For thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, for ever. Amen." Elsewhere the two Bibles read different words. At 1 Timothy 3:16 the KJV has "God was manifest in the flesh" and the ESV "He was manifested in the flesh"; at Revelation 22:19 the KJV has "the book of life" and the ESV "the tree of life."

At 1 John 5:7–8 the ESV changes the text without any note. The KJV reads, "For there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one. And there are three that bear witness in earth, the Spirit, and the water, and the blood: and these three agree in one." The ESV reads, "For there are three that testify: the Spirit and the water and the blood; and these three agree." In a few places the ESV leans the other way and keeps words its own Greek edition marks as doubtful. Luke 22:43–44, the angel strengthening Jesus in Gethsemane and his sweat like great drops of blood, stands in the ESV text with the footnote, "Some manuscripts omit verses 43 and 44."

The traffic also runs the other way. The ESV prints "and so we are" in 1 John 3:1, "into salvation" in 1 Peter 2:2, "Isaiah the prophet" in Mark 1:2 where the KJV has "the prophets," "God" in John 1:18 ("God the only Son" in the 2025 ESV text; "the only God" in earlier printings) where the KJV has "the only begotten Son," and "Jesus" in Jude 5 where the KJV has "the Lord." In the Old Testament it adds a line to Psalm 145:13. Chapter 13 returns to these.

Verse numbers are a printer's addition. The Paris printer Robert Estienne, known by his Latin name Stephanus, numbered the verses of his Greek and Latin New Testament of 1551, and the Geneva New Testament of 1557 carried his numbering into English. Every English Bible since has kept it, because commentaries, concordances, and sermons depend on it. When modern editors concluded that a verse in Estienne's text was a later addition, translators left its number empty. A vacant verse number marks a place where the Greek text printed in the sixteenth century and the Greek text printed today part company.

The differences start with the Greek. The KJV New Testament was translated from Greek editions printed between 1516 and 1598, a family later called the *Textus Receptus*, the "received text." The ESV New Testament was translated from the critical editions of the German Bible Society and the United Bible Societies, which draw on manuscripts the KJV translators never saw. Each of these printed texts reflects the manuscripts its editors could consult and the methods they trusted for choosing among them. The translators then decided how to show a reader where the evidence was divided. The KJV of 1611 did this in a few marginal notes about the Greek copies, and the ESV does it with brackets and footnotes.

How much is in question can be counted. The *Translators Amalgamated Greek New Testament*, compiled by the STEPBible project at Tyndale House, Cambridge, sets side by side the words of eight printed Greek editions: the 27th and 28th Nestle-Aland, the Textus Receptus (Scrivener's 1894 text), the Byzantine text (Robinson-Pierpont), and the SBL, Tyndale House, Westcott-Hort, and Tregelles editions. The summary in its data files counts roughly 142,000 Greek words, of which about 133,600, or 94 percent, are the same in all eight apart from spelling. About 4,200 words appear in the Textus Receptus and not in the Nestle-Aland text behind the ESV, and the compilers judge that some 2,350 of these change an English translation. About 900 words run the other way, of which some 270 change a translation. These figures measure how much is in question without deciding which text is right.

A few terms recur. A *variant* is a place where manuscripts differ, and each form of the text there is a *reading*. A *witness* is any source for the text: a Greek manuscript, an ancient translation (a *version*), or a quotation in an early Christian writer. A *critical text* is built by editors from a comparison of witnesses and printed with an *apparatus* reporting the evidence. The *Byzantine text*, also called the *Majority Text*, is the form found in most surviving manuscripts, most of them copied in the Greek-speaking East between the ninth and sixteenth centuries. It agrees with the Textus Receptus (TR) in most places and differs in some, and those differences matter later.

## A Text Copied by Hand

The books of the New Testament were written in Greek in the second half of the first century and copied by hand for the next fourteen hundred years. The originals were lost early. Every surviving witness is a copy of a copy, and no two handwritten copies of a book as long as Matthew are identical.

The earliest copies were made on *papyrus*, and Christians early preferred the *codex*, a book of folded leaves, to the scroll. From the fourth century the finest copies were made on parchment. Until the ninth century scribes wrote in *majuscule* letters, capitals set down without spaces between words. Then Greek scribes adopted a smaller joined script called *minuscule*, and most older books were recopied in it.

The Institute for New Testament Textual Research at Münster keeps the official register of Greek New Testament manuscripts. It lists more than five thousand: about 140 papyri, mostly fragments, the oldest from the second and third centuries; more than 300 majuscules, mostly from the fourth through the tenth centuries; nearly 3,000 minuscules, from the ninth century on; and some 2,500 lectionaries, books of readings for the church year. Only a few dozen contain the whole New Testament. Most were copied after the year 900.

A handful of majuscule books recur in what follows. Codex Vaticanus (B), of the fourth century and in the Vatican Library since at least the 1470s, contains most of the Greek Bible. Codex Sinaiticus, of the same century and designated by the Hebrew letter aleph (א), is the oldest complete copy of the New Testament. Codex Alexandrinus (A), of the fifth century, came to England in 1627. Codex Ephraemi (C), also of the fifth century, is a palimpsest: in the Middle Ages its Bible text was scraped off and the parchment reused for other writings, and scholars later recovered the underlying text. Codex Bezae (D), copied around 400 with Greek and Latin on facing pages, contains the Gospels and Acts with many readings found in few other Greek manuscripts.

The New Testament was also translated early into Latin, Syriac, and Coptic, and those versions preserve the Greek text their translators used. The Old Latin translations go back to the second century. In 382 Pope Damasus asked Jerome to revise the Latin Gospels, and Jerome's work became the core of the Vulgate, the Bible of the Western church for a thousand years. The Fathers, Christian writers of the early centuries, quoted the New Testament constantly, showing what text was read in a given place and time, though they often quoted loosely and later copyists sometimes conformed their quotations to the text of the copyists' own day.

Most variants are accidents. A scribe's eye could skip from one word to the same word farther down and leave out everything between. Similar letters were confused, words written twice, vowels that sounded alike exchanged. Slips of this kind are usually easy to recognize and rarely affect translation.

Other changes were deliberate, made by scribes who believed they were correcting or clarifying. The commonest is harmonization: a scribe who knew a saying in one Gospel filled out the parallel in another to match. Scribes also smoothed grammar, supplied a subject ("Jesus said" for "he said"), expanded titles, and added the forms in which a passage was used in worship. A note in a margin could be taken by the next copyist as words accidentally left out.

Several of the ESV's vacant verses fit this pattern on their face. Mark 9:44 and 9:46 repeat word for word the saying of Mark 9:48, "where their worm dieth not, and the fire is not quenched." Matthew 18:11, "For the Son of man is come to save that which was lost," closely matches Luke 19:10. Luke 17:36, "Two men shall be in the field; the one shall be taken, and the other left," matches Matthew 24:40. An editor who regards these as additions sees the source of each in its parallel. An editor who regards them as original sees what one expects of Gospels that report the same teaching more than once. The evidence is the same; its weight is assessed differently.

Copies made in one region tend to share readings, and scholars group manuscripts into families they call *text-types*. The names are modern labels with debated boundaries. The Alexandrian text, associated with Egypt, is found in Vaticanus, Sinaiticus, and many papyri, and tends to be shorter and less polished. The Western text, in Codex Bezae, the Old Latin, and some early Fathers, is marked by paraphrase and additions, especially in Acts. The Byzantine text, found in the great majority of later Greek manuscripts, tends to be fuller and smoother, with parallels brought into agreement and difficulties eased.

The Byzantine text came to dominate for reasons of history. After the fourth century the Latin West read the Vulgate, and in the seventh century Egypt and Syria passed under Arab rule. Greek copying continued chiefly in the Byzantine Empire, and the manuscripts made there from the ninth century on share a broadly uniform text. Egypt's dry climate preserved papyri that perished elsewhere, so many of the oldest witnesses come from Egypt.

What these facts mean is the central question of New Testament textual criticism. One school holds that the older Egyptian manuscripts preserve the earlier text and that the Byzantine text grew by accumulation and smoothing. Another holds that the Byzantine majority reflects the text the Greek-speaking church received and kept, and that the Egyptian manuscripts survived by accident of climate and represent one region's text. The rest of this book follows the history of that disagreement.

## The Greek New Testament Comes to Print

Johannes Gutenberg printed the Latin Bible at Mainz around 1455. The Greek New Testament did not reach print for another sixty years: the Latin Vulgate was the Bible of the Western church, few scholars read Greek, and Greek type was scarce. The first printed Greek New Testament belonged to a multilingual Bible prepared at Alcalá in Spain under Cardinal Francisco Jiménez de Cisneros, called the Complutensian Polyglot from the town's Latin name. Its New Testament volume was printed in 1514, but the set was not approved for sale until 1520 and circulated from about 1522.

Desiderius Erasmus of Rotterdam, the most celebrated scholar of northern Europe, published the first Greek New Testament actually put on sale. The Basel printer Johann Froben issued it in March 1516, setting the Greek beside Erasmus's own new Latin translation and annotations. The edition was prepared quickly from about half a dozen manuscripts available in Basel, most of them Byzantine minuscules of the twelfth century or later. One, known today as minuscule 1, carries an older type of text, but Erasmus made little use of it, suspecting it had been altered to agree with the Latin.

For Revelation he had a single manuscript, borrowed from the humanist Johann Reuchlin. Its last leaf was missing, so that it broke off before Revelation 22:16. Erasmus supplied the final six verses by translating the Latin Vulgate into Greek, producing readings found in no Greek manuscript. One stands in the KJV at Revelation 22:19: "God shall take away his part out of the book of life." Nearly all Greek manuscripts of the verse read "tree of life," and the Byzantine and critical texts agree. The reading "book" is usually traced to Latin copies in which *ligno*, "tree," had become *libro*, "book."

Erasmus also carried Latin wording into Acts. At Acts 9:5–6 the KJV reads, "it is hard for thee to kick against the pricks. And he trembling and astonished said, Lord, what wilt thou have me to do?" These words stand in the Vulgate and the Textus Receptus and are absent from nearly all Greek manuscripts at this point; the saying about the pricks stands in every text at Acts 26:14, in Paul's own retelling. At Acts 8:37, the Ethiopian's confession, "I believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God," Erasmus printed a verse he had found written in the margin of one Greek manuscript and knew from the Latin tradition, judging that careless scribes had dropped it. It is absent from the Byzantine majority, though Irenaeus, writing around 180, cites the confession in words that presuppose it.

Erasmus revised his text in 1519, 1522, 1527, and 1535. Martin Luther translated his German New Testament of 1522 from the 1519 edition, and William Tyndale used the 1522 and later editions for his English New Testament.

### The three witnesses

Erasmus's editions of 1516 and 1519 lacked the words about three witnesses in heaven in 1 John 5:7–8, because no Greek manuscript he had seen contained them. The words were familiar from late medieval Latin Bibles, and critics faulted him for the omission. A Greek manuscript containing them then came to light in England, a copy made in the early sixteenth century and now in Trinity College, Dublin (minuscule 61). Erasmus printed the passage in his third edition of 1522. In his annotations he said he did so to leave no occasion for slander, and he voiced the suspicion that the manuscript had been made to agree with the Latin.

A familiar story holds that Erasmus had promised to include the words if anyone showed him a single Greek manuscript containing them. The Dutch scholar Henk Jan de Jonge showed in 1980 that no such promise appears in Erasmus's writings. The facts stand without the story: the words entered the printed Greek New Testament in 1522 from a manuscript of that century, and every later edition of the Textus Receptus kept them.

### The received text

Robert Estienne, royal printer at Paris, issued Greek New Testaments in 1546, 1549, and 1550. The 1550 folio followed Erasmus's later editions closely and was the first to print variant readings in its margins, drawn from about fifteen manuscripts, among them Codex Bezae. Estienne then moved to Geneva, where in 1551 he published the edition with verse divisions. In England his 1550 text became the standard Greek New Testament for three centuries.

Theodore Beza, Calvin's successor at Geneva, published editions of the Greek New Testament between 1565 and 1604 with a Latin translation and annotations. He owned two ancient manuscripts, Codex Bezae and Codex Claromontanus, and in 1581 gave the first to the University of Cambridge, but he used them sparingly. His text was Estienne's, altered in a number of places. His editions of 1588–89 and 1598 were the Greek texts the King James translators used most.

In 1633 the Elzevir printing house at Leiden issued a Greek New Testament whose preface announced, "You have therefore the text now received by all, in which we give nothing altered or corrupt." From that sentence the family of editions from Erasmus to the Elzevirs took its name, *textus receptus*.

The Textus Receptus is a group of closely related printed editions that differ among themselves in a few hundred places. Its text is largely Byzantine, because Erasmus's manuscripts were, but in some places it stands alone against both the Byzantine majority and the critical text. Several of these have already appeared: Acts 8:37, which Erasmus printed from one Greek margin and the Latin tradition; the words at Acts 9:5–6 he took from the Latin; the three witnesses of 1 John 5:7–8, printed in Greek in the Complutensian and by Erasmus in 1522; and "the book of life" at Revelation 22:19, from his translation of the Latin. With them belong Acts 15:34, "Notwithstanding it pleased Silas to abide there still," found in a minority of Greek and Latin witnesses; Acts 24:6b–8a, "and would have judged according to our law," found in a minority of Greek witnesses; and "through his blood" at Colossians 1:14, which matches Ephesians 1:7 and appears in a few late copies. At Luke 17:36 the printed editions disagree among themselves. Erasmus and Estienne's 1550 text lack the verse, while Estienne's 1551 edition and Beza's include it. The point returns in Chapter 14, because the two traditional positions on the text answer differently in these places.

## The Making of the King James Version

The King James Version was a revision. The first complete English Bibles, made in the circle of John Wycliffe in the 1380s, were translated from the Latin. William Tyndale made the first English New Testament from the Greek, printed at Worms in 1526, and translated much of the Old Testament from the Hebrew before his execution in 1536. Coverdale's Bible (1535), Matthew's Bible (1537), and the Great Bible (1539), placed in English churches by order of Henry VIII, built on Tyndale. Protestant exiles produced the Geneva Bible (1560), with verse numbers and extensive marginal notes, and it became the Bible of English homes and of the Puritans. The bishops issued their own revision, the Bishops' Bible (1568), which was read in churches but never displaced the Geneva at home.

James VI of Scotland became James I of England in 1603. In January 1604 he met bishops and Puritan representatives at Hampton Court Palace to hear the Puritans' requests for reform. Most were refused. John Rainolds, President of Corpus Christi College, Oxford, proposed a new translation, and the king agreed. According to the official account, James judged the Geneva the worst of the English translations and objected to its notes as partial and seditious. A Bible without partisan notes, read in every church, suited him.

Richard Bancroft, Bishop of London and from late 1604 Archbishop of Canterbury, organized the work and issued fifteen rules. (Quotations from this period modernize the spelling.) The first set the base: "The ordinary Bible read in the Church, commonly called the Bishops' Bible, to be followed, and as little altered as the truth of the original will permit." Another named earlier translations, Tyndale's and the Geneva among them, to be used "when they agree better with the text." A third forbade marginal notes "but only for the explanation of the Hebrew or Greek words."

About fifty scholars served in six companies, two each at Westminster, Cambridge, and Oxford. At Westminster, Lancelot Andrewes directed the company that took Genesis through 2 Kings, and William Barlow the one that took the letters from Romans to Jude. At Cambridge, Edward Lively's company took 1 Chronicles through the Song of Solomon, and John Duport's the Apocrypha. At Oxford, John Harding's company took the prophets from Isaiah to Malachi, and Thomas Ravis's the Gospels, Acts, and Revelation. Each finished book went to the other companies for review. In 1610 a committee of representatives reviewed the whole at Stationers' Hall in London; notes taken there by John Bois survive and show the reviewers debating single words of the Greek. Miles Smith and Thomas Bilson saw the work through the press, and Smith wrote the preface, "The Translators to the Reader." Robert Barker, the King's Printer, issued it in 1611 as a large folio "Appointed to be read in Churches." No record of a formal act of authorization survives, but by common usage it became the Authorized Version.

### What the translators translated

For the Old Testament the translators used printed Hebrew Bibles descended from the Rabbinic Bible printed by Daniel Bomberg at Venice in 1524–25. For the New Testament they used the printed Greek editions, chiefly Beza's of 1588–89 and 1598 and Estienne's of 1550, choosing between them where they disagreed, most often with Beza. No printed Greek text matched all their choices until F. H. A. Scrivener reconstructed one in 1881, revised in 1894. Scrivener's text is the Textus Receptus printed today by the Trinitarian Bible Society.

The translators knew that manuscripts differed, and the 1611 edition records some of what they knew. At Luke 17:36 the margin reads, "This 36. verse is wanting in most of the Greek copies." At Matthew 26:26, where the text says Jesus "blessed" the bread, the margin reads, "Many Greek copies have, gave thanks." The printed editions disagreed over Luke 17:36, and the translators followed Beza in keeping it. They kept the reading in Matthew too, and told the reader what they knew.

They could not know what later centuries found. Codex Alexandrinus reached England sixteen years after 1611. Codex Vaticanus was in Rome and closed to full study. Codex Sinaiticus lay unrecognized at Mount Sinai until 1844. None of the New Testament papyri had been found.

The preface states their aim: "we never thought from the beginning that we should need to make a new translation, nor yet to make of a bad one a good one, ... but to make a good one better, or out of many good ones, one principal good one." It defends the marginal notes: "variety of translations is profitable for the finding out of the sense of the Scriptures: so diversity of signification and sense in the margin, where the text is not so clear, must needs do good, yea, is necessary, as we are persuaded." And it defends earlier English Bibles: "the very meanest translation of the Bible in English, set forth by men of our profession, ... containeth the word of God, nay, is the word of God."

The 1611 Bible printed the Apocrypha, books found in the Greek and Latin Old Testament but absent from the Hebrew, as a separate section between the Testaments, which the Church of England read "for example of life and instruction of manners" without using them "to establish any doctrine." In 1826 the British and Foreign Bible Society resolved not to fund Bibles that contained them, and most KJV printings since have left them out, as do the standard editions of the ESV. Which books belong in the Bible is a question of canon; this book concerns the text of the books.

The KJV most people read today is Benjamin Blayney's Oxford edition of 1769, the last of several revisions (1629, 1638, 1762) that corrected printers' errors, modernized spelling, and regularized the italics marking English words supplied for sense. These revisions changed the English in hundreds of small ways and left the Greek and Hebrew basis unchanged. By the end of the seventeenth century the KJV had displaced the Geneva Bible, and it remained the English Bible for three hundred years.
