# Part Four: Reading the Same Evidence

## Four Positions

The facts set out in the preceding chapters are not seriously disputed. Their meaning is. Christians who regard the Bible as the word of God read them in more than one way today, and each reading gives its own account of why the ESV and the KJV differ.

*Reasoned eclecticism* is the position of the editors of the Nestle-Aland, UBS, SBL, and Tyndale House editions and of the translators of most modern English Bibles, including the ESV, NIV, NASB, CSB, and NRSV. It holds that no manuscript or family is free of error, that readings must be weighed one at a time by the criteria described in Chapter 7, and that the oldest witnesses usually, though not always, preserve the earlier wording. On this view the ESV omits or brackets the passages discussed here because the evidence indicates they were added in copying. Its holders regard the text as established with high confidence in the great majority of places and uncertain in a small number. Many evangelicals who hold it also affirm the providential preservation of Scripture and locate that preservation in the whole body of surviving witnesses. B. B. Warfield of Princeton, a defender of biblical inerrancy who followed Westcott and Hort's method and wrote *An Introduction to the Textual Criticism of the New Testament* (1886), held an early form of this view.

The position called *Byzantine priority* holds that the original text is best represented by the form found in the majority of Greek manuscripts. Its holders argue that the text's transmission in the Greek-speaking church across many centuries and regions is better evidence than a few early copies preserved by Egypt's climate, and that Hort's arguments against the Byzantine text fail. Zane Hodges and Arthur Farstad published *The Greek New Testament According to the Majority Text* (1982); Maurice Robinson and William Pierpont published *The New Testament in the Original Greek: Byzantine Textform* (1991; revised 2005). On this view Mark 16:9–20, John 7:53–8:11, the doxology of Matthew 6:13, and twelve of the sixteen vacant verses belong in the text, while Acts 8:37, Luke 17:36, "through his blood" at Colossians 1:14, and the heavenly witnesses of 1 John 5:7–8 do not. The resulting text differs from both the ESV's and the KJV's.

Defenders of the *confessional text* hold that the Textus Receptus, the Greek text received and used by the Protestant churches of the Reformation, is the providentially preserved text of the New Testament, as the Masoretic text is of the Old. They begin from doctrine. The Westminster Confession (1646) and the Second London Baptist Confession (1689) state that the Old Testament in Hebrew and the New Testament in Greek, "being immediately inspired by God, and, by his singular care and providence, kept pure in all ages, are therefore authentical; so as, in all controversies of religion, the Church is finally to appeal unto them" (1.8). Defenders of the confessional text argue that the text so described is the one the framers of those confessions possessed and used, and that a method which treats Scripture like any other ancient book, revising its text as manuscripts are found, conflicts with the doctrine of preservation. Edward F. Hills set out the position in *The King James Version Defended* (1956), and Theodore Letis and others have followed him. The Trinitarian Bible Society, founded in 1831, publishes Scrivener's text and promotes the KJV. On this view every passage the KJV prints and the ESV brackets or omits belongs in the text, and the ESV omits them because it translates a text built by a mistaken method.

Holders of the critical text, Warfield among them, read the same section of the Confession as affirming that God has preserved his word in the copies taken as a whole, and that the church's final appeal to the Hebrew and Greek requires the church to study them. Both positions appeal to the same confession.

The *King James Only* position holds that the King James Version itself, as an English text, is the preserved word of God for English-speaking people, and that modern versions are corruptions. Benjamin G. Wilkinson, a Seventh-day Adventist, argued in *Our Authorized Bible Vindicated* (1930) that the modern text descends from corrupt sources, and David Otis Fuller's anthology *Which Bible?* (1970) carried his arguments to a wide fundamentalist audience. Peter Ruckman taught that the KJV corrects the Greek and Hebrew where they differ from it, and Gail Riplinger's *New Age Bible Versions* (1993) charged the modern versions with deliberate doctrinal corruption. Many defenders of the confessional text reject this position, because it places an English translation above the Hebrew and Greek that the Westminster Confession calls authentical.

Individual scholars cross these lines. Some eclectic critics defend the longer ending of Mark, and some defenders of the Byzantine text hold particular minority readings to be original.

## What Each Bible Tells Its Reader

The 1611 translators told their readers that the margin would show where the text "is not so clear," and their margins at Luke 17:36 and Matthew 26:26 reported what they knew of the Greek copies. Most KJV printings today leave out both the preface and most of the margin. The ESV tells its readers through brackets and footnotes, which show where the translators chose; the reasons for the critical text's choices are set out in the Nestle-Aland and UBS apparatus and, in plain prose, in Metzger's *Textual Commentary*.

A reader who wants to see the choice in a given verse can open three books. Scrivener's edition prints the Greek the KJV translated. The Nestle-Aland or UBS edition prints the Greek the ESV translated, with the evidence beneath it. The New King James Version prints the received text in English with footnotes giving the critical and majority readings, so the three texts discussed in this book can be compared on one page.
