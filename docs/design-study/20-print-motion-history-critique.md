## Print production
- Document setup:
  - trim size
  - bleed (usually 0.125 inch or 3 mm) for anything that runs off the edge
  - a safety margin inside the trim for live content
  - a slug for notes
- Colour:
  - CMYK for process work, spot inks for exact colours
  - convert with the printer's profile
  - rich black for large solid areas (for example 60C 40M 40Y 100K)
  - plain 100K for small text, to avoid registration fringes
  - respect total ink limits (around 300 percent on coated stock, lower on uncoated)
  - black text set to overprint
- Images: about 300 pixels per inch at final size, higher for line art. Check that colour images are converted properly.
- Fonts: embed them or have them licensed for output. Export to PDF/X (X-1a or X-4) and preflight.
- Imposition and signatures:
  - books are printed on large sheets folded into signatures of 8, 16 or 32 pages, so page counts work in multiples
  - saddle-stitched booklets need page counts in multiples of 4 and allowance for creep
- Proofs: soft proofs on screen, hard and contract proofs, press checks.
- Paper:
  - weight (text against cover)
  - coated (gloss, silk, matte) against uncoated
  - shade (warm or cool white), opacity, bulk
  - grain direction, which should run parallel to the spine
- Binding:
  - saddle stitch (thin booklets)
  - perfect binding (glued, calculate spine width from page count and paper bulk)
  - sewn and case-bound hardcovers (Smyth sewing)
  - wire-o and spiral (lies flat)
  - hand bindings (see [15](15-grids-layout-editorial.md))
- Finishing: foil, embossing and debossing, die cutting, spot varnish, lamination.
- Process: offset lithography for runs and colour accuracy, digital printing for short runs and variable data.
- Halftones: about 150 lines per inch on coated stock, coarser on uncoated and newsprint. Dot gain darkens midtones. Stochastic screens avoid moiré.
- Riso and screenprinting: see [16](16-color.md). The Riso workshop's lesson, in its own terms: well-planned files produce high-quality, reproducible outputs.

## Motion graphics and sound (Motion, Sound and Vision, Music Video)
- Disney's twelve principles adapted to graphic motion:
  - squash and stretch
  - anticipation
  - staging
  - straight-ahead and pose-to-pose
  - follow-through and overlapping action
  - slow in and slow out (easing)
  - arcs
  - secondary action
  - timing
  - exaggeration
  - solid drawing
  - appeal
- Easing:
  - things entering decelerate (ease-out)
  - things leaving accelerate (ease-in)
  - linear motion looks mechanical
- In interfaces, durations of about 150 to 400 ms are typical. Small changes are faster, large travel slower. Motion should explain spatial relationships and state changes.
- Kinetic typography: Saul Bass's title sequences (Vertigo, Psycho, North by Northwest), Kyle Cooper's Se7en. Type as performer.
- Rhythm and sound:
  - cut and animate on the beat
  - sound design in layers (dialogue, music, effects, ambience)
  - sync
  - loudness normalization for delivery (streaming platforms target around minus 14 LUFS)
- Workflow:
  - storyboard, then animatic, then production
  - After Effects: keyframes, the graph editor for easing, precompositions, masks and track mattes, expressions
  - frame rates (24, 30, 60)
  - aspect ratios for each platform
- Music video: video design and sound design build a visual narrative, drawing on two-dimensional and three-dimensional design, lighting and sound.

## Photography for designers (Photo/Graphic workshop)
- Exposure triangle:
  - aperture sets depth of field
  - shutter speed sets motion blur or freeze
  - ISO sets sensitivity and noise
- Light:
  - hard or soft (the size of the source relative to the subject)
  - three-point lighting (key, fill, back)
  - colour temperature (tungsten about 3200 K, daylight about 5600 K), white balance
- Composition: thirds, leading lines, framing, negative space reserved for type.
- Shooting for layout: cover both orientations, leave room for headlines, keep consistent light across a series.
- Shoot RAW. Retouch honestly, and know where manipulation becomes deception.

## Digital 3D (workshop)
- Modelling: polygons, subdivision surfaces, sculpting.
- Materials: physically based shaders (base colour, roughness, metallic).
- Lighting: area lights, HDRI environments.
- The virtual camera: focal length, depth of field.
- Rendering, then compositing renders with 2D graphic work (render passes, alpha). Animation for motion graphics.

## History of graphic design (GRAPH 3225)
The course studies design since 1450 as both shaped by and shaping social, political and technological change, with emphasis on printed work from 1880 to 1970. Timeline from Meggs' History of Graphic Design:
- Before 1500:
  - Gutenberg's movable type in Mainz in the 1450s (the 42-line Bible, about 1455)
  - Jenson's roman (Venice, 1470)
  - Aldus Manutius and Griffo (Hypnerotomachia Poliphili 1499, the first italic 1501)
- 1500s to 1700s:
  - Garamond (1530s to 1540s)
  - Caslon (1720s)
  - Baskerville's transitional type and smooth wove paper (1757)
  - Didot and Bodoni's moderns (late 1700s)
- The 1800s:
  - the Industrial Revolution brought fat faces, slab serifs, wood type and posters
  - lithography (Senefelder 1796) and then chromolithography
- Reform and Art Nouveau:
  - the Arts and Crafts movement (William Morris's Kelmscott Press, 1891)
  - Art Nouveau (Chéret, Mucha, Beardsley)
  - Plakatstil (Bernhard, 1906)
- The avant-gardes:
  - Futurism (1909)
  - Dada
  - De Stijl (1917)
  - Russian Constructivism (Rodchenko, Lissitzky, Stepanova)
- The Bauhaus (1919 to 1933): Moholy-Nagy's "typophoto", Bayer's universal alphabet.
- Tschichold's Die neue Typographie (1928): asymmetry, sans serif, functional order.
- Isotype, Art Deco (Cassandre).
- American modernism: Brodovitch at Harper's Bazaar, Lustig, Bass.
- Swiss or International Typographic Style (1950s):
  - Basel (Ruder, Hofmann) and Zurich (Müller-Brockmann)
  - grids, objectivity, sans serif
  - Helvetica and Univers (1957)
- Corporate identity: Rand's IBM (1956), Chermayeff and Geismar, Unimark, Vignelli.
- Push Pin Studios (Glaser, Chwast), psychedelic posters, Pop.
- Postmodern and new wave:
  - Weingart in Basel
  - April Greiman
  - Cranbrook under Katherine McCoy
  - Emigre (Licko and VanderLans, 1984)
  - Carson's Ray Gun
- The desktop revolution: the Macintosh (1984), PostScript, desktop publishing. Then the web and screens.
- Wider histories: Meggs is criticized as Eurocentric and male-centred. Newer work broadens it: Eskilson's Graphic Design: A New History, The Black Experience in Design (2022), decolonizing design.

## Critique and studio culture
- RISD courses expect students to talk about their own and their peers' work in supportive, respectful ways, often in pin-up crits, desk crits and reviews with visiting critics.
- Protocols: Lerman's Critical Response Process, Connor and Irizarry's objective-based critique, Feldman's description-analysis-interpretation-judgment sequence. See [04](04-design-process-prototyping-service.md).
- Describe before judging. Tie each comment to the work's intent. Separate process from outcome. Ask questions first.

## Professional and independent practice
- The BFA prepares students for professional practice, independent studio practice or entrepreneurship. That means presentation, communication and collaboration, and a portfolio ready for the field.
- Portfolio case studies: the problem, the student's role, process (with the dead ends), the outcome and its effect.
- The MFA adds teaching and mentoring, curating, original research conveyed through writing, publishing and exhibiting, and form as proof of concept.
- The BFA degree project is self-defined, drawing on the student's strengths and interests.
- The MFA thesis is described as equal parts exploration, explanation, provocation and contribution. Visiting critics review it, and it's shown publicly.

## Reflexive, responsible and sustainable practice (BFA aim)
- Sustainability:
  - paper sourcing (FSC certification, recycled content)
  - inks and coatings
  - print quantities and waste
  - digital carbon (page weight, media, hosting)
  - lifecycle thinking (McDonough and Braungart, Cradle to Cradle)
- Responsibility: the First Things First manifesto (Garland 1964, renewed 2000) asked designers to put their skills toward worthwhile ends over consumer persuasion.
- RISD's history course has students analyse and critique visual communication from historical, social and intersectional points of view: whom a design serves, whom it leaves out, what power it encodes.
