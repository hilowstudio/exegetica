# 18. Information design, mapping, exhibition and wayfinding

Serves RISD courses:
- Mapping Information (GRAPH 3282)
- Exhibit Design: Story, Space, Identity (3273)

It also covers the BFA's "information design" focus in the junior year. Shares ground with CMU data visualization ([12](12-data-visualization.md)).

## What the courses cover
- Mapping Information:
  - organizing and analysing data, and the concepts and methods of visualizing it
  - maps, graphs, charts and diagrams
  - working issues: mapping, hierarchy, location, time, comparison, motion, format, and symbolic visual language
  The course calls visualizing information one of the oldest forms of graphic design.
- Exhibit Design:
  - stories and information in a designed environment
  - concept, space, time, movement, form, colour, typography and objects working together
  - spatial storytelling and an identity system

## Organizing information
- Wurman's LATCH (Information Anxiety, 1989). Information can be organized only five ways:
  - Location
  - Alphabet
  - Time
  - Category
  - Hierarchy (magnitude, continuum)
  Each answers a different question, so pick by what readers will ask.
- Hierarchy and grouping come first. Visual form follows the structure.

## Tufte on information design (Envisioning Information, 1990)
- Escaping flatland: put several variables on a flat surface.
- Micro and macro readings: the same display rewards the overview and close study.
- Layering and separation:
  - quiet the secondary layers (grids, borders) with lighter value and subtle colour, so the data layer stands forward
  - Tufte's "1 + 1 = 3" effect: two strong lines create a third, unwanted element, the active space between them
- Small multiples.
- Colour:
  - after Imhof, use pure, bright colours only for small areas against muted backgrounds
  - nature's muted palette for large areas
- Narratives of space and time: Minard's map of Napoleon's Russian campaign (1869) shows army size, two-dimensional location, direction, temperature and dates on one sheet.

## Isotype (Otto Neurath, Marie Neurath and Gerd Arntz, Vienna 1920s to 1940s)
- Quantities shown by counting repeated pictograms, more icons for more, never bigger icons.
- Pictograms reduced to essentials and drawn consistently.
- Colour by category.
- The "transformer" turns data and expert knowledge into a picture a lay audience can read.

## Diagrams
- Types:
  - flowcharts and process diagrams
  - trees and hierarchies
  - networks
  - Venn and Euler diagrams
  - matrices
  - timelines
  - cycles
  - Sankey flows
  - concept maps (Novak)
- Dubberly Design Office's "models" practice: a diagram of a system makes it discussable and improvable.
- Rules:
  - one visual grammar (consistent shapes, line meanings, arrow types)
  - label the relationships, not only the nodes
  - minimize crossings
  - use reading direction for sequence

## Maps
- Projection: Mercator preserves angles and inflates area near the poles. Use equal-area projections for thematic maps of quantities.
- Generalization: select, simplify, smooth, exaggerate, displace, for the map's scale and purpose.
- Figure and ground (land and water), a clear visual hierarchy, and label placement rules (Imhof) for type that reads and identifies without clutter.
- Thematic forms:
  - choropleth (normalized rates only)
  - proportional symbols
  - dot density
  - isolines and heat maps
  - flow maps
  - cartograms
- Diagrammatic and topological maps:
  - Harry Beck's London Underground diagram (1933) dropped geographic accuracy for clarity: lines at 45 and 90 degrees, evenly spaced stations.
  - Vignelli's 1972 New York subway diagram was admired by designers and confused riders who expected geography. It was replaced in 1979 and later revived as the 2008 "Weekender" digital map.
  - The lesson: fit the abstraction to the reader's task.
- Maps are arguments. Monmonier (How to Lie with Maps) shows how choices of class breaks, projection and colour change the story. Critical cartography asks whose view a map encodes.

## Time
- Timelines: linear, logarithmic, or spiral for cyclical patterns.
- Joseph Priestley's Chart of Biography (1765) and New Chart of History (1769) set the template for showing lifespans and eras on a measured axis (Rosenberg and Grafton, Cartographies of Time).
- Gantt charts.
- Comparison across time needs the same scale and aligned baselines.

## Comparison and motion
- Comparisons need shared scales, aligned baselines, direct labels and small multiples.
- Motion: staged animated transitions help viewers follow change. Uncontrolled motion distracts. Motion should encode change, not decorate.

## Symbolic visual language
- Pictogram systems:
  - Aicher's Munich 1972 pictograms on a strict grid
  - the AIGA and US Department of Transportation symbol signs (Cook and Shanosky, 1974), 50 public-domain symbols
  - ISO 7001 public information symbols
- Icon design:
  - consistent grid, stroke and corner treatment
  - clear metaphors
  - test recognition
- Icons alone are ambiguous except for a few well-learned ones (home, search, print, close). Pair them with labels in interfaces.

## Exhibition design
- Interpretive planning:
  - Serrell (Exhibit Labels: An Interpretive Approach) argues each exhibition should have one "big idea", a single sentence that every element serves
  - organize content by it
  - write a label hierarchy: title, introductory panel, section or group labels, object labels and captions
- Label writing:
  - short (Serrell's guidance caps many labels at about 50 words)
  - concrete, written to what visitors can see
  - a layered depth of information
- Visitor behaviour (Serrell, Paying Attention, 1998):
  - visitors typically spend under 20 minutes in an exhibition
  - most stop at fewer than half the elements
  - her "sweep rate index" and "percentage of diligent visitors" measure this
  Design for browsing: make each stop meaningful on its own.
- Circulation: forced, suggested or open paths. Early studies found visitors tend to turn right on entering (Melton). Use pacing (dense rooms against breathing rooms), sight lines that pull visitors forward, rest points, and landmarks.
- Ergonomics and access:
  - centre key text and objects near standing eye level, about 54 to 60 inches
  - stay inside seated sight lines and reach ranges for wheelchair users
  - large type and strong contrast, since conservation lighting is often dim (around 50 lux for light-sensitive works)
  - multisensory and tactile options
  - captions and audio description for media
- Integration: the course's list of concept, space, time, movement, form, colour, typography and objects means graphic design, architecture and story are designed together. The identity system has to work on walls, labels, graphics, merchandise and screens.
- Evaluation: front-end, formative (mock-ups with visitors), remedial, summative (Screven and others).

## Wayfinding and signage
- Lynch, The Image of the City (1960): people form mental maps from paths, edges, districts, nodes and landmarks. "Imageability" makes places navigable.
- Arthur and Passini, Wayfinding (1992): wayfinding is decision-making, carrying out decisions, and processing information. Put information at decision points.
- Sign types:
  - identification
  - directional
  - orientation (maps)
  - regulatory
  - interpretive
- "You are here" maps must be aligned with the viewer's forward direction (forward-up), or people make errors (Levine 1982).
- Typefaces for signs need large x-heights, open apertures and distinct letters:
  - Transport (Kinneir and Calvert, UK roads)
  - Frutiger (made for Charles de Gaulle Airport)
  - Clearview (US highways: its federal approval was withdrawn in 2016 and restored in 2018)
- Digital equivalents: breadcrumbs ("you are here"), consistent navigation, landmarks such as stable headers, and search as the last resort.
