## Purpose and stance
- Two traditions run through RISD's sequence. The course describes typography as both communication and a medium for experimental expression.
  - The service tradition: typography serves the text and the reader. Beatrice Warde's "crystal goblet" (a 1930 talk) argued type should be a clear vessel for content. Bringhurst holds that typography exists to honour content.
  - The expressive tradition: type as image and voice. Futurist "words in freedom", Dada, Constructivism, concrete poetry, Wolfgang Weingart's new wave, Cranbrook, Emigre, David Carson.
- The sequence, as RISD describes it:
  - Typography I zooms in: how and why letters are formed, their meaning and voice. It then zooms out, setting type from letter to word to paragraph to page.
  - Typography II covers systems across scales and narrative structures, and extended reading.
  - Typography III carries meaning and voice across contemporary display platforms.
- RISD's own line: "Typesetting is the score for the reading experience."

## Anatomy and measurement
- Vertical metrics: baseline, x-height, cap height, ascender, descender, overshoot.
- Parts of letters:
  - stem, bowl, counter (open or closed), aperture
  - arm, leg, spine, shoulder, bar
  - terminal, ear, link and loop (the double-storey g), spur
  - serif (bracketed, unbracketed, slab, hairline)
- Stress or axis: the angle of thick and thin. It is diagonal in humanist letters, vertical in rational ones.
- Contrast: the difference between thick and thin strokes.
- Units:
  - the point (1/72 inch in digital practice) and the pica (12 points)
  - the em (equal to the type size) and the en (half an em)
  - fonts are drawn on a units-per-em grid (often 1000 or 2048)
- Type size is the height of the em body, not of any letter. Two fonts at the same size can look very different because their x-heights differ. Compare by x-height.

## Classification
- Vox-ATypI (1954, adopted 1962, withdrawn by ATypI in 2021 but still a useful map):
  - humanist (Venetian)
  - garalde (old style)
  - réale (transitional)
  - didone (modern)
  - mécane (slab)
  - lineale (sans serif: grotesque, neo-grotesque, geometric, humanist)
  - incise (glyphic)
  - script, manual (graphic), blackletter
  - non-Latin scripts sit outside it
- History you can read in the forms:
  - The axis rotates from diagonal (pen-made, humanist) to vertical (rational, Baskerville then Bodoni).
  - Contrast rises toward Didone.
  - Apertures open in humanist designs and close in neo-grotesques.
  - Serifs move from bracketed to hairline to slab.
- Use classification to understand rhythm and lineage, and to pair faces. Pairs work through contrast in structure with harmony in proportion (similar x-heights and widths), or by using a superfamily drawn to work together.

## Legibility and readability
- Legibility: how easily individual characters are recognized. It depends on x-height, aperture, distinct shapes (I, l, 1, rn against m), spacing, weight and contrast.
- Readability: how easily continuous text is read. It depends on size, measure, leading, spacing, contrast with the background and familiarity (Tracy's distinction).
- Research:
  - Readers recognize words through their letters, processed in parallel, not through word outlines (Larson 2004, Pelli and colleagues). The "bouma" shape theory is not supported.
  - Serif against sans serif shows no reliable readability difference for body text at adequate resolution and size. Familiarity, size and spacing matter more (Arditi and Cho 2005, Beier, Reading Letters 2012).

## Setting text
Butterick's key rules, verified this session, plus Bringhurst:
- Size: 10 to 12 points in print, about 15 to 25 pixels on the web.
- Line spacing (leading): 120 to 145 percent of the point size. Long lines need more, short lines and large display type less.
- Line length:
  - Butterick: 45 to 90 characters including spaces.
  - Bringhurst: 45 to 75 for single-column text, 66 as the ideal, 40 to 50 for multi-column work.
  - On the web, set the measure in ch or em units (about 60 to 75ch).
- Alignment:
  - flush left, ragged right is the safe default, especially in narrow columns
  - justified text always needs hyphenation (Butterick) and good spacing settings to avoid rivers
  - shape the rag to avoid wedges and steps
  - use centred text sparingly
- Paragraphs:
  - a first-line indent of 1 to 4 times the point size, or 4 to 10 points of space between paragraphs, but not both (Butterick)
  - no indent on the first paragraph after a heading (Bringhurst)
- Widows, orphans and runts: avoid single short lines stranded at the top or bottom of a column, and single-word last lines.
- Spacing:
  - kerning always on
  - add 5 to 12 percent letterspacing to all caps and small caps
  - do not letterspace lowercase body text
  - tighten large display type slightly
  - one space between sentences
- Emphasis:
  - use bold or italic as little as possible, and never together
  - never underline except web links
  - all caps only for less than a line
  - use real small caps or none
- Correct characters:
  - curly quotes and apostrophes (apostrophes point down)
  - hyphen for compounds, en dash for ranges, em dash for breaks
  - a real ellipsis character
  - straight prime marks for feet and inches
  - a true multiplication sign and minus sign
  - proper trademark and copyright symbols
  - a non-breaking space after paragraph and section marks
- Figures:
  - oldstyle (text) figures in running text, lining figures with capitals and in tables
  - tabular (fixed-width) figures in columns, proportional figures in text
- Ligatures: standard ligatures (fi, fl) on in text. Discretionary ones sparingly.

## Hierarchy
- Tools: size, weight, case, style (italic), colour or value, position, indentation, and space above and below.
- Use as few variables as you need. Each level should differ from its neighbours by a clear step, usually changing one or two variables, not five.
- Space binds: put more space above a heading than below it, so it belongs to the text it introduces.
- Type scales:
  - Bringhurst's classic sequence: 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14, 16, 18, 21, 24, 36, 48, 60, 72.
  - Modular scales multiply by a ratio (1.2, 1.25, 1.333 the perfect fourth, 1.5, 1.618 the golden ratio). Musical intervals are the usual metaphor.
- Typographic colour: the overall grey and texture a block of text makes on the page. Even colour comes from consistent spacing, weight and leading. Squint to judge it. Typography I teaches students to work with typographic colour and texture in a fixed composition.

## Micro and macro typography
- Micro: letters, spacing, characters, figures, punctuation.
- Macro: layout, grids, hierarchy, sequence. RISD's Typographic Studies covers text at both levels.
- RISD's zoom path:
  1. letter
  2. word
  3. line and paragraph
  4. page
  5. system across formats (Typography II)
  6. platforms and changing states (Typography III)

## Systems across scales and formats (Typography II)
- The same content becomes a poster, a book and a phone screen. Each format changes viewing distance, reading mode (glance, scan, read) and how hierarchy has to work.
- Students design large-scale and small-scale work at the same time to learn the trade-offs.
- Readability as a condition the designer sets (RISD's framing): create order, express emotion, make meaning.
- Book conventions and their screen equivalents (Typography II binds a book and relates the codex to onscreen reading):
  - Front matter: half title, title page, copyright, dedication, contents, foreword, preface, introduction.
  - Back matter: appendices, notes, bibliography, index, colophon.
  - Page furniture: running heads, folios (page numbers), chapter openings, epigraphs, section breaks, drop caps.
  - Notes: footnotes, endnotes and sidenotes. On screen these become popovers, sidenotes in the margin (Tufte CSS) and linked endnotes.
  - Indexes and contents become search, a persistent table of contents, and anchors.
  - Pages and turns become scrolling, position indicators and reflowable or fixed layouts.

## Screen typography
- Rendering: hinting, antialiasing and pixel density. High-density screens have narrowed the gap with print.
- Units: rem for global scale, em for local, ch for measure. Fluid type uses clamp(min, preferred, max) so sizes scale with the viewport between limits.
- Font loading:
  - FOIT (invisible text) against FOUT (unstyled text, then a swap)
  - font-display: swap or optional
  - subsetting, WOFF2, preloading critical fonts
- OpenType features in CSS: kerning, ligatures, onum (oldstyle figures), tnum (tabular), smcp (small caps), case-sensitive forms, stylistic sets.
- Variable fonts (OpenType 1.8, 2016):
  - registered axes: weight (wght), width (wdth), optical size (opsz), italic (ital), slant (slnt)
  - custom axes, such as GRAD, adjust stroke weight without reflowing text, which is useful for dark mode
  - optical sizing adjusts contrast and spacing for small and large sizes automatically
- Hyphenation: hyphens: auto with the correct lang attribute. text-wrap: balance for headings, text-wrap: pretty for paragraphs (browser support varies).

## Expressive, interactive and computational typography
- Type + Code: variable fonts plus HTML, CSS and JavaScript make typography that responds to input, time and context. Students make fonts in Glyphs, web specimens and digital articles.
- Advanced Type Studio treats typography as four-dimensional: text designed to be interacted with and experienced over time and space, at human scale.
  - The tools themselves become design sites.
  - Participatory feedback loops are designed to reveal unexpected behaviour.
  - Outcomes include responsive tools, live visuals and installations.
- Kinetic typography: film titles (Saul Bass, Kyle Cooper), motion as meaning.
- Computational poetics treats language as both content and code. See [17](17-identity-branding-systems.md).
