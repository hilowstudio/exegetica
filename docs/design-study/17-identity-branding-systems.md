# 17. Identity, branding, systems and generative design

Serves RISD courses:
- Identity Design (GRAPH 3286)
- Experimental Branding (2323)
- Generative Design: Tool, System, Network (2310)
- Computational Poetics (2215)

It also covers the BFA outcome on applying visual systems across print, digital and 3D. Bridges to CMU design systems ([03](03-interaction-design-principles.md)).

## Terms
- Brand: what people think and feel about an organization, its reputation in their minds. Neumeier (The Brand Gap) puts it as a person's gut feeling about a product or organization.
- Identity: the designed system that expresses the brand. It includes marks, type, colour, imagery, voice, layout and behaviour.
- Kinds of mark:
  - wordmark (logotype)
  - lettermark (monogram)
  - pictorial symbol
  - abstract mark
  - emblem (name inside a shape)
  - combination mark

## The mark
- Paul Rand: a logo identifies, it does not explain. Meaning builds through use and association over time. His tests for a mark (Design, Form, and Chaos, 1993): distinctiveness, visibility, usability, memorability, universality, durability, timelessness.
- Practical requirements:
  - works at small sizes and in one colour
  - reproduces on any medium (embroidery, signage, favicon)
  - survives reduction and reversal
  - has a construction that can be documented
- Process: research (the organization, audience, competitors, history), positioning, many sketches, refinement, testing in real applications. The mark is judged in use, never alone on an artboard.

## The system
- Components:
  - primary and secondary marks, clear-space rules, minimum sizes
  - typography (families, roles, hierarchy)
  - colour palette with proportions and specifications (Pantone, CMYK, RGB, hex)
  - imagery (photography direction, illustration style)
  - iconography
  - graphic devices and patterns
  - grid and layout principles
  - motion and sound
  - voice and tone
  - templates for common applications
- Guidelines (standards manuals) document the rationale, construction, correct and incorrect use, specifications and examples.
- Landmark manuals:
  - Otl Aicher's Lufthansa identity (1962) and his 1972 Munich Olympics system with its pictograms
  - Unimark's New York City Transit Authority Graphics Standards Manual (Vignelli and Noorda, 1970)
  - Danne and Blackburn's NASA manual (1975, the "worm")

## The Vignelli Canon (2010)
Its table of contents was verified by search. The glosses below rely on summaries and should be checked against the book.
- Part one, "the intangibles":
  - semantics: search for the meaning of what you design, through research into its subject and history
  - syntactics: the grammar of the design (grid, typefaces, consistent relations between parts)
  - pragmatics: the design must be understood or it fails
  - discipline: attention to detail and coherence, which keeps design from becoming arbitrary
  - appropriateness: find the specific solution for this problem, independent of style
  - ambiguity: a design can carry several readings that add richness, which differs from vagueness
  - design is one: the same principles from a spoon to a city
  - visual power
  - further entries in the book include intellectual elegance, timelessness, responsibility and equity
- Part two, "the tangibles", covers paper sizes, grids, margins, columns, typefaces, type sizes, layout, scale, texture, white space and related craft. Vignelli famously worked with a very small set of typefaces.

## Flexible and dynamic identities
- Coherence through a stable rule set with variable outputs, instead of one fixed logo used everywhere.
- Examples:
  - Casa da Música (Sagmeister, 2007): one architectural shape, coloured by the image it sits with.
  - MIT Media Lab (TheGreenEyl, 2011): an algorithm generated tens of thousands of variants, one per person.
  - City of Melbourne (Landor, 2009): one M with many fills.
  - The Whitney Museum's "responsive W" (Experimental Jetset, 2013): a zigzag line that adapts to every format.
- The constant can be a shape, a typeface, a colour, a behaviour or a logic.
- Taxonomies of dynamic identities name kinds such as container, wallpaper, DNA, formula, customized and generative (Kreutz).
- The risk: too much variation dissolves recognition. Decide what must stay fixed.

## Experimental branding (RISD course)
- The course frames the brand designer as a multidisciplinary world-builder: illustrator, art director, typographer, web designer and production specialist at once.
- Branding is both a practical discipline and a speculative tool.
- Provocative constraints in the course:
  - Can an identity be generative?
  - What if a brand used only historic materials?
  - Can an identity be reimagined without changing any existing asset?
  The last one teaches that art direction and use, not just the assets, make the identity.
- The skill being built is balancing invention with coherence.
- Art direction extends a brand into the world: photography, sets, styling, casting, environments.
- Presentation: context, problem, insight, concept, the system shown working across applications, rationale. Tools named are Figma, Illustrator and Keynote.
- In the second half, students take a complete identity from research to final guidelines and applications.

## Identity Design (RISD course)
- Goes beyond "a logo plus rules" to work with:
  - language: naming, verbal identity, tone
  - materials
  - audience expectation and participation: identities people co-create or complete
- Two briefs: an arts organization (which needs flexibility across changing exhibitions and programmes) and a socially constructive campaign (which needs clarity and a call to action).

## Brand strategy basics
- Positioning (who it is for, what it offers, how it differs), audience, attributes, story.
- Audits: visual, competitive, touchpoint.
- Stakeholder interviews.
- Naming approaches: descriptive, invented, evocative, founder, acronym, each with trade-offs in clarity and protectability.

## Generative design (RISD course)
- The course says the designer's role has shifted from making single static outcomes to building systems of modules, algorithms and datasets.
- Karl Gerstner, Designing Programmes (1964):
  - design the programme (the rules) rather than the single solution
  - his "morphological box" lists parameters and their values, then combines them to explore a solution space systematically
- Lineage:
  - Bauhaus and Ulm systematic design
  - Sol LeWitt's instruction-based wall drawings
  - Muriel Cooper's Visible Language Workshop at MIT
  - John Maeda's Design By Numbers
  - Processing (Casey Reas and Ben Fry, 2001) and p5.js (Lauren McCarthy, 2013)
- Techniques:
  - randomness and noise (Perlin)
  - recursion and L-systems
  - particle systems
  - tiling (Truchet)
  - parametric variation
  - data-driven form
  - responsive layouts and variable type as generative systems
- The course's critical stance: automation makes endless variations without intent or context easy. The designer's answer is form-making methods that bring depth and reflection:
  - choose parameters that carry meaning
  - map data to form for a reason
  - set constraints
  - curate hard
  - edit outputs
  - treat the tool itself as something designed
- The course pairs screen work with physical, analog exercises to understand computation in the body and the hand.

## Computational poetics (RISD course)
- Digital text is an interface for emotional, interpersonal, industrial and political life. Language on screen is at once content, code and data.
- Methods for transforming text:
  - paper cut-up (Tzara, Burroughs and Gysin)
  - copy and paste
  - autocomplete
  - crowdsourcing
  - Markov chains
  - Oulipo constraints (lipograms, N+7)
  - natural language processing models
- Tools: HTML and CSS, p5.js, RiTa.js, Twine (hypertext), Scratch, early language models.
- The social side: the people whose labour produces digital language, such as transcription workers, human "computers" and web moderators.
