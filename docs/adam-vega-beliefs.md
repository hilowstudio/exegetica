# What Adam Vega Believed Most Passionately

*A synthesis drawn from a full reading of all 136 sermons in the Adam Vega Sermon Library — his preaching at Mercy's Door (Mascoutah, IL / Scott Air Force Base), spanning verse-by-verse series through John, Hosea, Galatians, Colossians, Habakkuk, Obadiah, 1–3 John, the Psalms of Ascent, and topical series on the female disciples and the Beatitudes.*

*A note on method and honesty: a handful of these sermons were preached by others — Michael Collins, Brett Barton, Chaplain Howard, Andrew Campbell — and a few texts were re-preached. Where a sermon was not Vega's own voice it is excluded from what follows. What is described here is drawn from the sermons he unmistakably owns (he names himself, his wife Sarah Rose, his sons Jack, Boaz, and Augustine, his co-pastors, his own story). The consistency across those sermons is the striking thing: the man preached one gospel, from many angles, for years, and almost never drifted from it.*

---

## The one thing

If you had to reduce Adam Vega's preaching to a single sentence he would gladly die on, it is this:

> **You are made right with God entirely by the finished work of Jesus Christ, received as a free gift through faith alone — never by anything you do, and never by anything you suffer to punish yourself.**

He said it, in his own words, "once, and I'll tell you a thousand times." It is the load-bearing wall of every sermon he preached, whatever the text. Genesis to Revelation, minor prophets to Beatitudes, he was always working his way back to the same clearing. Everything below is really an elaboration of it.

What makes his version of this old Protestant conviction *his* is the two-front war he waged for it. He was relentlessly against the obvious enemy — **self-righteousness**, the instinct to add your obedience, your morality, your church attendance, your "20 in the baby bottle" to Christ's work. But he was just as relentless, and more distinctively so, against the quieter enemy: **self-punishment**. He returned again and again to the person who "can't forgive himself," who flagellates, who isolates, who does penance, who offers God "the right amount of remorse" — and he called it what he believed it was: *the same pride as self-righteousness, only inverted.* "Either his punishment was sufficient or it was not." Both the man adding good works and the man adding hot tears are, in his preaching, doing the identical blasphemous thing — insisting that the perfect life and substitutionary death of Christ needs their contribution to be complete.

From this flowed his single most-repeated formula: **you work *from* an identity, not *for* one.** Christianity, he said, is the one religion where obedience flows *because of* who you already are, not *in order to become* someone. Christ did not hand you a clean slate and an assist to do better next time; "he filled in your résumé with his perfect life." On the day of judgment the record read over you is either the record of your deeds or the record of the Son's deeds credited to you — and he begged his people, every week, to make sure it was the latter.

## The engine underneath: union with Christ

Ask *how* Christ's righteousness becomes yours, and you reach the doctrine Vega invoked more than any other — so often he joked it came out of him "every other sermon" whether he planned it or not: **union with Christ by the baptism of the Holy Spirit.**

This was not decoration for him; it was the mechanism of the entire Christian life. Because you are united to Christ, his death counts as *your* death (you were "nailed to that cross with him" — four things pinned there: Jesus, the sign, the record of your debt, and *you*), and his resurrection as your resurrection. His obedience is not merely admired but *imputed*. And the same Spirit that raised him now indwells you, making you — his favorite word for it — a **new creation**. This is why he could preach both "there is nothing you can do" and "you really can walk in righteousness" without contradiction: the old self, the "flesh" hostile to God, was put to death; a new nature was placed in you; the good works that follow are the *evidence* of an identity received, never its price.

He pressed this to its most vivid claim repeatedly: **you are now the dwelling place of God.** The Ark, the temple, the Holy of Holies — all of it fulfilled first in Christ ("the fullness of deity dwelt bodily") and then, through him, in you. "How long would you have to sit with your brother before you cock your head and see God living in him?" To see Jesus is to see the Father; and now, to see a Spirit-filled believer love you, is to see Christ himself at work — "I know who I'd be without him."

## What he believed *about the cross*

Vega preached a cross with wrath in it, and he named the alternative as a defect. **"A wrathless cross is an incomplete gospel."** He went out of his way — even naming popular teachers he otherwise respected — to reject the view that Jesus merely "sucked the venom out," removing sin's power without paying its penalty. In his telling, Christ did not only cleanse your nature; he *satisfied the just wrath of a holy God* against your sin. He "drank the cup to the dregs" and said "it is finished," so that — a phrase Vega loved — there is "not one drop of wrath left" for the children of God.

This is why, in his preaching, **suffering is radically reoriented for the believer.** Because the wrath was spent on Christ, hardship in a Christian's life is *never* God's punishment or displeasure — it is at most a Father's *discipline* (which he sharply distinguished from destruction), and more often the very instrument through which God produces Christlikeness and spreads the gospel. "Goodness comes *through* suffering" — the cross is the proof. He held this with unusual tenderness, having watched a lot of it: his wife Sarah's lupus and chronic pain ("the longest unanswered prayer of my life is that my wife would be healed"), his father's death, his own bout of panic attacks, which he confessed from the pulpit.

## What he believed *about God's freedom*

Vega was a convinced monergist, and he preached it as good news even while acknowledging it offends. Salvation, from first to last, is God's work. "**The Lord opened her heart.**" Regeneration precedes faith. Faith itself is "a gift *from* God, not a gift you give *to* him" — and he noted, from John 6, that crowds *shrink* the moment a preacher says so, because nothing offends the human heart like being told it did not choose God but was chosen. He preached election as "a discomfort until it's a comfort," and captured the resistance in his wife's honest confession: "I don't want to be *carried* across the finish line — I want to have *run* the race."

But he insisted the comfort is enormous once received. If your standing rested on you, it could be lost; because it rests on God's choice and Christ's finished work, it *cannot*. Hence his equally passionate insistence on **eternal security and adoption**: "you can't fail your way out because you couldn't work your way in"; "he doesn't *unadopt* anybody." He drove it home with the image of the new birth certificate, the toddler running from his father in three-inch steps, the child held in the Father's and the Son's overlapping hands. And crucially — because God foreknew and folded in even Judas, even Herod and Pilate doing "what your hand had predestined" — Vega preached that *no evil ever catches God off guard*. Your own sin, and the evil done *to* you, were both accounted for and are even now being worked into his redemptive plan. He offered this specifically to two people in every room: the one drowning in guilt over what he'd done, and the one wounded by what was done to him.

## What he believed *about Jesus' body — and yours*

Few convictions animated him more than the sheer physicality of the faith. He hammered the **incarnation** — "God right here," not a general idea but a specific man whose fingers went into a deaf man's ears — and treated the confession of "Jesus Christ come in the flesh" as *the* test of who is truly a brother. He preached the resurrection as bodily and defended it against every swoon theory ("dead dead," the blood and water, the pericardium).

And he refused to let his people spiritualize the future. Over and over he corrected what he considered a sub-biblical, half-pagan picture of heaven as disembodied souls or "brains" or "stardust." **You are getting a new body.** The hope is the *New Earth* — the material world renewed, every tear dried, worship offered with glorified hands and feet. This governed how he read almost everything: this present world has been "subjected to futility" on purpose, precisely so we would stop demanding that it be our home and would long for the one that is coming. A remarkable amount of his pastoral counsel reduces to: *stop asking this world to be the next one.*

## What he believed *about the Word and the church*

Vega was an expository preacher by deep conviction, not habit — "we go left to right through whole books of the Bible" — and he tied it to a high view of Scripture as the *codified teaching of the apostles*, carrying "the full backing of the authority of Jesus Christ." He told his people, repeatedly, "the only reason to listen to anything I say is if it's really flowing from *here*," the Word. He balanced this with an equally high view of the Spirit as the believer's true teacher, the pastor's job being reminder, not innovation.

His ecclesiology was warm, embodied, and pointedly local. He preached the **priesthood of all believers** (including, explicitly and at length, the office of deacon open to women, and the honored ministries of single and widowed women — Phoebe, Lydia, Mary of Bethany, Mary Magdalene). He prized **hospitality as the first mark of conversion**, **confession and "walking in the light"** as the ordinary mode of Christian life ("confession of sin is confession of faith"), and care for **the whole person, body and soul**. He set all of this against two errors he named often: disembodied hyper-spirituality ("peace be with you, go be fed" with no hands or feet) and gospel-less social good ("anyone can dig a well — who digs a well *in Jesus' name*?"). And he was sharply critical of celebrity and "downloaded-sermon" Christianity: the face-to-face local church, where people actually know and love you, over the one-way teacher who "doesn't know your name."

Unity, for him, was not a project to achieve but a *fact to behold* — beautiful precisely because "you didn't make it." "By our love for one another the world knows we're his"; a professed Christian who does not love the brothers is, by 1 John's logic, simply "not one of them yet" — "that doesn't make you my enemy, it makes you my mission field."

## What he preached *against*

His polemics were consistent enough to be a portrait in themselves. He was against:

- **Legalism / "Jesus-plus"** — the Judaizer instinct to add the law (or its modern equivalents: prosperity, family values, self-fulfillment, respectability, mere morality) to the gospel of grace. The gospel is free, "but you must lay everything else down to take it."
- **The "deserving poor."** This is one of his sharper and less common notes: he rejected not only self-righteousness but its counterfeit humility — the idea that your neediness, your wounds, your victimhood, earn God's pity as a kind of merit. "There are no *deserving* poor," "no good people in the family of God" — only "dogs made children" by grace. Neediness is not a claim on God; it is simply the truth about all of us.
- **The gospel fused with the culture war.** He repeatedly insisted that Israel is an allegory for *the people of God*, not the United States; that Christians "war the world by evangelism, and war within the church by discipline," and not by trying to make the surrounding culture "look Christian." He warned against making a nation, a party, or an ideology into "Jesus-plus."
- **A domesticated, invented Jesus.** "There are 50 million Jesuses worshiped in this country, and only one of them is real" — and the real one "won't be characterized by his followers." He preached the offense of Christ's *exclusivity* ("no one comes to the Father except through me") as the necessary scandal, tracing the resistance to it back to the original sin of claiming the throne for oneself.

## The temperament of the man

Two things stand out about *how* he preached, and both are convictions in their own right.

First, he modeled from the pulpit the confession he demanded from the pews. He told on himself constantly — bullying his little brother Jake as a boy and seeking his forgiveness twenty-five years later; wearing "the jersey" of Christianity for years before he was actually born again; walking into gospel community "fresh off a fight with my wife"; his panic attacks; his opportunistic thoughts during the COVID market crash. He believed that honesty is *the mode* of the Christian life, not a pragmatic tool — you "walk in the light first, come what may" — and he refused to preach a vulnerability he wouldn't practice.

Second, he insisted, against a stoic and sentimental Christian culture both, that God wants the *whole* person — including the tears. He preached lament as faithful (Habakkuk, the Psalms), told people to "bring your doubts and anger to God with open hands," warned them not to use Scripture to *numb* rather than *commune*, and taught — with a touching turn to neurobiology — that "nobody ever cried alone," because God bottles the tears and counts the tossings. "God does his best work in the dark."

---

## What it all came to

Strip the 136 sermons down and Adam Vega believed, most passionately, in a **God who does the whole thing.** Who chooses the unwilling, opens the closed heart, satisfies his own wrath in his own Son, unites the sinner to Christ, indwells him with his own Spirit, credits him a perfect righteousness he could never earn and could never lose, disciplines but never condemns him, folds even his sin and his sufferings into a good and unstoppable plan, and will one day raise his body to worship on a renewed earth where every tear is dried. Man's part in this, for Vega, was almost embarrassingly small: to stop trying — to stop climbing *and* to stop punishing — and to receive. "Breathe out," he said, over and over. "It's his five-thousand-year plan, not yours."

And then, having received it, to do the one thing the gospel actually asks: to go and gather the others. "It's why you're still here." He didn't believe God needed him to do it — "I have many people in this city" — but he believed a person who has truly tasted being *chosen*, by no merit of his own, cannot help but turn and set that same undeserved affection on someone else. That, finally, is the shape of the man's faith: everything is grace, all the way down, and the only fitting response is worship that overflows into the same reckless, choosing, self-giving love that first found you.
