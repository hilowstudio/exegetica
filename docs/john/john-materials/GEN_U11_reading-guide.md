# Reading guide, Unit 11: A note on the text

> Passage: John 7:53-8:11
> Big question: your Bible has a note here saying the oldest copies don't include this story. What should you make of that?
> Memory work: no new verse this unit. Keep your six review verses sharp.

## How to use this guide

You don't turn this in. Keep it open next to your Reading Journal while we work the passage in class.

This unit is a little different. It's one lesson, and it does two things. First, we deal honestly with a note you'll see in your Bible. Second, we read one of the most famous stories about Jesus and mercy.

- **Read** the passage first, slowly, and look at the top of it in your own Bible.
- **Observe** means say what the text actually says.
- **Interpret** asks what it means and who Jesus is in it.
- **Respond** asks what it's asking of you.

## First: the note in your Bible

Open your ESV to John 8 and look right above verse 1. You'll see a line like this: "The earliest manuscripts do not include 7:53-8:11." And the whole story is inside brackets.

That's not a mistake, and nobody's hiding it. Here's what it means and why it should actually strengthen your trust in your Bible.

- We don't have the original page John wrote. We have thousands of very old handwritten copies.
- Scholars compare those copies line by line, letter by letter, to figure out exactly what the originals said.
- This story doesn't show up in the oldest and best copies of John. In some copies it shows up in a different spot entirely. So the people who make our Bibles flag it, right on the page.
- That flag is proof they're telling you the truth. They're not sneaking anything past you.

Two more things worth knowing:

- The story is really old. A Christian writer named Papias knew a version of it around the year 130. The church has always believed this really happened.
- Nothing about the Christian faith changes because of this one passage. Jesus is still God (chapter 1). He still dies and rises. The whole rest of the Bible is solid. This is one paragraph, and it's marked.

So we read it with open eyes: very old, almost certainly a true event from Jesus' life, probably not part of what John originally wrote, and completely safe and good to read.

## The story: a woman, a trap, and mercy (John 7:53-8:11)

**Read it.** John chapter 7 verse 53 through chapter 8 verse 11.

Jesus is teaching in the temple early in the morning. Religious leaders drag in a woman caught in adultery and demand he decide whether to stone her. It's a trap. Jesus stoops down, writes on the ground, and finally says, "Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone." One by one, the accusers leave. Then Jesus speaks to the woman.

- **Observe.** In verse 4 they say she was "caught in the act of adultery." That takes two people. Who's missing from this scene? What does that tell you about the men?
- **Observe.** In verse 9, in what order do the accusers leave? Who goes first?
- **Interpret.** In verse 11 Jesus says two things: "Neither do I condemn you" and "go, and from now on sin no more." Why does he say both? What would go wrong if he only said the first, or only the second?
- **Interpret.** Jesus was the one person there who had never sinned, the only one actually qualified to throw a stone. He's the one who drops it. What does that show you about him?
- **Respond.** Be honest: in this story, are you more like the woman caught with no defense, or the men holding stones for someone else's sin? Write which, and why.

## Key things to know

- **The trap.** The leaders used the woman to catch Jesus between two bad options: break the law of Moses, or break Roman law. He escapes the trap and rescues her.
- **"Without sin."** Jesus means a conscience clear of this kind of sin. He doesn't cancel the law. He shows that these particular men, with dirty hands and a rigged case, can't be the ones to carry it out.
- **Grace and truth.** Back in John 1:14, John said Jesus came "full of grace and truth." Here you see both. Truth: he calls sin what it is. Grace: he refuses to condemn her.

## Where this lesson lands on Jesus

The only man in that courtyard who could have thrown a stone is the one who put it down. He tells the truth about the woman's sin and refuses to condemn her, and he can do both because he's on his way to a cross where the punishment she earned will fall on him instead. "Neither do I condemn you. Go, and from now on sin no more."
