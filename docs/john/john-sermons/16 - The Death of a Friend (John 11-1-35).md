# The Death of a Friend (John 11:1-35)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 11:1-35

---

We're continuing in John 11 this morning, starting at verse 1. I want you to know up front that this one is highly personal for me, and I may get more personal than you're used to. This passage contains three of the most significant and meaningful sentences in the whole Bible for my own life, so there's no way for me to preach it any other way.

Some of you know, and many of you probably don't, that my wife is afflicted with a number of autoimmune disorders. The chief one she battles is lupus. Some of you know what that is and some don't. The heart of it is that her immune system doesn't like her, so it attacks otherwise healthy tissue.

It involves many organs and systems. It attacks what it wants, when it wants, and that causes pain. When it isn't causing pain, it's still causing damage, and the damage builds over time. The treatment for lupus is to shut down the immune system with a class of medications called immunosuppressants. They shut everything down, which leaves you open to all the things you want your immune system to fight off. So she's either in pain or she's sick. That's what it comes down to. She picks up the things the rest of us normally fight off.

That would be hard enough on its own. But if you know anything about autoimmune diseases, you know they often come in threes. My wife also battles rheumatoid arthritis, which you probably know: it's much the same thing, only attacking her joints rather than her organ systems. And she has something called interstitial cystitis.

It attacks the lining of her bladder, so she gets sharp, stabbing pains in her lower abdomen. Put it all together and you have to keep the immune system shut down, so she's always taking immunosuppressants and always getting sick. And she still gets what we call breakthrough flares, when the immune system overcomes the immunosuppressants. When that happens, she takes broad steroids to shut everything down again. But if you don't do that quickly enough, it can do irreparable harm, and that has happened to my wife. She has damage to her small and large nerve fibers, which sends shooting pains up her arms and legs all the time. So she takes another class of medications to block the nerve pain, and that causes brain fog.

She says, I'm not as mentally sharp as I'd like to be, but my choice is pain or brain fog. Entirely separate from all of this, my wife has another chronic condition: she forms a kind of kidney stone called brushite. They're a rare form of calcium phosphate stone, denser and harder than ordinary stones, faster-forming and very large, and they resist shock-wave and ultrasonic lithotripsy, which means they can't be broken up the way stones normally are.

The surgeons have to go in and blast them apart, and then extract the pieces surgically. I'm losing some of you, because you're thinking, I thought this was Pastor Adam, not Doctor Adam. But I didn't get here overnight either.

That's ten years of walking with my wife through the suffering of doctor's visits and hospital stays. You pick up the jargon as you seek answers and healing. One way I know I'm with someone who is battling chronic illness, or loving someone who is, is that they start talking like a doctor. They use terms normal people don't know, because they're living on medical websites searching for answers.

It's hard. The result of these brushite stones is that my wife is less likely to be left stone-free after they're surgically removed. They resist every kind of medical treatment, so they keep coming back. Every year, often several times a year, my wife is passing stones or needing surgery to remove them. So if you picture a day in my wife's life, it's most often either pain or sickness, and it doesn't let up. I bring this up because over these years of walking through pain and suffering with my wife, I have heard, or said myself if I'm brutally honest, every well-intended but theologically wrong thing you can say to a suffering person. And living my life among the church, I've found that we have a woefully underdeveloped theology of pain and suffering.

So we just say things, and those things add to the suffering when they're not from the Lord. That's why I'm telling you up front about the three sentences in this passage that speak to me, so that you can listen for why they speak to me. If you fit any of what I've described, if you have battled pain and suffering yourself, especially chronic suffering, or you consider yourself a friend to someone who has, I hope these sentences will minister to you too. We'll come to them in order, but here they are up front. The first is chapter 11, verse 3: the sisters sent to him, saying, "Lord, he whom you love is ill."

Write that down. He whom you love is ill. The next comes a little further on, verses 5 and 6: "Now Jesus loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus. So, when he heard that Lazarus was ill, he stayed two days longer in the place where he was."

Write that down too. When he heard that Lazarus, whom he loved, was ill, he stayed two days longer where he was. That's a hard one. If it doesn't strike you as strange, you're reading your Bible too fast.

And lastly, verse 35: "Jesus wept." Jesus wept. Write that down.

Let's make our way through it. John 11, verses 1 to 4: "Now a certain man was ill, Lazarus of Bethany, the village of Mary and her sister Martha. It was Mary who anointed the Lord with ointment and wiped his feet with her hair, whose brother Lazarus was ill. So the sisters sent to him, saying, 'Lord, he whom you love is ill.' But when Jesus heard it he said, 'This illness does not lead to death. It is for the glory of God, so that the Son of God may be glorified through it.'"

Let me set the scene. The disciples will point it out in a minute: Jesus has just narrowly escaped being stoned by the Jews. We saw last week how he again made himself equal with God and brought their hatred on himself. Having narrowly escaped, he's out across the Jordan, in the area where John had first baptized.

Mary and Martha know where he is, and they send word that his friend Lazarus is ill. We don't know much about Lazarus, but we know he was dear to Jesus. Mary, Martha and Lazarus, sisters and brother, were all dear to Jesus. And John points to a particularly intimate moment between Mary and Jesus: it was Mary who anointed the Lord with ointment and wiped his feet with her hair.

You may know that story. It was her brother who was ill. These were beloved friends of Jesus who showed him great hospitality when he passed through. Some have suggested that Lazarus was in the garden of Gethsemane, the unnamed young man in the linen cloth who ran away naked when the guards arrived.

We don't know that. But these people, the ones Jesus loves, send to him, and this is the word that reaches his ear: "Lord, he whom you love is ill." This sentence is so important in my household, and needs to be important in yours, because the church today is plagued by bad doctrine that says sickness is a sign that you are out of God's favor. It's not so different from what we saw a few chapters ago, when the disciples asked Jesus about the man born blind: who sinned, this man or his parents?

Jesus says, neither. But it's a very common idea, even in Christian circles, that if you're sick, you're out of God's favor. Something is wrong between you and God, and he's doing this to you.

This is his displeasure with you. So if you could just work out what sin he's trying to uproot, or what displeasure he's showing you, you could make yourself well by cleaning up that area of your life. Or maybe it's not that extreme for you. Maybe it's more like, God is trying to teach me a lesson. I'm hard-headed. I'm proud.

I'm all these things, so he's using illness as a tool to make me a better person. And once I've learned my lesson, once I've reached some level of righteousness, hopefully he'll lift this hard lesson from me. He's using suffering to make something great out of me. It's a pull-yourself-up-by-your-bootstraps view of illness: God will never give you more than you can handle.

I just wish he didn't trust me so much. Have you ever heard that garbage theology? The Lord gives us far more than we can handle. But the sisters send to him and say, "Lord." They acknowledge him as Lord.

"He whom you love": they acknowledge his love for Lazarus. "Is ill": they acknowledge his illness. All of these are true at the same time. Christ has not surrendered his lordship, and Lazarus is ill.

Christ has not surrendered his love, and Lazarus is ill. He is still Lord and he still loves Lazarus, while at the same time Lazarus is ill. The sisters understood this and addressed him that way, and we'd do well to do the same. So I want to speak against another lie. Maybe it's not that Jesus doesn't love me and that's why I'm ill.

Or that Jesus is unhappy with me and that's why I'm ill. It's that Jesus isn't Lord, and that's why I'm ill. He isn't able. He isn't in control. Somehow he doesn't know.

Somehow he's unaware. Somehow he doesn't care. Or he wants to, but he can't do anything. He's not Lord, and that's the problem. He's a well-meaning Jesus.

He sees and he cares. He's heartbroken over it. But he's more like Adam: Adam the husband, who cares that his wife is suffering but can't fix it. So I look to Jesus as a well-meaning friend, but I don't acknowledge him as Lord, because surely he can't do anything. Otherwise, why am I sick?

But when the report reaches Jesus' ear, "Lord, he whom you love is ill," he says, "This illness does not lead to death. It is for the glory of God, so that the Son of God may be glorified through it." Hold on to verse 4; we'll come back to it. Verses 5 to 7: "Now Jesus loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus. So, when he heard that Lazarus was ill, he stayed two days longer in the place where he was. Then after this he said to the disciples, 'Let us go to Judea again.'"

So he gets the word, and it reaches him in time. Lazarus isn't dead yet. Jesus has heard that Lazarus is sick.

And how does he respond? Jesus loved Martha and Mary and Lazarus. So when he heard Lazarus was ill, he stayed two days longer where he was. Doesn't that strike you as strange? Doesn't it?

When we talk about long suffering, about chronic illness, about a long wait for deliverance, we need to turn to sentences like this. That's why this is such an important message in my home. Here's another thing we tend to believe: that my suffering is because I didn't call out to him in time. The word didn't reach his ear in time. I waited too long. I was too far gone before I turned to Jesus for help.

The word definitely reached him in time. In fact, it reached him too soon, because Jesus was resolved that Lazarus needed to die. He needed to wait long enough that when he arrived, Lazarus would already be in the tomb. Jesus could have. Jesus knew.

Jesus was able. Jesus cared. Jesus loved Lazarus. And he waited two days before heading back. He waited two days.

Some of us have been waiting, waiting so long. And in our suffering, our pain, our illness, as we wait for bodily deliverance from what afflicts us, we tell ourselves that the delay of Jesus is his displeasure. That we haven't done what's required to bring his favor on our lives. That he's waiting for us to have enough faith, or pray the right words, or do our part, or meet him halfway. Or that he sent deliverance and we missed it, and we're getting it wrong. But we usually have no category for the idea that nothing is wrong on your part.

That Jesus is fully aware, and of his own will, for his own good purposes, he stays two more days. That's a hard truth. I'm glad there's more Scripture. Let's try to make sense of it.

He has waited two more days where he was. Then, verses 7 and 8: "Then after this he said to the disciples, 'Let us go to Judea again.' The disciples said to him, 'Rabbi, the Jews were just now seeking to stone you, and are you going there again?'" To go to Lazarus, Jesus has to risk his own safety, because in Jerusalem they're actively seeking to stone him, and Bethany is only two miles off.

It's risky enough that the disciples bring it up: we can't go back there. And Jesus answers, verses 9 and 10, "Are there not twelve hours in the day? If anyone walks in the day, he does not stumble, because he sees the light of this world. But if anyone walks in the night, he stumbles, because the light is not in him."

He gently rebukes his disciples here. Are we going to start walking in fear? Is that what we're going to do? You're with me, the light of the world, and you want to sneak around under cover of darkness?

Let's wait until things die down? For fear of the Jews, we're going to fail our friend? No. We won't do any such thing. Instead he says, verse 11:

"After saying these things, he said to them, 'Our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep, but I go to awaken him.'" We know Lazarus has died. That's what he means. But Jesus speaks of it as sleep. For him, he's simply going to wake him up.

Of course this confuses the disciples, and I love how honest they are. Verses 12 and 13: "The disciples said to him, 'Lord, if he has fallen asleep, he will recover.' Now Jesus had spoken of his death, but they thought that he meant taking rest in sleep." I think it's worth pausing on. They're living with Jesus, the Messiah, the Christ, the Lord Jesus.

And they think he doesn't understand how rest works. Oh, we understand, Jesus. But if he's just resting, he'll recover.

Verses 14 and 15: "Then Jesus told them plainly, 'Lazarus has died, and for your sake I am glad that I was not there, so that you may believe. But let us go to him.'" Listen closely to that: for your sake I'm glad I wasn't there, so that you may believe. Verse 16: "So Thomas, called the Twin, said to his fellow disciples, 'Let us also go, that we may die with him.'"

That we may die with him. When Jesus says, for your sake I'm glad I wasn't there, so that you may believe, we start to see the kind of belief he wants for his people, just as we're introduced to doubting Thomas in all his doubting glory. You might think "die with him" means die with Lazarus.

He means die with Jesus. They've just said, we can't go back there; they were just trying to stone you. Jesus says, our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep. And they say, oh, don't worry.

And Thomas says, all right, let's all go, so we can die with him. Jesus is walking into his stoning, so let's all go die. And Jesus says, for your sake I'm glad I wasn't there, so that you may believe.

Let us go to him. Verses 17 to 19: "Now when Jesus came, he found that Lazarus had already been in the tomb four days. Bethany was near Jerusalem, about two miles off, and many of the Jews had come to Martha and Mary to console them concerning their brother."

This is a small note, but if Jesus had gone to Lazarus before he died, he'd have had a far better chance of avoiding the Jews. Bethany was only two miles from the city, but he could have reached Lazarus there without being noticed by many of them. The sisters are surrounded by Jews. And he arrives. Verses 20 to 22: "So when Martha heard that Jesus was coming, she went and met him, but Mary remained seated in the house. Martha said to Jesus, 'Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died. But even now I know that whatever you ask from God, God will give you.'"

That sounds like a wonderful declaration of faith, and it is. I don't want to take anything away from our sister Martha. She said, whatever you ask from God, God will give you. Then Jesus says to her, "Your brother will rise again."

She has no category for his meaning that literally, and right now. She's just said, whatever you ask, he will do. He says, your brother will rise. That was mainstream Jewish belief, at least among the Pharisees. Verse 27: "She said to him, 'Yes, Lord; I believe that you are the Christ, the Son of God, who is coming into the world.'" This is obviously the crux of the Christian faith.

We believe that by faith alone, in Christ alone, according to the Scriptures alone, we receive resurrection. We will not taste death. We are united with Christ in his resurrection, and we will live forever in the presence of our Creator God.

This is the heart of Christianity, and Jesus declares it with his own mouth. I am the resurrection. I am the life.

Whoever believes in me, though he die, yet shall he live. It sounds as if he's talking about two groups of people, doesn't it? Those who die and yet live, and those who live and believe in him and will never die. There were two groups of people he was referring to when he walked the earth. There were those who had died.

These were our Old Testament brothers and sisters, who believed by faith in a Christ who would come but never lived to see his first coming, and they died. And he says, yet shall they live. But everyone who lives and believes in me, he says to Martha, shall never die. Shall never die. Like the thief on the cross, the day you close your eyes on this side of eternity, you'll open them to your new life in Christ. Jesus told that thief, today you will be with me in paradise.

I don't know that we really understand this. I don't know that we really get our heads around it. We'll see how people respond to Lazarus next week, though we know how this ends. Seeing Lazarus will be great, but there's someone else you're really hoping to see, and you will, if they were found in Christ.

Do you believe this? When you're asked, do you believe this, is your answer, yes, Lord, I believe that you are the Christ, the Son of God, who has come into the world? Is that your answer? Because the way we handle pain and suffering tells us a lot about our theology of who Jesus is.

The pain and suffering in your life may be a real diagnostic of where you stand today in your understanding of the Messiah. Here's what I mean. He became sin. And now we look to him to understand what it means to be human. We don't present ourselves to him as worthy. Jesus said it with his own mouth, so he calls us to believe, and here Martha says, I believe that you are the Christ, the Son of God, who is coming into the world.
