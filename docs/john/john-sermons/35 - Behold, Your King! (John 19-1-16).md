# Behold, Your King! (John 19:1-16)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 19:1-16

---

If you've been with us the last few weeks, you know my tone has been on the heavy side. It's been tough, because as we move through the passion account, whether it's Annas or Caiaphas or the religious mob or Pilate, the story is laced with wickedness, and I've been holding out sober warnings about how these kinds of wickedness can take root in us so that we're as blind to them as they were. It's been hard. Fortunately, this passage really centers on Jesus. We get to look at him.

Sadly, though, he's suffering in this passage. My prayer as I prepared was that the church would be ministered to, that the Lord would draw near to you as you behold the suffering Messiah, that he would do a work in the church to reorient the way we think about suffering and about wrath and judgment, and that we'd be renewed in a full understanding of what actually happened in Jesus Christ's life, suffering and death, and ultimately his resurrection. There's a specific doctrine I want to get after this morning. I'll lay out the problem up front, and then we'll work through how this Gospel account shows the error. Often, even in Christian circles, we reduce what happened at the cross, in the suffering and death of Jesus Christ, to something like a soldier jumping on a grenade.

That's a beautiful picture of sacrifice, a soldier jumping on a grenade, but it's a very incomplete way to see the atoning work of Jesus. Here's why. Often, when we think about the cross of Christ, we say it freed us from the power of sin and will free us from the presence of sin, but it had nothing to do with freeing us from the penalty of sin. In that picture, sin itself was the bad guy Jesus came after, the thing he was trying to rescue us from.

Sin is another entity, a grenade rolled into the room that threatened to harm us. And Jesus loved us so much that he threw himself on the grenade to spare us the consequences of sin, maybe the presence of sin, the power of sin. He just wanted to save us from sin.

And he did do those two things. We'll talk about them a little this morning. But something is missing if that's all Jesus was doing on the cross.

He wasn't only saving us from the presence of sin or the power of sin. Jesus came to save us from the penalty for sin. We didn't think of sin as an enemy when he came. We weren't asking to be saved from sin. We enjoyed it.

What stood against us was the just wrath of God against sin, and the punishment and penalty coming for sin. There's a wrathless cross being preached in pulpits all across America today: something beautiful happened at Calvary, but it had nothing to do with satisfying wrath. It's not that God was angry, the story goes; it's that God cared so much about the damage of sin that he had to find a way to fix it. But what he was fixing had nothing to do with the punishment for sin.

If that's the case, it's really hard to make sense of how bloody this account is. We're going to behold Jesus this morning in a way that's really uncomfortable, and it's only good news if it's accomplishing something. I want to hold out to you up front that it was far more than a man jumping on a grenade. Let's see how. Verses 1 to 3: "Then Pilate took Jesus and flogged him. And the soldiers twisted together a crown of thorns and put it on his head and arrayed him in a purple robe. They came up to him, saying, 'Hail, King of the Jews!' and struck him with their hands."

If you were with us last week, we did a deep dive on Pilate, and the last words out of his mouth were that he found no guilt in the man. This morning the same Pilate who finds no guilt in Jesus takes him and has him flogged. First I need you to see what that means. The law of Moses capped a beating at forty lashes, and the Jews stopped at thirty-nine to be safe. This was a Roman scourging, and Rome set no limit. They stripped him, bound his hands to a post with his back bent, and soldiers whipped, flogged and scourged him with a whip of leather thongs fitted with pieces of bone and lead to rip and tear away the flesh. They kept at it until they were worn out or their officer called them off. They did this brutally and mercilessly.

Josephus tells of a man the Roman governor had "whipped till his bones were laid bare." Eusebius records Christian martyrs torn by the scourge "even to the innermost veins and arteries." Men sometimes died under the whip before they ever reached a cross, and scourging was called "the intermediate death." They tore Jesus' flesh to the bone and left him mangled, bloodied and scourged. Pilate says, I find no guilt in him. And then there's the crown Jesus wears on his head. The only crown he ever wore in this life was a crown of thorns. And on top of the lashes, the exposed bone and muscle and tendon and blood, they throw a purple robe, the color of majesty, and they begin to mock him.

This King of heaven came down into human flesh and received the blows of the people he came to save, as they used his true title, King of the Jews, to jeer at him and mock him. You have to see it. Jesus, our God, became punchable. When we talk about Jesus dying for us, we sometimes skip right over this magnificent truth: to die for us, he had to become killable. He took on flesh, and it's on full display here. I've said it before, but I want you to know that my faith was transformed the moment it really dawned on me that Jesus was fully man.

I had always imagined Jesus like a human-shaped robot with a little alien inside pulling the controls. He looked like us so that we could relate to him, but he wasn't really one of us. But he let us break him open. And we saw that he bleeds blood. We saw that his flesh tears like ours.

It's all on display. Jesus becoming fully man is incredibly important, because the doctrine I want to teach this morning is one some of you know and some of you may deny: the doctrine of penal substitutionary atonement. If that's a new five-dollar term for you, write it down. Penal substitutionary atonement.

It's a fancy way of saying that Jesus died an atoning death that paid the punishment, the penalty, that was due to us, and he couldn't do it unless he was one of us. So first we need to see his humanity before we can delight in what it accomplished. He became flesh and bone. He became punchable. He became killable.

And then he handed himself over to have these things done to him. So this morning we have Pilate taking a man in whom he finds no fault, flogging him, beating him, pressing a crown of thorns into his head, mocking him, dressing him up, striking him, and bringing him back before the people: hail, King of the Jews. This fulfills Isaiah 53, prophesied some seven hundred years before it happened, starting in verse 3: "He was despised and rejected by men, a man of sorrows and acquainted with grief;

"and as one from whom men hide their faces he was despised, and we esteemed him not. Surely he has borne our griefs and carried our sorrows; yet we esteemed him stricken, smitten by God, and afflicted. But he was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities; upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace, and with his wounds we are healed."

Verse 7: "He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth; like a lamb that is led to the slaughter, and like a sheep that before its shearers is silent, so he opened not his mouth. By oppression and judgment he was taken away; and as for his generation, who considered that he was cut off out of the land of the living, stricken for the transgression of my people? And they made his grave with the wicked and with a rich man in his death, although he had done no violence, and there was no deceit in his mouth. Yet it was the will of the LORD to crush him;

"he has put him to grief; when his soul makes an offering for guilt, he shall see his offspring; he shall prolong his days; the will of the LORD shall prosper in his hand. Out of the anguish of his soul he shall see and be satisfied; by his knowledge shall the righteous one, my servant, make many to be accounted righteous, and he shall bear their iniquities."

"Because he poured out his soul to death and was numbered with the transgressors; yet he bore the sin of many, and makes intercession for the transgressors." Amen? This was no pointless death, and he didn't simply jump on a grenade. He planned every step, so that he would take on flesh and lay it down again in a very specific way for a very specific reason, to fulfill what was written: that we had a great need, and that need could be met only by a perfect atoning sacrifice.

This is the testimony of all of Scripture. We needed saving not only from the effects of sin, not only from its power, but from its penalty. Our just God has seen our sin. He has looked on our rebellion, and his wrath has been kindled. The wages of sin is death.

Don't forget that the curses in the garden fell on all mankind. Not only was the ground cursed; all of mankind was thrown into spiritual death. That's where we get the teaching we preach week after week: we were all dead in the trespasses in which we once walked, but have been made alive together with Christ. Our spiritual death was meant to be eternal: eternal separation from God.

That was the penalty. That was the wrath of God against sinful man. And yet all through the Old Testament we see these stories, don't we? Abraham and Isaac, where God provides a ram. The sacrificial system in ancient times, where he allowed the priesthood to stand in and make atoning sacrifices for the people. And after the priest made a sacrifice for the people, what happened?

The day would come again when they needed to make another, and another, and another. The Passover, where the destroyer, the judgment of God, passed through a specific land on a specific night. The people needed a substitute to protect them from the wrath of God. So when they painted the doorposts and lintel of their houses with the blood of a spotless lamb, they were spared the judgment of God. All these Old Testament examples point us to a truth.

We need help. We need atonement. We need someone to stand in for us, because we can't pay for our sin. If we tried, we would be, as I said last week, always paying and never paid up. All the lesser sacrifices were temporary at best, pointing us to our need for the perfect sacrifice. And Christ Jesus came in human flesh to be that perfect sacrifice for us.

He became our atoning Lamb. He became our sacrifice for sin. He stood in for us and bore the wrath of God on himself, dying on a Roman cross. One man, receiving the rejection of the Father.

How could one man's descent into death be enough to atone for the sins of all mankind? I'll answer you two ways. First, because the Father said it is enough, and that's good enough. His wrath was satisfied as he looked on.

But he gives us more than that. Second, we can know it because of Christ's perfection as an atoning sacrifice. Jesus Christ came and lived a sinless life. Do we really grasp that he lived a sinless life?

A sinless life. I've said it like this before: take the greatest commandment, just one command, and apply it to yourself. Love the Lord your God with all your heart, soul, mind and strength, and love your neighbor as yourself. Take only the first half: love the Lord your God with all your heart, soul, mind and strength. None of us has ever done that for one second of our lives, but Jesus did it every moment of his, along with everything else that honored the Father.

He walked perfectly in the will of God, not only in what he abstained from, but in what he did. Jesus Christ lived a life of perfect righteousness before God. He perfectly pleased the Father. Jesus Christ was that good, and is that good. We have to see him as fully human first before we can really marvel at how good he is.

He stood in as one of us, God and man brought together, and lived the life we were meant to live, so that when he willingly offered it up as an atoning sacrifice, it was so good that it satisfied the wrath of a holy God against all mankind. Goodness. He lived for you. He suffered for you. He died for you, and he rose for you.

Those are the mechanics of the gospel. But do we take the mechanics and separate them from the person? Do we really see him and understand what we mean when we say, he lived for you? When we read this, do we understand that he suffered for you? Seeing that perfect sacrificial Lamb bloodied and scourged, beaten and mocked, tried and denied and crucified, is like the law in the Old Testament: it's a mirror that shows us how badly we needed atonement. But it also shows us how good our God is, that he would go to these lengths.

The horror of the cross shows us the wickedness of sin and the goodness of God at the same time. You're worse than you think. The problem is worse than we think, and the cross shouts it. But he's better than we think, and the cross shouts that too. It's both.

He suffers for you. Verses 4 and 5: "Pilate went out again and said to them, 'See, I am bringing him out to you that you may know that I find no guilt in him.'" He scourges him, bloodies him, beats him, crowns him with thorns, dresses him in a robe, mocks him, brings him before the crowd and says, I'm bringing him out to you so that you may know that I find no guilt in him. "So Jesus came out, wearing the crown of thorns and the purple robe. Pilate said to them, 'Behold the man!'" Verses 6 to 8: "When the chief priests and the officers saw him, they cried out, 'Crucify him, crucify him!'

"Pilate said to them, 'Take him yourselves and crucify him, for I find no guilt in him.' The Jews answered him, 'We have a law, and according to that law he ought to die because he has made himself the Son of God.' When Pilate heard this statement, he was even more afraid." And he went back into his headquarters to talk to Jesus. I want to keep holding this out to you.

I don't want to preach another Pilate sermon, truthfully. But I want you to see that Pilate isn't what you see in the movies. He isn't conflicted in the way media has trained our instincts to see him. Philo of Alexandria, a Jewish philosopher writing a few years after these events, wrote about Pilate.

He said Pilate was known for his "briberies, insults, robberies, outrages, and wanton injuries, executions without trial constantly repeated, ceaseless and supremely grievous cruelty." I told you last week about his raiding the sacred treasury to pay for an aqueduct. When a crowd gathered to protest, he sent Roman soldiers in civilian clothes out among the protesters, and at his signal they drew clubs from under their cloaks and beat many of them to death. We know enough about Pilate, and the story itself bears it out. How is he supposedly torn by a moral dilemma while he has an innocent man flogged and mocked, and then, at the cross, puts up a sign that reads, King of the Jews?

He seems to enjoy taunting the Jewish people with it: look at your king. He just doesn't want to be held responsible for Jesus' death. And if you still don't buy it, we know from the other Gospels that his wife had a dream that disturbed her, and she sent him word: "Have nothing to do with that righteous man." Given the history I gave you last week, how shaky his position was, that would have rung loud in his ears. And here they say, we have a law, and according to that law he ought to die because he has made himself the Son of God.

So when Pilate heard this, he was even more afraid. Verse 9: "He entered his headquarters again and said to Jesus, 'Where are you from?'" This is a bit of conjecture on my part, but I believe he's asking, if I go through with this to appease this crowd, is there another crowd that will hold me to account for it? Where are you from? Who is calling you king?

These people don't think you're their king. They want you dead. Who doesn't want you dead? And are they going to come after me? He's just trying to save his own skin. It won't work.

A few years after this he'll be removed from office anyway, for needlessly and violently killing some Samaritans. So he brings Jesus into the headquarters, and he just wants to know, is it safe to kill you? Where are you from? "But Jesus gave him no answer." Verses 10 and 11: "So Pilate said to him, 'You will not speak to me?

"Do you not know that I have authority to release you and authority to crucify you?' Jesus answered him, 'You would have no authority over me at all unless it had been given you from above. Therefore he who delivered me over to you has the greater sin.'" Just a couple of words on this. It's true of Pilate.

It's true of every authority. No one has any authority that hasn't been lent to them by the Father. God is having his way even through the wicked rulers of the earth. It's the testimony of Scripture from beginning to end: "The king's heart is a stream of water in the hand of the LORD; he turns it wherever he will."

Our God reigns supreme over all the nations of the earth, all the kings of the earth, all the authorities he has established, and he's even using what they mean for evil to carry out his good purposes in creation. That's really good news. God in human flesh, Jesus Christ, is questioned before Pilate. Pilate says to his face, don't you know my authority here? And Jesus says, you don't have any authority, except what God has given you.

My Father. And I trust him. Do what you're going to do. The greater sin belongs to those who delivered me over to you. And that's simply true.

Because those who delivered him over were the leaders of his own people. They said, we have a law. We have a law. Jesus is saying, I gave you that law. It testified about me. It pointed you to your need for me. And I'm standing before you, and you're using my law to justify killing your Savior.

They certainly had the greater sin. Verse 12: "From then on Pilate sought to release him, but the Jews cried out, 'If you release this man, you are not Caesar's friend. Everyone who makes himself a king opposes Caesar.'" Verses 13 to 16: "So when Pilate heard these words, he brought Jesus out and sat down on the judgment seat at a place called The Stone Pavement, and in Aramaic Gabbatha.

"So he delivered him over to them to be crucified." Getting them to say those words was literally Pilate's job. His whole job was to bring the people of his region into subjection to the Roman Empire, which is why he was constantly at odds with them. These are the words that led him to go through with it.

The Jewish leaders knew exactly how to play him. It's all political posturing, back and forth. No one cares about the King of heaven on trial here. Everyone is just trying to get their way. And when Pilate heard, "We have no king but Caesar," he delivered him over to be crucified. We'll come to the crucifixion over the next two weeks.

So far in this account we see a Jesus who hands himself over to wicked authorities, over whom his Father is the higher authority. When you see Jesus standing in that place and enduring that suffering, walking willingly through that persecution, that calamity, that judgment, that wickedness, that harm, he isn't only identifying with suffering, although he is, and that will be a major closing point. He's showing you what he came to accomplish.

He's standing in for you, to take your penalty. I remember when I first talked about salvation with my youngest. He's seven now; he was probably four then.

From the penalty for my sin. From the penalty for my sin. The cross accomplished two things, and Christ's return accomplishes a third: three things, which should be in your notes if you take notes.
