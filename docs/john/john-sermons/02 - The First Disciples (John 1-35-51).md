# The First Disciples (John 1:35-51)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 1:35-51

---

We're continuing through the Gospel of John, in chapter one, from verse 35 down through verse 51. While you find your place, let me tell you a story.

Years ago, a close friend and I were serving side by side in our church. We were young dads, new husbands and working fathers, which means we were overwhelmed and busy. One day at a park, with our wives beside us and our kids eating mulch by the swing set, we started dreaming out loud about planting a church. We had both grown up around church, but there we had found something new to us: a church that worked through the whole counsel of God, found the gospel at the center of it, and taught us to fix our eyes on the riches and majesty of Christ and apply them to the everyday stuff of life. We thought, everyone needs this. Everywhere needs this.

We dreamed about all kinds of places. Then one day my friend sat in my living room while our kids played and told me the Lord wanted him to plant a church in a town I had never heard of and never been to. My instinct, by the Spirit, was, "When do we leave?" Over time, as we fixed our eyes more and more on the person and work of Jesus Christ, our lives had come to the place where we simply wanted to say yes when we saw the Lord moving. So we went. And it was costly. We gave up careers we could not step back into, friendships, and the church we loved. Almost no one in my family who doesn't know Christ understood what we were doing or why.

There is something about following Jesus that I want us to see in this passage, as he calls his first disciples. We are not here only to see Jesus, learn about Jesus, talk about Jesus and enjoy Jesus for ourselves. We are here to follow him. When he moves and says, "Follow me," we follow. Let me read the passage, and then we'll pray.

"The next day again John was standing with two of his disciples, and he looked at Jesus as he walked by and said, 'Behold, the Lamb of God!' The two disciples heard him say this, and they followed Jesus. Jesus turned and saw them following and said to them, 'What are you seeking?' And they said to him, 'Rabbi' (which means Teacher), 'where are you staying?' He said to them, 'Come and you will see.' So they came and saw where he was staying, and they stayed with him that day, for it was about the tenth hour. One of the two who heard John speak and followed Jesus was Andrew, Simon Peter's brother. He first found his own brother Simon and said to him, 'We have found the Messiah' (which means Christ). He brought him to Jesus. Jesus looked at him and said, 'You are Simon the son of John. You shall be called Cephas' (which means Peter). The next day Jesus decided to go to Galilee. He found Philip and said to him, 'Follow me.' Now Philip was from Bethsaida, the city of Andrew and Peter. Philip found Nathanael and said to him, 'We have found him of whom Moses in the Law and also the prophets wrote, Jesus of Nazareth, the son of Joseph.' Nathanael said to him, 'Can anything good come out of Nazareth?' Philip said to him, 'Come and see.' Jesus saw Nathanael coming toward him and said of him, 'Behold, an Israelite indeed, in whom there is no deceit!' Nathanael said to him, 'How do you know me?' Jesus answered him, 'Before Philip called you, when you were under the fig tree, I saw you.' Nathanael answered him, 'Rabbi, you are the Son of God! You are the King of Israel!' Jesus answered him, 'Because I said to you, "I saw you under the fig tree," do you believe? You will see greater things than these.' And he said to him, 'Truly, truly, I say to you, you will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man.'"

Father, as we look at the calling of the first disciples, the very beginning of your public ministry, make us so captivated by who you are, by what you're about and by your mission in this world, that everything else we have going is measured against you and made small. Like the first disciples, when you invite us to come and see, may we receive the invitation and gladly leave whatever else we have going to follow you in whatever you have going. Lord, we say that we count everything as loss because of the surpassing worth of knowing you. Wherever that is not yet true of us, make it true by your Spirit. We ask in Jesus' name. Amen.

Let's go verse by verse. Verse 35: "The next day again John was standing with two of his disciples, and he looked at Jesus as he walked by and said, 'Behold, the Lamb of God!'" This is not the first time John has said this. The day before, in verse 29, he saw Jesus coming toward him and said, "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" Picture John the Baptist doing his work. He has a following and disciples around him, and he is baptizing people in the Jordan River, calling them to confess their sins and repent as he prepares the way for the one greater than he who is coming. His whole ministry, which went on for years before Jesus appeared, was a ministry of preparation: I am gathering you so that I can prepare you for the one who comes after me, the one who is greater.

Then yesterday Jesus comes, and John declares, "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" And the story does not go on to say that many followed him. No one did. He made that declaration, and nobody left John to follow Jesus.

But here, the next day, John does it again. "Behold, the Lamb of God!" And this time two disciples hear him and follow Jesus. When I read this, I thought of the episode of The Office where Jim talks Dwight into loudly announcing each guest as they arrive at a party, and then Jim keeps walking out and back in so Dwight has to announce him again. Yes, I just compared John the Baptist to Dwight. But hear me, because there's something beautiful here. We tend to think John's whole ministry was building toward one loud declaration: Behold, here he is, and now I'm done. That wasn't the case. The moment his whole ministry pointed to came and went, and not one person followed Jesus. So the next day, faithfully, John declares it again: This is the one. Behold, the Lamb of God. And on that day, two follow. Nor is John finished once two have followed. He keeps going until he is imprisoned and beheaded.

This passage isn't about us, but we ought to learn from it. We are not in the business of getting people to acknowledge the Lamb of God once. Each of us is called to a ministry like John's, pointing to Jesus over and over: Behold the Lamb of God. Behold the Lamb of God. Behold the Lamb of God.

Notice too that these are John's own disciples. John has built a following through hard ministry, mocked and ridiculed as he prepared the way. Two of his own disciples hear him say, "Behold, the Lamb of God," and they leave him to follow Jesus.

I'll confess that part of me wants to gather you all in a room and say, behold Jesus, see Jesus, enjoy Jesus, rest in the gospel, and then, when you catch sight of him and he starts to move and you want to follow, grab you by the ankles and say, don't go with him. Stay here. When people I love are called away, I don't want them to go where Jesus isn't calling me. But you don't see John grabbing his two disciples by the ankles: Hold on, we're doing the baptism thing in the river. You're my disciples. Our job is to stay here and tell people to behold the Lamb of God. No. He gladly hands his disciples over to walk directly with Jesus. That was always the point, and we'll see a lot more of it from John in chapter three.

When it says they "followed Jesus," take the word in its plainest sense. He was walking, and they tagged along behind him. The other Gospels tell the fuller story of the moment Jesus calls them to be his disciples. This isn't that moment. Here John says, that's the one, and they follow on foot, trying to figure him out. Jesus will call them in very personal, specific ways later.

Verse 38: "Jesus turned and saw them following and said to them, 'What are you seeking?'" Picture it with me, because it's funny. They are following him without saying a word, just tagging along. So he turns around and asks, "What are you seeking?" And they say, "Rabbi, where are you staying?" If you were a disciple of John the Baptist, sitting under his teaching, you were seeking something. You don't end up in the wilderness with John by chance. So when John says, this is the one I told you about, the people who know what he means are the ones who were there when he said it. A whole ministry comes to its point, John can point and say, that's the one, and these men know what he means and follow.

They were seeking something, and that will become clear in a minute. But they didn't tell him what. They said, Teacher, where are you staying? You can almost hear them thinking: not here, not now, this is awkward, we don't know you, but John said something. Where are you staying? Could we come at some appointed time and talk privately about what we're looking for? And Jesus says to them, "Come and you will see." They weren't expecting an invitation right then and there. They were trying to find a place to follow up on who this man is. And Jesus says, right now. Come, and you will see.

"So they came and saw where he was staying, and they stayed with him that day, for it was about the tenth hour," about four in the afternoon. They arrive late in the day, he invites them to stay, and they do. They receive his invitation to come, so they come, and they stay with him.

Verses 40 and 41: "One of the two who heard John speak and followed Jesus was Andrew, Simon Peter's brother. He first found his own brother Simon and said to him, 'We have found the Messiah' (which means Christ). He brought him to Jesus." Let's break it down. Andrew is one of John's two disciples. They're following Jesus, they reach the place where he's staying, and Andrew says, I'll be right back, I have to get my brother. He goes, finds Simon, and brings him to Jesus.

A lot of people will tell you Andrew is the first missionary. He sees the Messiah and goes to get someone else. I'd say Andrew isn't the first missionary. You might answer, of course not, it's John the Baptist. No, not him either. Listen to what Andrew says: "We have found the Messiah." He doesn't know it yet, but they haven't found anyone. He has found them. This is the story of Jesus choosing his disciples, Jesus bringing dead people to life. It's a story about Jesus' own mission. He has broken into time and space, taken on flesh, walked among the peoples of the earth and called his disciples. The first missionary is Jesus. And the first response to the great missionary is to go and do likewise, to find others. So Andrew goes: I have to get my brother.

And he tells Simon what he didn't say to Jesus. When Jesus asked, "What are you seeking?" the right answer was, the Messiah. Instead they said, where are you staying? But to Simon, Andrew says, "We have found the Messiah," the Christ, the Anointed One, and brings him to Jesus.

Notice that in verse 40 John calls Andrew "Simon Peter's brother" before Simon has even entered the story, because by the time John wrote, everyone knew who Simon Peter was. Peter would be the far better known disciple. But Andrew will always be remembered as the one who went and got his brother. That's a model for us.

Then Jesus looks at Simon and says, "You are Simon the son of John. You shall be called Cephas," which means Peter, and Peter means rock. Imagine you walk into church for the first time, I shake your hand, you tell me your name, and I say, Yes, that's your name, but you shall be called Rocket. You'd wonder what kind of cult you had just walked into. Yet by meeting Simon and saying, you are Simon, you shall be called Rock, Jesus declares from the very start of his ministry: I am the one with the power to rename you. I am the one who can change your identity completely.

What's funny is that Rock is the least fitting name I've ever seen given to anyone. Peter will be, in every way, the opposite of a rock. And write this down: Jesus gives Peter his new name before Peter has done anything. They have just met. Peter has had no success in ministry and no failure either. He has simply been given a new name by Jesus: you shall be called the rock. And Peter will go on to be anything but. He will be the most emotional of the disciples. He will waver all over the place, boldly announce what he will do and then do the opposite, and fail hard. He is the disciple who by faith walks on water and then by doubt sinks and has to be saved. He is the Peter who denies Jesus three times in his hour of greatest need. At the Last Supper he says, "Though they all fall away because of you, I will never fall away." Jesus answers, before the rooster crows, you will deny me three times. And then, three times, Peter says, I don't know him.

Peter was not a rock, not in the way we would use the word. But in Matthew, when Jesus asks the disciples, "Who do you say that I am?" Peter answers, "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God." And Jesus blesses him, tells him that the Father revealed this to him, and says, "And I tell you, you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church, and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it." The name came before that moment, before anything happened, because it was about what Jesus intended to do with Peter's life.

So here's a side point I want us to hear this morning. When we think about our core identity, we should think in terms of the name Jesus has given us, not the names we want to give ourselves. Some of you are Myers-Briggs or Enneagram people. If I asked your personality type, you could tell me. A few years ago it seemed everyone wanted to know theirs. Hi, my name is Adam, but I shall be called ENTP. It became a way to describe the core of who I am: introvert or extrovert, intuitive or sensing, thinker or feeler. As if, once I give you my combination of letters, you'll understand me, and once you give me yours, we'll know whether we're compatible. We do it because we love to define ourselves, to name and explain who we are and what we're made of.

Even if that's not you, you still do it with what you're good at or bad at: I'm Adam the dad, Adam the husband, Adam the pastor, Adam the health care leader, Adam the guy who loves music or reading or writing. Or I define myself in the negative: Adam the failure, Adam the one who blew it. When you rest your identity on any of these lesser self-definitions, the moment you fail to live up to one of them, your identity is in crisis. If I'm Adam the musician and I lose my right hand in a car accident, my identity is in crisis.

That's why it matters that Jesus named Peter, and that he gives every one of us our first, primary and highest identity: Christian. I'm Adam the Christian, Adam the blood-bought, Adam the covered one. That is the unshakable name Christ gave me, and he carries it out in me, so I can't fail my way out of it. I can't ruin it, because he gave it to me first. He defined what it means. He prepared beforehand the good works I would walk in. And he determined to take all my sin, brokenness and failure and still work them into his good purposes for that identity. That is an entirely different way to understand who you are. It will be huge in Peter's life, and we'll get there. But for now, before Peter has done a thing, he has been given a new name that Christ means to see through.

Verses 43 to 45: "The next day Jesus decided to go to Galilee. He found Philip and said to him, 'Follow me.' Now Philip was from Bethsaida, the city of Andrew and Peter. Philip found Nathanael and said to him, 'We have found him of whom Moses in the Law and also the prophets wrote, Jesus of Nazareth, the son of Joseph.'" Right at the start of Jesus' ministry, a pattern emerges. Andrew follows Jesus and goes to get his brother. Jesus calls Philip, and Philip runs to get Nathanael. Andrew said, we have found the Messiah. Philip says, we have found the one Moses in the Law and the prophets wrote about.

The disciples' view of who this is keeps rising, and it is that view that drives them to go get a friend, to tell someone, to bring someone along, to invite someone in. And they did it after knowing him for about a day. Don't hear this as condemnation, but some of us have known the Lord for twenty years. If Jesus Christ is the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world, the King of Israel, the one Moses and the prophets wrote about, the Messiah, the Anointed One, and if we spend time with him and see for ourselves that this is who he is, then the natural impulse is the one the first disciples had: go and tell a friend.

That's the big takeaway. This is big news, and everyone needs to hear it, so go and tell a friend. Find them, whether in the next town over, at work, on your own block or across the world. And maybe the Lord will move you somewhere to do it. I certainly wouldn't have guessed, years ago, where he would take me. Go and tell them you have found the one, and that he has found you.

Nathanael answers, "Can anything good come out of Nazareth?" That's the response I usually get when I try to tell people about Jesus: nothing good can be found there. And Philip says, "Come and see." He doesn't launch into an apologetic or try to convince him. He knows Jesus. He knows where Jesus is staying and what it's like to be with him. He lets Jesus make his own defense. Come and see. Come taste and see for yourself.

So Nathanael comes, and Jesus sees him coming and says, "Behold, an Israelite indeed, in whom there is no deceit!" Nathanael asks, "How do you know me?" Jesus answers, "Before Philip called you, when you were under the fig tree, I saw you." And Nathanael answers, "Rabbi, you are the Son of God! You are the King of Israel!"

We don't get the details about the fig tree. It's fig country, so Nathanael would have sat under a fig tree at some point, and you might think Jesus is pulling a card trick, like saying to me, Adam, you are truly an American, and I saw you in a car. No. We need to tie the two sentences together. Jesus says of Nathanael, here is an Israelite in whom there is no deceit. That lands on Nathanael in a particular way, and he feels seen: how do you know me? Jesus answers, under the fig tree, I saw you. Something happened under a fig tree that had to do with deceit. At some point under a fig tree Nathanael had an opportunity to deceive, and he didn't. Jesus knows it and brings it up. It must have been a big moment in Nathanael's life, because he knows exactly what Jesus means and feels completely seen. This is no card trick. Jesus has just shown special knowledge of a time and place where he was not physically present, and that's all it takes for Nathanael to declare, you are the Son of God, you are the King of Israel.

Jesus answers, "Because I said to you, 'I saw you under the fig tree,' do you believe? You will see greater things than these." And he says, "Truly, truly, I say to you, you will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man." I wish I had more time here, but write this down and go read Genesis 28 if it isn't familiar. Jesus is pointing back to Jacob's ladder. In Genesis 28, heaven opens to Jacob in a dream, and he sees a ladder reaching to heaven with the angels of God ascending and descending on it. The place becomes holy. It's called Bethel, and it becomes a place where God's people would go to know they were in his presence.

And we should remember Jacob's character. He was a great deceiver, an Israelite in whom there was much deceit. He took his brother Esau's birthright and tricked his father out of Esau's blessing, and he was tricked in turn by Laban when he pursued Rachel. To Jacob the deceiver it was given to see angels ascending and descending, and the place was named Bethel.

Now Jesus calls Nathanael a true Israelite in whom there is no deceit, which raises him above Jacob, one of the patriarchs, and tells him, you will see heaven opened and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man. Not on Bethel. In the Old Testament, God dwelt with his people at designated places and times, approached in designated ways: with a mediator, with everything done rightly, with the tent set up as he commanded, you could commune with God. Bethel was one of those places where people went to feel close to heaven. Jesus is saying the angels will ascend and descend on me. My very presence here is God dwelling among his people. Unlike Jacob, you won't need to go to Bethel. You are standing in the presence of heaven right here and right now. That is quite a way to open a public ministry.

Look at all the names Jesus is given in this passage. It struck me as I read it. John the Baptist calls him the Lamb of God. The disciples call him Rabbi, which means Teacher; Messiah, which means Christ, the Anointed One; the one Moses and the prophets wrote about; Jesus of Nazareth, the son of Joseph; the Son of God; and the King of Israel. Jesus calls himself the Son of Man. You don't have to take notes. Read the passage and you'll find them all, in verses 35 to 51, on his first couple of days with the disciples.

Three of them stood out to me as I prepared: son of Joseph from Nazareth, Son of Man and Son of God. How could those three possibly go together? "Of Nazareth" was a name for mockery, and we will see it again, along with "King of Israel," when he hangs on a cross. Pilate will write an inscription and put it on the cross: "Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews." The names given to Jesus at the start of his ministry will show up again on a Roman cross. In Acts, when they're trying to stamp out the Christian movement, Paul is accused of being "a ringleader of the sect of the Nazarenes." Yet Jesus, son of Joseph from Nazareth, Son of Man and Son of God, doesn't shy away from any of these titles. He owns them all.

As I close, I want us to wrestle with Son of Man and Son of God. We spent several weeks in John's prologue fixing our eyes on the deity of Christ: he was there in the beginning with God, and he is himself God. That gives us a high view of him. When he calls himself the Son of Man, he shows us something different and beautiful about himself. He entered human flesh, born of a virgin, and lived a human life. Scripture calls him the firstborn of all creation, and firstborn is an inheritance term: the firstborn is the one to whom the family's inheritance is owed. As the Son of Man and the firstborn of all creation, everything in this world belongs to him by right. He created everything. His creation departed from him. He entered the story to become like us, and he bought back what was already his, so that it would become his inheritance. What he already had a rightful claim to, he purchased again with his own blood. It's as if Jesus says, you are all mine, twice over. I made you, you left me, and I bought you back.

Each of these names would take a sermon of its own, so I won't preach them all now. But I want you to see that Jesus is introducing himself to the disciples, and to us as his disciples, in a major way at the start of his ministry. Over the next chapters he will give clarity to each of these names as he walks this out with his disciples. And notice: they didn't need clarity on almost any of it before their response was right. With whatever grain of faith they had, they said, all I know is that I want to come and see, and I want to bring others so they can see too.

So I invite us, like the first disciples, to count Jesus worthy: to leave everything else behind, to count everything as loss, to come and see for ourselves, to stay with him and follow him wherever he goes, and to bring others along the way so that they might see this Jesus. The Lamb of God, the Messiah, the Christ, the one Moses and the prophets wrote about, the Son of Man, the Son of God, the son of Joseph, the King of Israel. He alone is worthy of our obedience, our lives and everything we're holding back from him. Whatever we want to grab by the ankles and say, look, but don't go, let's open our hands, encourage the people around us, move our feet and follow him. Let's pray to that end.
