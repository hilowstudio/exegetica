# Lost Brothers (John 7:1-13)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 7:1-13

---

Some years ago a man in our church organized a men's camping trip. He had served in the military, jumping out of airplanes and teaching men how to survive behind enemy lines until help came, so he was a great man to lead a camping trip. I brought my two oldest kids. My middle son was about five and still afraid of the dark, and I wasn't sure he was ready to sleep in a tent in a strange place.

It was incredible. Our friend sun-dried a snake on a rock for eating. He taught the kids that you could eat ants. He taught my son to strike a fire with flint and steel and showed him several other ways to start one. They blazed trails and splashed around in a brook. We sang worship songs around a bonfire until the middle of the night, and then it was time for bed.

My son slept with me in our little two-person tent, which had a mesh opening at the top of the dome, so that when you lay down you could see straight up. No light pollution, a beautiful clear sky, every star and the moon. We lay there with his head tucked under my arm and marveled at the night sky together. I talked to him about how the stars testify to the majesty of our God. We talked about father Abraham, who would have looked up at these same stars when the Lord promised him that his offspring would outnumber them, and how he didn't know, when God said it, that God was talking about us. We were counted among the stars. It was an incredible night, and my son fell asleep so peacefully. The next afternoon, as we were leaving, I asked him, "Of all the things we did, what was your favorite part of your first camping trip?" Without a moment's hesitation he said, "Sleeping under the stars."

That story came to my heart as I prepared this message, because this passage takes place during the Feast of Booths. The Feast of Booths was an annual feast of remembrance that God gave his people. When the Hebrews were slaves in Egypt, the Lord set them free and led them by a pillar of cloud and fire for forty years through the wilderness, providing for them in miraculous ways. While they traveled, trusting God for their provision, they lived in booths, or tabernacles: little tent-like huts, temporary shelters, because they were on the move. For forty years the Lord was preparing a promised land for them, and they had to trust him for everything. So the Lord commanded them, once they entered the land, to keep this feast of remembrance, so that as they entered a season of plenty they would not forget the God who had always provided for them: the God of their exodus, the God who would keep providing for them, the God who was giving them the land. In their prosperity they were not to forget where they came from or the God who had provided for them.

So for one week every year, with a day of solemn rest at each end, they lived in booths to remember those forty years in the wilderness, and to see their God as the God who provides. I believe that when my son and I lay in our little tent looking up at the stars, we got the tiniest glimpse of what they felt at the Feast of Booths. When you put yourself in a small dwelling, it somehow measures the majesty of your God. When you lower your estate and set aside the things you normally count on to provide for you, your eyes are lifted upward and you see the God of the heavens. That's what was meant to happen at the Feast of Booths. It was a festival of remembrance, so that when they tabernacled, they would see the God who provides for them.

We need to understand this feast to understand this morning's passage, and I'll show you why. Verses 1 to 5: "After this Jesus went about in Galilee. He would not go about in Judea, because the Jews were seeking to kill him. Now the Jews' Feast of Booths was at hand. So his brothers said to him, 'Leave here and go to Judea, that your disciples also may see the works you are doing. For no one works in secret if he seeks to be known openly. If you do these things, show yourself to the world.' For not even his brothers believed in him."

We've just finished three sermons on the miracle of the bread and the fish. After this, Jesus is going about in Galilee. We already read that when Jesus went back to Galilee, he testified that a prophet has no honor in his own hometown. He went home knowing he would not be honored there.

And he won't enter Judea. Why? Because the Jews were seeking to kill him, and the Feast of Booths was at hand. Here's why I set this up for you. In John 1, our author tells us that the Word, Jesus, "became flesh and dwelt among us." The word translated "dwelt" is eskēnōsen. It comes from the Greek word for a tent, and it means he pitched his tent among us. He tabernacled among us. And the Feast of Booths is also called the Feast of Tabernacles; the Greek name John uses for it means the pitching of tents. So during the Feast of Tabernacles, the Word was tabernacling among his people. I want you to see these two collide.

The Jews were keeping a celebration God had given them as a command: you shall dwell in booths, so that you will see me as your great provider and remember me as your great deliverer. That's why they kept the feast. And the very God who instituted that feast is tabernacling among them, dwelling among them. And what are they doing about it? They're seeking to kill him. They're seeking to kill him.

So as we consider the setting, I think we need to hear it as a caution and a lament. I don't know how to say this any bigger: the whole earth was a tent to him. All that we claim as ours, everything we claim lordship over, was for him like a tent. The God of the heavens came down into a tent to dwell among his creation. And as he does that, in his wonderful pursuit of mankind, taking on the lesser likeness of men and making himself killable, what do we do? We jump at the opportunity. As Jesus Christ, God in human flesh, makes himself killable in order to dwell among us, we jump at the chance. The Jews jumped at it, seeking to kill him as the Feast of Booths drew near.

So there's a tension here I want to invite us into, with two poles. We celebrate that our God came in human flesh to tabernacle and dwell among us, and we lament that our response is exactly what he said it would be: to kill him.

That's the tension Jesus is in. You see a Jesus who is keeping out of sight, going about in Galilee, not going into Judea because the Jews were seeking to kill him. And the Feast of Booths is at hand, so the Jews would all be gathered in one place.

Then, in verse 3, his brothers speak to him: "Leave here and go to Judea, that your disciples also may see the works you are doing. For no one works in secret if he seeks to be known openly. If you do these things, show yourself to the world." Listen to what they're saying. The author has just told us Jesus isn't going into Judea because the Jews are seeking to kill him. And his brothers taunt him: why don't you go to Judea and show off there? They're trying to lure him in front of the very people who want him dead. It makes me think of Joseph, betrayed into the pit and sold into slavery by his own brothers. Jesus is looking at brothers he loves. He grew up with them for thirty years before his public ministry, and they don't believe in him.

As a side note, this is evidence for what we've been preaching these last weeks. If Jesus' own brothers, who grew up in his home and knew him all their lives, reject him and don't believe in him, surely he was telling the truth when he said that no one can come to him unless the Father draws him, unless it is granted him by the Father. But all that the Father gives him will come to him.

Here, though, his brothers squarely reject him. More than reject him: they tempt and taunt him. "If you do these things, show yourself to the world." They're accusing him of doing his miracles in secret because in some way they aren't legitimate. He's lying. He's not who he says he is. He's not really doing these things by the power of God, because if he were, he'd do them in public. Why do it out by the Sea of Tiberias, on the side of a mountain? Why do it in Samaria?

Let me tell you why. Because he cared about the outsider. He was carrying the work of God beyond the city center to outsiders, like the Samaritans, so that people like you and me might be reached. That's the tension we're invited into: Jesus Christ, in doing the very things that got him rejected by his inner circle, was extending his grace to the outer circle. He was coming after you and me. And it brought him jeering, mockery, taunting and persecution from his own brothers.

So they say to him, let them all see the works you're doing. No one works in secret if he wants to be known openly. If you do these things, show yourself to the world. "For not even his brothers believed in him."

This is resonating with me in this season of my life, and it's likely resonating with you in some way in yours, because this is a relatable moment. If Jesus' own brothers did not believe in him, if they mocked him because they had no faith in him, if they taunted him and tried to lure him into danger, then surely when you look at your friends, your siblings, your parents, your grandparents, your cousins and uncles, your neighbors and coworkers, and they reject you, mock you or ridicule you on account of your faith in Jesus, you're in good company. You're in good company.

You are enduring what Christ has already endured before you. Jesus suffered mockery and ridicule at the hands of his own brothers. So when you look at your relationships and find tension, rejection, mockery and ridicule from the people you love most because they don't believe, know that Jesus has gone before you. I want to start there, because there is a wonderful resolution to this encounter with his brothers.

There is. But not every encounter goes the way this one finally does. In Mark 3, Jesus' family goes out to seize him, "for they were saying, 'He is out of his mind.'" And in Matthew 10, warning his disciples that persecution will come for them, Jesus says this, starting in verse 34: "Do not think that I have come to bring peace to the earth. I have not come to bring peace, but a sword. For I have come to set a man against his father, and a daughter against her mother, and a daughter-in-law against her mother-in-law. And a person's enemies will be those of his own household. Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me, and whoever loves son or daughter more than me is not worthy of me. And whoever does not take his cross and follow me is not worthy of me. Whoever finds his life will lose it, and whoever loses his life for my sake will find it."

These are hard words, especially in an American context. For most of our nation's history we've called ourselves a Christian nation. Whether that was ever true isn't worth debating from the pulpit, but for most of our history the great majority of Americans would tick the box marked "Christian" on a survey. When some version of Christianity is broadly accepted by the culture, it feels strange and uncomfortable when the culture around us, or our own families, turn against us over our faith in Jesus. That's less normal where we were raised than in other parts of the world. Today in places like India, Nepal, Iraq or Iran, putting your faith in Jesus can cost you your life, and it will often cost you your family: your mother and father, your brother and sister.

In many parts of the world today, to choose Christ is to surrender your closest relationships to him. Maybe that hasn't been your experience. But it is absolutely the experience of many of your brothers and sisters in the faith around the world. And I'd suggest that if nobody in your life feels any tension with you over your allegiance to Christ, then either celebrate that everyone around you is a Christian, or lament that somehow you've made friends with the world rather than with your Savior. We know this from verses 6 to 9.

"Jesus said to them, 'My time has not yet come, but your time is always here. The world cannot hate you, but it hates me because I testify about it that its works are evil. You go up to the feast. I am not going up to this feast, for my time has not yet fully come.' After saying this, he remained in Galilee."

Jesus tells his brothers that the world cannot hate them. Why? Because they're of the world. They belong to it. As he tells his disciples later, "If you were of the world, the world would love you as its own." The world loves them because they reject him, and it hates him because he testifies that its works are evil. To give your allegiance to Christ, to come into his family, to be adopted by him and worship him as your King, is to be at odds with the world, because he came to overcome the world. And take heart: he has.

If you have no enemy in the world, and I don't mean you should ever go looking for one, hear what I'm saying: you will find one. In honoring Jesus and surrendering to him as Lord, you will put yourself at odds with the world. Jesus promised it. He said persecution would come. He said mother and father, son and daughter would be divided over him. And that is the worst.

Surely you have people in your life you've been praying for, people you long to see know Christ, and without their own saving faith in Jesus there is enmity between you, because your greatest treasure is no treasure to them at all. There's a wedge you can't overcome in your own strength. Maybe there are places where you want to put your faith away so you can get along. I know. It's the worst. And first, I just want to say to you that he's good, that he's sovereign, and that you were no different from them when he gave you saving faith.

Here is my encouragement this morning. He tabernacled among us, and the word can carry the sense of a sojourn: for a time, he dwelt among us. And even as those who rejected him mocked him, paid him no mind and tempted him toward his death, he waited. Part of us wants to read this as if he's biding his time: when his time comes, he's going to wreck some people. He's going to climb down off that cross and prove to everyone who persecuted him who he really is. But that's not what he means when he talks about his time coming. He's not staying out of Judea because he's afraid to die. He's staying out of Judea because it isn't yet his hour to die. He chose ahead of time the hour in which he would give up his life, and he won't let his brothers taunt him into moving that timeline up by one minute, one day, one meal. The day he would allow himself to be betrayed was the day of his betrayal, and no other day would do, because he was fulfilling the promises of God.

So when he says, "My time has not yet come," he isn't waiting for his moment to prove himself. He's talking about the hour of his crucifixion, the hour of his passion, the hour of his great sacrifice. He waited patiently for that hour, for our sake. He marched toward it for these men, for his brothers, for those who rejected him, for you and me.

When we sing Stuart Townend's "How Deep the Father's Love for Us," we confess that we stood among the scoffers at the cross, and that our sin kept him there until his work was accomplished. We were the ones who rejected him. We were the scoffers. We were no different from those who jeer and scoff at you today for your faith in Jesus. And surely, if you were not beyond his reach, then neither are they. Neither are they.

It's nothing short of a miracle that Jesus' brothers, who never acknowledged him as Lord in any biblical account before the cross, came to faith after it. When his hour came, when he breathed his last and then took up his life again, his brothers came to saving faith in him. These men, who taunted him toward the people who wanted him dead, would have their eyes opened to new life by his death. His brother James writes, "My brothers, show no partiality as you hold the faith in our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory." And after Jesus ascended, Acts 1:14 says, "All these with one accord were devoting themselves to prayer, together with the women and Mary the mother of Jesus, and his brothers." After his hour had come, after he had died for his accusers and for those who rejected him, he purchased them and brought them into new life. These brothers turned to him.

I know some of you have loved ones you longed to see come to Jesus who died apart from him. In that grief I just want to point you to him: he said it would be so. He went to the cross anyway, knowing this world would be full of rejection and mockery. It breaks his heart more than ours. But his love is so great that he still ransoms a remnant. And you don't know whom he will yet save.

Here's what I know about my own life: there's no reason for me to be up here. If you had told me at any point that Jesus was going to rescue me into new life with him, I would have laughed at you. For him then to give me the privilege of preaching the gospel to others is a joke. It's a good joke. It's the Lord showing he can speak through a donkey.

And that's you too. You rejected him. You were bent against him. You loved the world and couldn't hate sin, because sin was your nature. And he saved you. So have you stopped praying for that one? Have you decided they're too far gone? Have you started avoiding someone because the mocking and jeering aren't worth it? When they spit on your God, when they spit at you for your faith, they're doing what comes naturally. They're doing what you were doing when Christ saved you. But God, rich in mercy and steadfast love, has endured the world's rejection with much patience so that many would be saved. So keep going for them.

Let's see how this wraps up. Jesus doesn't go up to the feast right away. It can look as if he's lying, because the English reads a little oddly. His brothers say, go up to the feast. He says, I am not going up to this feast. He's simply telling them no. They're saying, go, because we said so; go with us. He says no and sends them without him. He's telling them, you don't set my timeline; my Father sets my timeline. Then, at its own appointed hour, perfectly fulfilling the will of God, he goes. Verses 10 to 13: "But after his brothers had gone up to the feast, then he also went up, not publicly but in private. The Jews were looking for him at the feast, and saying, 'Where is he?' And there was much muttering about him among the people. While some said, 'He is a good man,' others said, 'No, he is leading the people astray.' Yet for fear of the Jews no one spoke openly of him."

So there are two groups among the people at the feast. Some call him a good man. Others say no, he's leading the people astray; they call him a deceiver. Both are badly wrong. He is not merely a good man, and he is no deceiver. And the Jews are looking for him: where is he?

I hate stopping in the middle of a passage, because he's about to break into the feast. But here he is among them, and they don't know he's there, and they're talking among themselves. They represent the people in your life we've been talking about. They're talking about Jesus. Either he's a liar, a nobody, a deceiver, a dead man, or he was a good man, a good teacher, certainly not hurting anybody, tame. People who haven't recognized Jesus as Lord have either reduced him to a good man or rejected him as a deceiver. Both are wrong, and neither is any closer to seeing him as Lord. Calling him a good man brings no one closer to bowing to him as King of their life and Lord of the universe, and calling him a deceiver doesn't either. Both are plainly wrong.

So I want you to see this. Think about the people in your community who tick that box marked "Christian." Most of our culture doesn't find Jesus offensive, but it doesn't worship him as Lord either. In fact, it doesn't find him offensive because it doesn't worship him as Lord. If he's not Lord, I can keep the good and throw out the bad. I can craft him into whatever I like. So hear this: when you see Jesus rightly, you see that he can save anyone, and that until he saves us, we are entirely bent against him. You're going to preach the gospel both to those who think Jesus is a good guy and to those who revile him, because both stand equally opposed to Jesus, equally apart from him. Both need his saving affection to come on them so that they can see him as Lord.

And as they debated among themselves, some saying he's a good man and some saying he's leading the people astray, none of them spoke openly of him for fear of the Jews.

This is my final point. It's small, but it matters. Fear of man drives our wrestling, our doubts and even our faith into the closet. I'm speaking to two groups. First, Christians: if you won't wrestle out loud with your thoughts about Jesus and your faith in him, if you won't make your questions and struggles known among God's people because you're afraid someone will think you're not a good Christian, hear this. There are no good Christians. There are only perfect Christians, and your perfection is a gift given to you by the blood of Jesus Christ. So among God's people, we wrestle out loud with our doubts about Jesus. Second, if you're on the outside, if you've refused to submit yourself fully to Jesus as Lord for fear of man, I rebuke you too, and I say: he is worthy, and he commands the whole thing. The whole thing.

We profess our faith in Christ, and we don't hide it under a basket. And we have this blessed assurance: when we enter suffering, persecution, jeering and mockery, we are entering what Christ went through before us. Insofar as we take up our crosses, endure suffering as he did and die to ourselves as he did, we will also join him in new life and resurrection by the power of the Holy Spirit. That is the engine of the church, and it's the difference between these brothers at the feast and you.

So, three takeaways. First, you are not alone when you endure rejection. Second, he died for those who rejected him, and you were one of them. If he can save you, whom can't he save? So take heart, and keep praying. Third, put aside your fear of man and boldly proclaim the gospel, because your Jesus is that big. He's that big. Will you pray with me that the Spirit would do these three things in us?
