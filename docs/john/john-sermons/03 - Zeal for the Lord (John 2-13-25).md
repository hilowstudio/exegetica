# Zeal for the Lord (John 2:13-25)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 2:13-25

---

As we open this passage, the scenery has changed. At the wedding at Cana we saw Jesus as the lord of the feast, the lord of the wine. It was a beautiful picture. Now we're at the Passover of the Jews, which is at hand.

There are four Gospels in the Bible, Matthew, Mark, Luke and John, and you won't find this scene right after the wedding at Cana in the others. In fact, you won't find the wedding at Cana in the others at all. So this is unique to John, and some would say John wrote with a different approach than the other Gospel writers. Rather than following a strict chronology, he arranged the stories in an order that made sense theologically, to show different attributes of Jesus and tell the story of who he is. That's obvious from the first chapter. John opens with "In the beginning," going back to creation to exalt the nature and character of God and to show from the start that Jesus himself is God. From there he jumps to John the Baptist, which seems backwards: first you're at creation, then at the Jordan River with John, then at a wedding, and now at the Passover. John doesn't seem very concerned with chronology. He is very concerned with highlighting certain attributes of Jesus in a certain way. And this morning he takes us to the Passover.

We know the story of the Passover from Exodus 12. It's the story of God delivering his chosen people from captivity in Egypt. They had been enslaved and oppressed, and they cried out to the Lord. The Lord heard them and appointed Moses and Aaron to go before Pharaoh and tell him, "Let my people go," so that the captives would be freed from their hard oppression and led into a promised land. But Pharaoh's heart was hardened, and he kept saying no. He considered himself a god and set himself against God: I will not let them go. So God sent a series of plagues on Egypt, to compel Pharaoh to obey and to put his own power and majesty on display, both to his people and to Egypt.

The last plague was the Passover. God told his people to take a spotless lamb into the home, keep it several days, then slaughter it, eat it, and put its blood on the doorposts and lintel of the house, because he was sending the destroyer through the land of Egypt. Every household found under the blood of the lamb was spared as the destroyer passed through. Every household not covered by the blood of the spotless lamb was not.

In the grief of that great plague, Pharaoh let the people go. And God made it the beginning of a new calendar for his people: every year from now on you shall keep this feast of the Passover. Once again they would eat a lamb with bitter herbs and unleavened bread and remember the time the Lord spared them from the destroyer and led them out of captivity toward the promised land. So every year, faithfully, the day came around and the Passover was kept.

It was one of the feasts when the people made pilgrimage to the holy city of Jerusalem to bring their sacrifices and offerings to the temple and to share the feast there. That's the time of year it is. It's worth setting our minds on Exodus 12 this morning to remember why it mattered. When John says "the Passover of the Jews was at hand," he means something very specific: the high point of the Jewish calendar, when everyone stopped to look at the faithful God who delivered them by the blood of a lamb.

We have to jump ahead to see the irony. Jesus himself is about to offer himself as the perfect, spotless lamb for the salvation of his people. John is writing in a deliberate order, and he has already told us that John the Baptist pointed to Jesus and said, "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" That title has already entered the story. So the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world, is going to the Passover.

"The Passover of the Jews was at hand, and Jesus went up to Jerusalem. In the temple he found those who were selling oxen and sheep and pigeons, and the money-changers sitting there."

The temple was designed in a particular way, with outer courts and inner courts, a series of concentric rings, each one a picture of God opening up access to himself to the peoples of the earth. The outer ring was for the Gentiles, people who were not Jewish by birth but wanted to worship the one true God. All through the Old Testament, in the design of the temple in Jerusalem and even earlier in the tabernacle, God uses physical structures to show the world who he is: an inviting God who wants to open access for all people to be reconciled to him, starting with his chosen people, the Jews. This temple was supposed to be the access point to God, with its levels and rings and dividing walls, a place to which all these people could be invited.

And inside its walls Jesus finds people selling oxen and sheep and pigeons, and money-changers sitting there. For a market like that to be open to everyone, it would have had to be in the outer ring. Otherwise only some people could reach it. So it would have been in the part of the temple reserved for the Gentiles. Gentile worshipers who had come from far away for the Passover would come into the only part of the temple where they were allowed to draw near to God, and what would they find? A market.

So picture it. You've made the long pilgrimage to Jerusalem. You arrive at the temple to make your offering and worship the Lord, and you hear the bleating of sheep, you smell the dung of the animals, and you hear the chatter and clamor of a marketplace. Jesus sees this, and his response is to make a whip of cords. But before we get to his response, let's see why this is wrong.

Imagine you're on your way to a birthday party, and you pop into CVS to grab a gift and hand it over in the CVS bag with the receipt. Is that okay? Not quite. Or you go to a wedding, and as your gift to the bride and groom you buy something at the venue's gift shop and drop it on the table. Remember what this feast is. Through your sacrifices you are celebrating and remembering the God who brought you out of captivity, who declares that he is your God, who will bring you into a land flowing with milk and honey and provide for you. This is the time of year when you are supposed to come in awe and wonder at the long history of God's faithfulness to you. And you can't be bothered to bring a lamb, an ox or pigeons with you. As a matter of convenience, you'll pick one up when you get there: I'll go into the temple, buy one at the market, carry it over there and sacrifice it to the Lord.

That by itself is an offense. That there was a market for this at all, enough demand that there was profit in it, and that this became the main way people handled this time of year, is an offense in itself.

But it isn't an offense because God is stingy. That you could offer an ox, a sheep or two pigeons was a concession God made because he wanted everyone included in this feast. If you can't afford an ox, a sheep will do. If you can't afford a sheep, two pigeons will do. The Lord was wonderful at opening this celebration to all people. He cared more about the spirit of what he was doing than the letter of the law. We see that when Jesus contends with the Pharisees, who were excellent at keeping the letter of the law but missed its spirit so badly that when perfect obedience to the law stood before them in Christ, they didn't recognize him. This is a God who makes concessions in order to bring more and more people into his presence. The offense is people presuming on him, treating him as if he were powerless, just showing up to check the box: I'll buy it when I get there, make the sacrifice, and then get to the party.

Then there are the money-changers. The temple tax had to be paid in coinage the temple accepted. Pilgrims coming from the far reaches of the Roman Empire arrived with whatever money was standard where they lived, so they needed to exchange it in Jerusalem for coins the temple would take. So a currency exchange was set up, again inside the temple. And the money-changers made the exchange at a markup. They were profiting off the people of God who had come from the ends of the empire to worship their King.

So there's a major offense happening in the temple on several counts. People are hindering access to God. People are making a mockery of the sacrifices. And people are profiting off others, not even to increase what is given to the temple but to line their own pockets. You can see the problem Jesus meets when he walks in.

Now let's see how he responds. Verses 15 and 16: "And making a whip of cords, he drove them all out of the temple, with the sheep and oxen. And he poured out the coins of the money-changers and overturned their tables. And he told those who sold the pigeons, 'Take these things away; do not make my Father's house a house of trade.'"

I've heard this passage preached many times, and it's often treated as the story that justifies our anger. Jesus flipped tables, dumped out the money, made a whip of cords, so when I flip out, I'm just like Jesus. We say that Jesus modeled righteous, holy anger here, and we build a whole defense around it: there is such a thing as righteous anger, and that's what I have. I want to tell you that this passage can't be used to justify the way you express anger, and we'll get to why.

But first, verse 15: "And making a whip of cords." Jesus didn't have a whip of cords on him. He didn't see what was going on, pull a whip out of his pocket and get down to business. He didn't flip out and react on impulse to what he saw. He stopped, assessed the situation and found the materials he needed, whatever cords and twine were lying around the animal pens. He found a place to sit and braided a whip. I don't know how long that takes, but it's long enough to cool down. He knew exactly what he was doing. Then he went back, drove the cattle and sheep and money-changers out of the temple with the whip, overturned the tables, poured out the coins and said, "Do not make my Father's house a house of trade."

This is usually where the turn comes: see, as long as your anger is premeditated, as long as you thought it through, as long as your reasons are just and pure, that's how Jesus models righteous anger. Fine. But that would be a poor handling of this text, because of verse 17: "His disciples remembered that it was written, 'Zeal for your house will consume me.'"

I imagine that when they remembered this line, they focused on the word zeal: Jesus is really zealous for his Father's house. That much is true. But I don't think it's an accident that what the Lord brought to their minds was Psalm 69. It's important to take it in context, so let me read it. David writes, "For it is for your sake that I have borne reproach, that dishonor has covered my face. I have become a stranger to my brothers, an alien to my mother's sons. For zeal for your house has consumed me, and the reproaches of those who reproach you have fallen on me. When I wept and humbled my soul with fasting, it became my reproach. When I made sackcloth my clothing, I became a byword to them. I am the talk of those who sit in the gate, and the drunkards make songs about me."

That's the passage the disciples have in mind when they see Jesus' zeal for the house of the Lord. And the second half of the line they quote matters far more than the first. It's not only zeal for the house. It's zeal for the house that consumes him. To consume means to eat up, to devour. Zeal for the house of the Lord consumed our Lord. So if you want to compare your zeal to Jesus' zeal here, unless you're prepared to be consumed by zeal for the house of the Lord, to give up your life and spill your blood, you aren't comparing apples to apples. Jesus' zeal for his Father's house is so great that he is willing to be consumed by it for his Father's house.

Here is the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world. He has made the pilgrimage to the holy city and walked into the temple, the physical access point to God meant for all the nations. He goes into the outer ring and sees that the worship of the Lord is being hindered, that the people meant to worship God can't do it cleanly or the way the Lord intended. And he is so consumed with zeal for it that he will lay down his very life to fix what is broken there. Jesus didn't make a whip of cords only to clear a space, for the rest of that day, where people could draw near to the Father. He cleansed the physical temple that day so that later he could give up his life and make access to God clean and open to all people forever. He was consumed by zeal for the house of the Lord.

Verse 18: "So the Jews said to him, 'What sign do you show us for doing these things?'" Let's see this correctly. By this action Jesus is claiming authority to regulate the temple. He takes it on himself to make a whip, drive people out and say, "Do not make my Father's house a house of trade." The Jews look at that and ask, "What sign do you show us for doing these things?" And they are right to pause. Not just anyone would claim authority to regulate the temple of God at Passover. You've shown up claiming the right to govern what happens in the temple. What sign do you show us? They want to know: are we dealing with a crazy zealot, or a prophet sent from heaven to clean up the house of the Lord?

But the question isn't sincere. If they really wanted the answer, they would see that what Jesus had just done was sign enough that he was sent from the Lord. It was a deeply godly act. Of course the Lord cares about keeping the outer court clean for the outsider. They don't see it.

"Jesus answered them, 'Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.'" Notice what Jesus doesn't say. He doesn't refuse them a sign. He doesn't say, that was sign enough. He doesn't deny what they ask. He answers, destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up. He isn't being clever. He is literally offering them a sign. You asked for one; here it is.

"The Jews then said, 'It has taken forty-six years to build this temple, and will you raise it up in three days?' But he was speaking about the temple of his body. When therefore he was raised from the dead, his disciples remembered that he had said this, and they believed the Scripture and the word that Jesus had spoken."

"Destroy this temple." He is standing in the temple, and when he says "this temple," he is talking about himself. He is declaring, I have come to replace these stones, to become myself the cornerstone of a new temple. Tear this one down, and in three days I will raise it up. But they didn't understand that he meant himself.

So go back to the zeal for the house that consumed him. It wasn't zeal for a building. At Calvary, when Jesus is crucified, the curtain of the temple is torn in two, and within a generation the temple itself will come down. This was never about the structure. It was about what was hindering access to God, the very thing Jesus came to resolve. So he says, tear this temple down, and three days later I will raise it up. In giving up his body, he declares that his body is the temple of the Lord.

We'll see this all through John. Jesus walking on this earth among the people is himself doing what the temple used to do. God was dwelling among his people in him. The temple was no longer necessary. An arrangement of one special place and one special time for access to God was no longer necessary. Jesus was tearing down the dividing walls of hostility to give all people direct access to God by his own blood. His zeal for the house of the Lord consumed him, which means he was literally willing to be consumed to open the house of the Lord to all people.

So again: unless our zeal for the house of the Lord... but let's hear "house of the Lord" correctly. It used to be the temple. In this passage Jesus says, now I am the temple. He calls himself the temple and invites the people who want a sign to destroy him. You want a sign? Destroy me. They don't understand, but they'll do it anyway. They destroy him, and three days later, as he said, he raises it up. After he is crucified, he rises from the dead. He gives them the sign they asked for, and his zeal for the house of the Lord is what compelled him to do it.

And then, having risen, he sends the Holy Spirit to fill the church, and he says that you and I are now his body. Follow the logic. The temple used to be the house of the Lord. Then Jesus says, no, my body is the temple. Then he gives up his body: destroy it, and I will raise it up. And by his Spirit, he says, now you are my body. The church Christ is building, the whole purpose of all things, is a great temple made up of all the peoples of the earth, every tribe and tongue and nation, built together for one purpose: worshiping the King. You are now his body, so his zeal for his house now applies to you, to the church. "In three days I will raise it up." But he doesn't take his body up again in its old form, does he? He returns glorified, and in his glorification he gathers all of us into the new body he is creating.

So unless your zeal for the house of the Lord, which now means the people of God, leads you to be persecuted, beaten, mocked, stoned, imprisoned or crucified, please don't use this passage to say your zeal is like Jesus' zeal.

Verses 23 to 25: "Now when he was in Jerusalem at the Passover Feast, many believed in his name when they saw the signs that he was doing. But Jesus on his part did not entrust himself to them, because he knew all people and needed no one to bear witness about man, for he himself knew what was in man."

This can be a hard doctrine, but here's what we're reading. The people say, give us a sign. Jesus says, I will, and he will. And along the way he does many lesser signs. We'll read about them all: water turned into wine, the dead raised, the blind healed, the sick made well, walking on water. Many people will believe in his name when they see the signs he's doing. But that isn't enough. You can't look at the miracles of Jesus and stop there. Jesus did not entrust himself to them, because he knew all people and he knew what was in man.

All kinds of people will tell you they believe in Jesus on the basis of the facts: we saw the miracles, so we believe in his name. Believing in his name means different things to different people. Many won't deny that Jesus was a healer, a miracle worker, a prophet, something. He clearly wasn't nothing. He changed the world. A lot of people won't reject Jesus wholesale. But believing in Jesus because you saw his signs is apparently not enough. The second part must also be true: Jesus on his part must entrust himself to you. That's the far more important part. Not that you see something with your eyes and think, something's going on here, but that Jesus, looking into your heart, entrusts himself to you. Knowing what was in man, he did not entrust himself to those in Jerusalem who believed in his name.

So my question to you this morning is this: has Jesus entrusted himself to you? Or has this been a one-way exchange, where you've weighed the facts, read the stories, decided this seems worth believing, noticed some benefits to believing in Jesus, and decided you'd like some of the things he seems able to do? When he looks into your heart, does he find that you know you are a sinner who needs saving? Are you trusting in his life of righteousness, his life, death and resurrection on your behalf? Or are you still bargaining with him, coming with your righteousness, with what you've earned, with your excuses, saying in effect, Jesus, I know I need what you offer, but you kind of need me too? He does not entrust himself to people like that.

I know this is frightening, but Jesus said it himself: "On that day many will say to me, 'Lord, Lord, did we not prophesy in your name, and cast out demons in your name, and do many mighty works in your name?' And then will I declare to them, 'I never knew you; depart from me, you workers of lawlessness.'" How is that possible? Because when people see the signs he does, many will believe in his name. But on his part Jesus does not entrust himself to them, because he knows the inner workings of the human heart.

All of us need to hear these sobering words, and remember the setting. Jesus went into the temple. These were the people of God. That these things were happening there means they knew the observances. They knew the letter of the law and were trying to keep it. They were even finding convenient ways to keep it. They might have said they were doing everyone a service by letting them exchange their money when they arrived. And all of it missed the heart of God. Jesus stood in the very temple, among people who would have said, we are certainly God's people, people who saw what he did and thought, he's really something. And he did not entrust himself to them. If that could be true in the temple while he walked the earth, it can be true of someone in this room today.

You are not called to make some thin claim that you believe in Jesus. When Jesus looks into our hearts, he must find people who know he has to entrust himself to them. We bring nothing to that exchange. In our own power and righteousness we cannot bargain with him or earn anything. We come to him as sinners who need grace and nothing more, counting on his work, his faithfulness, his life of righteousness, his sacrificial death on our behalf and his resurrection. This is it. This is the gospel. Believing in the miracles of Jesus is useless apart from receiving the gospel.

So this morning I pray that we would all search our hearts again and ask, Lord, have you entrusted yourself to me? It doesn't matter what I've done in his name. When I stand before God on the day of judgment, I won't be able to say, I preached the gospel in your name, I planted a church, I shepherded people. You can go to school for this, get the right credentials, apply for a job and become a pastor. It doesn't gain you entrance into heaven or favor with the Lord. Good works done apart from the Spirit of Christ are like filthy rags. We don't earn the favor of the Lord. Christ earns it on our behalf.

He was so consumed with opening access to the Lord for all people that he gave up his own life to purchase it. So we receive his covering, his blood, his sacrifice. We let him be consumed on our behalf, because the truth is that none of us is consumed with this as we ought to be. If we were, we wouldn't need him. But by God's grace, the Father sent him anyway.

This is a simple message, friends. It's not about anger, or righteous anger, or zeal, or what you need to do. It's not about mimicking Jesus. It's about receiving him. He came to the place where he should most have found people who truly understood, and he found it dead. So he brought it to life. Let's pray together that he would not walk into a place like this and drive us out with a whip of cords. May this be a place where he, who knows all things, looks into our hearts and finds a people who depend on him. It is by dependence alone that we receive the favor of the Lord. Let's pray to that end.
