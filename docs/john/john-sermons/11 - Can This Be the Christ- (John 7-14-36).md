# Can This Be the Christ? (John 7:14-36)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 7:14-36

---

A couple of months ago I saw a meme. It was just text, and it said something like this: when I was a kid, I suspected the adults didn't really know what they were doing, but I had no idea the situation was this dire. I thought about that meme as I studied this interaction with Jesus.

I'm more of a flow guy than a points guy, but if you take notes, here's the flow. First, I want to set side by side the manifold wisdom of God, embodied in Christ Jesus, and the folly of the man who is wise in his own sight. Second, I want to glory in the power of the Holy Spirit to make a person wise in the things of Christ, and to caution us against the power of the flesh to confuse the voice of our own passions with the voice of God.

We'll see both as Jesus answers three questions he's asked in this passage. In effect he's asked: Where do you come from? Who taught you and sent you? And where are you going? As he answers those three, I believe we'll see both contrasts.

We're in John 7 again. Last week Jesus' brothers were goading him to go with them to the Feast of Booths. They were taunting him and calling him a liar and a deceiver: if you really do the things you say you do, you wouldn't do them in secret. Why do it out by the Sea of Tiberias, on the side of a mountain? Why do it in Samaria? In their minds it was because he didn't really want to be known publicly, because he wasn't really doing what he claimed. Jesus told them no, he wouldn't go with them; they should go on ahead. And he said something stark and startling: the world cannot hate you, but it hates me because I testify about it that its works are evil. So he set up that contrast between himself and his brothers and sent them to the feast without him. We ended last week with Jesus going up to the feast separately, by the will of his Father, not the will of his brothers, while the people searched for him. The Jews were looking for him and saying, "Where is he?" Everyone was muttering about him. Some said, "He is a good man." Others said, "No, he is leading the people astray." Both were totally wrong. No one had seen Jesus yet, but there was a search party out for him.

Remember the last time Jesus was in Judea, back in chapter 5, because he'll refer to it today. He healed the invalid at the pool of Bethesda and told him to take up his bed and walk on the Sabbath. That started a controversy with the religious leaders, who charged Jesus with working on the Sabbath. Jesus answered, "My Father is working until now, and I am working," in so many words saying, I am the God of the Sabbath. And John tells us the Jews sought all the more to kill him, because he was "making himself equal with God." After that, Jesus left them for the countryside.

It's been some time since he's been among these people. He's been avoiding Judea because the Jews were seeking to kill him, and yet here he is at the Feast of Booths. Verses 14 to 18: "About the middle of the feast Jesus went up into the temple and began teaching. The Jews therefore marveled, saying, 'How is it that this man has learning, when he has never studied?' So Jesus answered them, 'My teaching is not mine, but his who sent me. If anyone's will is to do God's will, he will know whether the teaching is from God or whether I am speaking on my own authority. The one who speaks on his own authority seeks his own glory; but the one who seeks the glory of him who sent him is true, and in him there is no falsehood.'"

I don't want to patronize Jesus by being a fanboy, but this is really cool. There's a search party looking for Jesus. The Jews expect him to come to the feast, so they're hunting for him. The people are so afraid of the Jews that they speak of Jesus in hushed tones; they don't want to be caught saying his name. They're muttering about him. And Jesus goes up privately, knowing they're seeking to kill him and they've got the search party out. Remember, this is the Feast of Booths, so you can imagine them tossing the booths looking for him. They're going to find him by their own might, with their search party and their strength. Nobody dares to talk about him. And then, about the middle of the feast, he waltzes into the temple and starts teaching.

While the search party is out, he takes their spot, their teaching spot, and starts teaching the people publicly. If you're taking notes, that's a point: Jesus is really cool. He isn't afraid of anyone. And the Jews marvel as he teaches: "How is it that this man has learning, when he has never studied?"

That's the wrong kind of marveling. They aren't marveling at what he's teaching so much as at the fact that he's teaching without having been taught by man. They know he wasn't raised in their schools, wasn't brought up under a scribe, wasn't an educated man. So they marvel: on whose authority? Under whose teaching? Who sent this man? We don't know who he studied under, so how can he teach like this, with this mastery of the things of God and of the Scriptures?

John doesn't tell us what he was teaching, but we can guess, because he refers to Moses later in the passage. He was most likely teaching from the Old Testament. He may well have been teaching on the kingdom of God, the subject the Gospels record him teaching more than any other. And as he teaches with power and wisdom, the people marvel, wondering how he can be learned if he has never studied. Jesus answers them, "My teaching is not mine, but his who sent me."

He's drawing on their own traditions here, and honestly he gives them some credit. Think about the context. The scribes, or anyone who taught in the temple or a synagogue, would almost never teach on their own authority. Teachers got their authority from precedent, from tradition, from the one who taught them, or from the Scriptures themselves when they opened the scrolls. You wouldn't dare speak more than a few minutes without saying, thus says someone other than me, whoever it was you drew your authority from.

In most cases you'd be citing Scripture directly, or the teacher who discipled you, the scribe who came before you and taught you the tradition of the elders, the body of rulings and precedents that was later written down as the Mishnah, the law book of the Jews. In a setting where all authority comes from someone else, from tradition, from precedent, from the rulings of those who came before you, from the scroll itself, Jesus stands up, never having been taught in any of those settings, and speaks with an authority that makes the people marvel. Where did he get his authority? And Jesus says, "My teaching is not mine, but his who sent me."

He means God the Father, of course. Jesus has already established this with this audience and with others, over and over: he only does the will of the Father. I only speak the words of the Father. I and the Father are one. This is Jesus' lofty claim.

We said the teachers and scribes wouldn't teach on their own authority. In ancient Israel, the ones who could say, "Thus says the Lord," were the prophets. A prophet could say it because God Almighty had spoken a message to him and commissioned him to speak it to the people. When the Lord spoke to a prophet, he could go and say, thus says the Lord. And once he had delivered that message, he went back to speaking in his own voice.

Jesus goes further. He isn't claiming to be a prophet who occasionally speaks words from God. He's claiming that every word he speaks comes from the mouth of the Father, that every time he opens his mouth he speaks with the full backing of his Father in heaven, the one who sent him. That's why you see Jesus persistently saying things like, "You have heard that it was said… But I say to you." Or, "Truly, truly, I say to you." Where does he get that authority? From the one who sent him. Again and again Jesus speaks in his own name and claims his words come straight from the Father, making himself equal with God.

We press on where Jesus gets his authority because he's about to contrast it with the one who teaches on his own authority. He's telling us why his words are trustworthy, why we can hang on every word. It's the reason I preach straight through books of the Bible on Sunday mornings. He tells us there's a world of difference between sitting under words that come from the will of the one who sent him and sitting under words that come from within a man.

He says, if anyone's will is to do God's will, he will know whether the teaching is from God or whether I am speaking on my own authority, because "the one who speaks on his own authority seeks his own glory; but the one who seeks the glory of him who sent him is true, and in him there is no falsehood."

We need to look at this in two parts. First, as I said we would, we need to marvel and glory in the power of the Holy Spirit.

We should never pull what Jesus teaches in one place apart from what he teaches in another. He's building a story for his people through his earthly teaching ministry. Back in chapter 6, in verse 44, Jesus taught that no one can come to him unless the Father who sent him draws him, carries him. That was a big sermon a few weeks ago. Verse 45 goes on: "It is written in the Prophets, 'And they will all be taught by God.' Everyone who has heard and learned from the Father comes to me—not that anyone has seen the Father except he who is from God; he has seen the Father." He means himself, of course. Of those the Father gives him, he says, every one of them, as the prophets said, will be taught by God.

So connect these two things Jesus said, and we take them seriously because Jesus said them. Read verse 17 again: "If anyone's will is to do God's will, he will know whether the teaching is from God or whether I am speaking on my own authority."

How will he know that the teaching is from God? Jesus said in chapter 6, verse 45, "Everyone who has heard and learned from the Father comes to me." So how do you know whether you're hearing and learning from God? Those who hear and learn from God come to Jesus.

That's the proof. That's the evidence: those who receive the teaching of God are those who come to the Son. In that same conversation in chapter 6, they asked him, "What must we do, to be doing the works of God?" And Jesus answered, "This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he has sent." To believe in the Son, to come to him, is to know you've received teaching from the Father, because the teaching that comes from the Father, his will, is that his people come to his Son.

I know it seems almost circular. Which comes first, the chicken or the egg? The reason it feels that way in our minds is that it's all God. It's all the Father. We keep trying to find the part where we enter in and take responsibility, but we find instead that this is a work of God. He opens you to receive the teaching that drives you to the Son. And as you come to the Son, the Son's teaching points you to the Father. Round and round, you're simply brought under the glory and majesty of the one true God, Father, Son and Spirit.

So where's our responsibility? Look at verse 17 again. If anyone's will is to do God's will, he will know whether the teaching is from God. But Jesus also said that no one can come unless the Father draws him, so by nature it's no one's will to do God's will. If by nature it's no one's will to do God's will, and yet I need the will to do God's will in order to know whether his teaching is from God, we have to stop and ask, Jesus, how do you resolve this tension? The answer is the Holy Spirit.

Jesus will promise this again and again as we read through this book, and he'll make it clear. Near the end of his ministry, the night before his death, he says to his disciples, "I still have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now. When the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth, for he will not speak on his own authority, but whatever he hears he will speak, and he will declare to you the things that are to come. He will glorify me, for he will take what is mine and declare it to you." Hear the same words as in our passage: the Spirit "will not speak on his own authority." Jesus goes to the Father and sends the Spirit, and the Spirit does only the will of the Father and teaches us the things of the Son.

That's the role of the Holy Spirit. The Spirit, who only ever does the will of the Father and who indwells the church, is the one who lets you say that it is your will to do God's will. The one who can say, it is my will to do God's will, is the one filled with the Spirit, who only ever does God's will. That person will carry out God's will. It will be his will to do God's will.

We'll come to our responsibility in a moment. But first it's important that we hold a high and true view of the role of the Holy Spirit in the life of the church. Your will to do God's will depends on the Holy Spirit in you. But you also have the assurance of the Holy Spirit in you, who is only ever inclined to do God's will, which means that at any moment you, the church, filled with the Spirit, can hear the will of God and do it. This is freedom. This is our rescue from bondage and slavery to sin.

Once we were dead in our trespasses; now we have been made alive in Christ by the Holy Spirit. Now you are able to hear and discern the voice of God. When the Son speaks, you know he is the one God has sent. This is the work of the Holy Spirit. It's your blessed assurance that when I'm up here reading the Bible, you can receive it as true and authoritative because of the work of the Holy Spirit in you. That's a gift of God, and it's your assurance.

But we'd be foolish to ignore the power of the flesh to resist the Holy Spirit, who only does, and only wants to do, the will of the Father. The flesh is always bent on doing its own will. So on this side of eternity, before you are perfectly conformed to the image of Christ, you feel the tension. It seems as though you have some autonomy to weigh what the Spirit is showing you is God's will against your own will, and the two pull against each other. I bring this up because whenever I'm in a room with you, and whenever you're in a room with one another, I firmly believe in what's called the priesthood of all believers. I believe it because I believe my Bible, and you should believe it too because you believe yours. I get really excited in your company because the Holy Spirit is in you.

I believe that at any moment your mouth can become the mouthpiece of God, because you are indwelt by the Holy Spirit. I expect to hear from my Lord through you, because he's taken up residence in you. That's why I value gathering with other believers so much. I need you in my living room so I can hear from God. That feels too big to say, doesn't it?

I don't want to say it, but I'm saying it. My Lord speaks to me through you, because his Holy Spirit has taken up residence in you. By the Holy Spirit you can know the manifold wisdom of God and the hidden things of Christ. You can. But I also want you to know that when you speak, I'm listening for whether it is your will to do God's will, or whether you're speaking on your own authority.

Are you seeking the glory of the one who sent you, or your own glory? That's what Jesus is talking about, and it matters. Let me say it more directly. Jesus is saying, my Father sent me, and I seek only to do his will, and that's why you can trust what I say.

And he says that whoever teaches seeking only the glory of the one who sent him is trustworthy. Well, we are sent. We go out sent by the Father, Son and Holy Spirit. So to the degree that you desire to glorify the one who sent you, Father, Son and Spirit, and to do his will, I ought to hang on every word you have to say. But to the degree that you resist the glory of God and fight for your own glory, and resist the will of God and fight for your own will, I should be highly suspicious of every word you have to say. And you should be just as suspicious of me.

The day I get up here and start saying, you know what I think, you know what I think, you need to get me out of here. Get me out of here, because Jesus said the one who speaks on his own authority seeks his own glory, but the one who seeks the glory of him who sent him is true, and in him there is no falsehood. My only standing up here is that what comes out of my mouth is what came out of Christ's mouth. And if Christ said it, it has all the authority in the world, so drink it up. It's nourishment for you and for me, and I need it back from you.

But sometimes it isn't obvious to me, or to you, who exactly sent you or who sent me. There are plenty of times, in a living room or on social media or somewhere else, when I think, these words aren't coming from the Father. These came from a news anchor on the left. That came from one on the right. The words coming out of your mouth aren't from someone seeking the glory of the one who sent him.

They're from someone seeking the glory of so-and-so, and in most cases, as Jesus says, seeking his own glory. When Jesus sets the folly of man beside the manifold wisdom of God in Christ Jesus, the glory of the one is meant to show the other as it really is: as small and as insignificant as it actually is. And one of the most dangerous things I see us do, and I see it so often, is speak with real authority about something we read twenty minutes ago.

You've seen it. I already think something, and I need a champion to back my position. So I search for it, find somebody who agrees with me, and then cite it as if I'd always known it, when I read it five minutes ago. It's what we do when we surround ourselves with teachers and champions who help us seek our own glory and teach from within ourselves. And it's folly. It's foolishness. But the one who seeks the glory and the will of the one who sent him, your Father, Son and Spirit, your one true God?

Well, goodness, I'm leaning in. I want to know what he has to say. And I want to know what you have to say when you've spent time with him, when you've sought him out.

Jesus continues, verses 19 to 24: "'Has not Moses given you the law? Yet none of you keeps the law. Why do you seek to kill me?' The crowd answered, 'You have a demon! Who is seeking to kill you?' Jesus answered them, 'I did one work, and you all marvel at it. Moses gave you circumcision (not that it is from Moses, but from the fathers), and you circumcise a man on the Sabbath. If on the Sabbath a man receives circumcision, so that the law of Moses may not be broken, are you angry with me because on the Sabbath I made a man's whole body well? Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment.'"

Here Jesus points back to the reason the Jews were seeking to kill him in the first place. Remember, he healed the invalid at the pool of Bethesda on the Sabbath.

He made a man's whole body well on the Sabbath, and he points them to their own law. The law said, keep the Sabbath. It also said, circumcise your sons on the eighth day. So when the two collide and the eighth day falls on a Sabbath, you circumcise on the Sabbath, because circumcision, the sign of the promise, takes precedence. And yet here I stand among you, God in the flesh, and by my word I make a man's whole body well on the Sabbath, and you seek to kill me.

You seek to kill me. It's foolishness. And this is the difference between a self-made godliness and the godliness, the righteousness, that comes from the Holy Spirit. A self-made godliness pores over the law and the tradition of the elders.

Now, we're not sitting under the Mishnah. We're not pulling out the Jewish law book to look up the precedent as we try to obey the law. That's not what we're doing.

But we've got our own Mishnah. In American Christianity we've got the things you can do, the things you're supposed to do and the things you're not supposed to do. And we want to follow that.

What we often do in this self-made godliness is follow and obey a god of our own making. A god of our own making. We certainly bake in some of the things he has actually said, to help it go down easier. But here's the thing. If your God never disagrees with you, it's a good question to ask whom you're worshiping.

If your God never says anything you disagree with, if he never commands anything of you that you weren't going to do anyway, if he never expects anything of you that you don't already think ought to be expected, then you've decided for yourself. You'll hear people say, "I could never believe in a God who…" and then fill in the blank. As if God auditions for our faith. As if God becomes real according to how much he agrees with what we already think.

What you're really saying is, I won't worship that God, because I didn't create that God. In some way we're all tempted to worship our own Jesus, and he won't permit it. Jesus won't permit it. He says we must seek the glory of the one who sent him and seek to do his will. And if we want to know what his will is, and whether a teaching comes from his mouth, the test is this: does it draw us to his Son?

Does the teaching compel us to worship and exalt and magnify the riches of the Son? The teaching that comes from the Father magnifies the Son. So if the way you handle the teaching you've put yourself under gives you a lower view of Jesus and a higher view of yourself, or puts anyone else in the place that belongs to Jesus, or conforms Jesus to the image you want him to take rather than conforming you to his image, that teaching does not come from the mouth of the Father. In this way our flesh is deceitful. And in this way, I think, so many of us decide whether God has spoken by how agreeable we find what we're hearing.

How do I know if it's from God? Well, do I like it? If I like it, he must be saying it. Stop it.

Let's keep going. Can this be the Christ? Verses 25 to 31: "Some of the people of Jerusalem therefore said, 'Is not this the man whom they seek to kill? And here he is, speaking openly, and they say nothing to him! Can it be that the authorities really know that this is the Christ? But we know where this man comes from, and when the Christ appears, no one will know where he comes from.' So Jesus proclaimed, as he taught in the temple, 'You know me, and you know where I come from. But I have not come of my own accord. He who sent me is true, and him you do not know. I know him, for I come from him, and he sent me.' So they were seeking to arrest him, but no one laid a hand on him, because his hour had not yet come. Yet many of the people believed in him. They said, 'When the Christ appears, will he do more signs than this man has done?'"

Jesus has just told them, "Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment," and the people immediately start judging by appearances. First they say, the Jews are seeking to kill this man, and now he's standing up teaching in front of everyone, in their spot, and nobody's doing anything. Could it be that they actually think he's the Christ?

That's humiliating for the leaders, and it's going to escalate their conflict with Jesus, as we'll see over the next several weeks. But the first thing the people do is try to work out what the religious leaders think of Jesus. That will clear it up for me. They're not seizing him; maybe they believe. If they believe, maybe we should. That's judging by appearances. Second, they say, but we know where he comes from, and we're not supposed to know where the Messiah comes from. That's a bad sign. Maybe we shouldn't believe in him.

They start debating on the basis of appearances: is this the Christ? Then two things are said at the end of that section. First, they were seeking to arrest him, but no one laid a hand on him, because his hour had not yet come. It's what Jesus told his brothers: I'm not going up yet, because my time has not yet come.

We talked last week about how, when Jesus says his hour has not come, he means the hour of his death. There's a scene in an early episode of Stranger Things, and I'm not saying you should watch it, where a bully is going after another kid to beat him up. The main character, a girl with telekinetic powers, stops him in his tracks with her mind, and he wets himself in front of everyone.

That's what I picture when I read that they were seeking to arrest him and yet no one laid a hand on him, because his hour had not yet come. They wanted to arrest him. They wanted to kill him. He was standing right there.

John doesn't say why they didn't. But we know: it wasn't his time. Jesus hadn't yet chosen to die. Jesus hadn't yet chosen to let them arrest him. When he allowed it, they could come and put him in cuffs. Once again he shows himself to be God. And in this very moment, it says, many of the people believed in him. They said, "When the Christ appears, will he do more signs than this man has done?"

That's the most encouraging sentence in the section. It's as if the veil was lifted. They stopped trying to figure it out for themselves. They stopped leaning on their own wisdom and trying to read the signs, and they just looked at him.

They finally saw him, and they asked the right question. When the Christ appears, could he do more than this? It's got to be him. And it's got to be him.

Then we're shown the contrast. The Pharisees can't see it. Verses 32 to 36: "The Pharisees heard the crowd muttering these things about him, and the chief priests and Pharisees sent officers to arrest him. Jesus then said, 'I will be with you a little longer, and then I am going to him who sent me. You will seek me and you will not find me. Where I am you cannot come.' The Jews said to one another, 'Where does this man intend to go that we will not find him? Does he intend to go to the Dispersion among the Greeks and teach the Greeks? What does he mean by saying, "You will seek me and you will not find me," and, "Where I am you cannot come"?'"

We said Jesus would answer three questions. Where did you come from? I came from the Father, from heaven. Who taught you? God. Who sent you? God. Where are you going? I'm going back to him who sent me.

What does he mean when he says, you will seek me and you will not find me, and where I am you cannot come? He has answered the question: he's returning to the Father. We'll see it play out in a resurrection they don't yet understand. But many more will come to believe, you and me included, and when we hear the Son's teaching as the very will of the Father, the rest of his life starts to make sense.

Suddenly these things connect, and we'll see it all through this Gospel. The disciples are humble enough at times to write it right into their account: we didn't understand it when he said it, but after he was raised, we remembered, and we realized this is what he meant.

They say that again and again, in this Gospel and in the others. And that's us. Sometimes we read something and say, I believe you're telling the truth, Jesus, but I don't really get it, until we see the fuller picture. Then suddenly it all makes sense: the God of all creation took on human flesh, laid down his life, took it up again and ascended to the right hand of the Father. And today the Father, Son and Spirit rescue and ransom a people for himself, you and me.

He does it by his power, by his Spirit in you, to fulfill his will in creation. So I want to encourage you to lay down your lesser righteousness, your lesser wisdom, your lesser preferences, your lesser teachers. Lay down all of it, and bow at the feet of Jesus, because that's the proof that what you're hearing comes from the mouth of the Father. If you're yielding to him, worshiping him and magnifying him, seeing him as more beautiful, more precious and more powerful, that's how you know you're under the teaching of the Father. And I pray that as you are, you'll carry the Father's teaching to one another. Let's pray together to that end.
