# The First Witnesses (John 20:1-18)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 20:1-18

---

Since it's Resurrection Sunday, it's fitting that we look this morning at John 20:1-18. This morning we'll be exploring the resurrection account of Jesus.

Let me read it for you: "Now on the first day of the week Mary Magdalene came to the tomb early, while it was still dark, and saw that the stone had been taken away from the tomb. So she ran and went to Simon Peter and the other disciple, the one whom Jesus loved, and said to them, 'They have taken the Lord out of the tomb, and we do not know where they have laid him.' She said to them, 'They have taken away my Lord, and I do not know where they have laid him.' Having said this, she turned around and saw Jesus standing, but she did not know that it was Jesus. "'Whom are you seeking?' Supposing him to be the gardener, she said to him, 'Sir, if you have carried him away, tell me where you have laid him, and I will take him away.' Jesus said to her, 'Mary.' She turned and said to him in Aramaic, 'Rabboni!' (which means Teacher). "Mary Magdalene went and announced to the disciples, 'I have seen the Lord'—and that he had said these things to her." This is the word of the Lord for us this morning. In verses 1 and 2, what I want you to see is Mary Magdalene, a beloved friend and disciple of Jesus Christ, approaching his tomb expecting to find it full, not empty. Full, and not empty.

Since we're jumping into chapter 20 and you may not be familiar with Mary Magdalene: she was a beloved friend and disciple of Jesus. She loved him. She's recognized as one of the most central women in all of the New Testament and in all of Christian tradition. Her life, as the Gospels portray it, gives us a picture of profound transformation. If you need an example, men and women, of what it looks like to have everything turned upside down because you have encountered Jesus Christ as Lord, you can look at Mary Magdalene.

She had an extreme transformation that gave her an enduring commitment to her Lord. She came from the town of Magdala, on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee. That's where you get Mary Magdalene. The Gospels, in Luke 8 and Mark 16, introduce her as a woman from whom Jesus cast out seven demons.

That's where we first meet Mary, filled with seven demons. Then Jesus shows up, and with this dramatic healing he casts the seven demons out of her, and this is what begins the discipleship of Mary Magdalene. This is what begins the devotion she has in her heart toward Jesus Christ. She's been freed from her afflictions, and Mary starts following him and even supporting his ministry out of her own means. She follows him on his travels, completely devoted, everywhere he goes.

Her role is especially prominent in this part of the Gospels, starting around John 19, where we see her devotion to him in his final hours: at his crucifixion, at his burial and at his resurrection. The Gospels show her as one of the few faithful ones who stayed right with Jesus at the cross. Peter wasn't there. Most of the disciples were not there. Mary was there.

Mary saw him crucified. She stayed with him in his darkest hour. That's in Matthew 27 and Mark 15. And after his death, she saw him buried. She followed along, and she saw the place where he was laid.

That's how she knew where to go in this passage. Following him to his burial and then coming again three days later shows that she remains faithfully devoted to him, even in his death. She loves him, and her love and devotion are sincere, even here, in her faithless, dark hour, after he has been killed. The most significant part the Bible gives to the life and ministry of Mary Magdalene is right here in John 20. Jesus Christ came into the world, the one in whom, through whom and for whom all things were made. He came into the world, born of a virgin, and lived thirty-three years, a life of perfection, perfectly fulfilling the law of God, perfectly obedient to the will of the Father, teaching only the words that came from the mouth of God.

He said to Philip, "Whoever has seen me has seen the Father." He is the very incarnation of God, Jesus Christ. Those thirty-three years of a perfect life of righteousness culminate in Good Friday, this past Friday, when we soberly remember that God, the Lord, the Son of God, Jesus Christ, living that perfect life, sinless in every way, turned himself over to wicked men like you and me. He let himself be killed at the hands of sinners as an atoning sacrifice for the sins of the very ones who put him on the cross, so that anyone who is united with him by faith is credited with the perfect life of righteousness he lived for us. And he offers his death as a substitute for the death we ought to die on account of our sin, the sinless one exchanging his life for ours, his death for ours. Then he's laid in the tomb, and Mary knows where it is. And she approaches that tomb on the third day, the first day of the week, in chapter 20, verse 1.

"Now on the first day of the week Mary Magdalene came to the tomb early, while it was still dark, and saw that the stone had been taken away from the tomb." That's all she saw. The stone wasn't there. And immediately, in verse 2, "she ran and went to Simon Peter and the other disciple, the one whom Jesus loved, and said to them, 'They have taken the Lord out of the tomb, and we do not know where they have laid him.'" This encounter at the tomb is recorded in all four Gospels.

I'm just going to preach it from John 20, but you should know that Matthew 28 tells us Mary Magdalene was not alone. Another woman, the other Mary, as Matthew calls her, was with her. Mark 16 tells us who that other Mary was: Mary the mother of James. Mark also tells us that Salome, another woman, was with them. Luke 24 adds Joanna, along with Mary the mother of James and Mary Magdalene, and it mentions that there were yet other women.

But here in John 20, what stands out to John is the personal encounter with this one woman, Mary Magdalene. Other women were with her. They were there too. But John fixes on the devotion of this one who had experienced total transformation, this one who really knew Jesus. And like I said, she was expecting to find the tomb full.

So when she finds that tomb open, rather than her heart leaping in delight, she panics. She is shocked to find it empty, and she turns and runs to whom? To her brothers, to Peter and John. What I want you to see here is that Mary approached the tomb even though she expected it to be full. Mary approached the tomb even though she expected it to be full.

That means that right now Mary is not some hero of the faith. Right? She didn't go anticipating that she would find it empty. We're not looking at Mary Magdalene here as the hero who really got it, who went anticipating that it would be empty.

She saw it was empty, and she got out of there. She went because she loved Jesus, she was devoted to him, and she wanted to be where she last saw him. Have you ever visited the grave of a loved one? You can raise your hand. Have you ever visited the grave of a loved one?

I have too. Why do we do that? There's something in us that wants to be in the place where we last saw them, so that we can mourn. Jewish mourning rituals went on for weeks, sometimes a month or more, and you would go day by day, back and forth, to and fro, from the tomb to your home, weeping and wailing and mourning. She was in deep despair over the loss of her beloved Jesus, and she wanted to be in the place where she last saw him.

She had followed them there and seen them lay him there. This is the third day, so it may not be the first time she's been at the tomb. But she goes on this third day expecting to find it full, just wanting to be in the place where she last saw him. And what you won't hear in my voice is any tone that wants to condemn her faithlessness. There is something absolutely beautiful about the fact that her despair, her hopelessness, her faithlessness still drove her, in her devotion to him, to want to be in the place where she last saw him.

See, before some of you can receive the good news of the resurrection, you need to be told, and I want to have this ministry over you this morning, that it's good to go to the place where you last saw Jesus, even with very little expectation, feeling totally hopeless, totally faithless, totally out of sorts, totally disoriented. Everything in your life tells you that you're going to go there and see death and more destruction and bad news. She went there expecting to find it full. And what did she find? She found it empty.

Jesus didn't wait to rise until she believed that he would. She went there expecting it to be full, and it was empty. By your faith you do not make Jesus rise. By your faith you do not make Jesus alive. Jesus is alive, and he is the living object of your faith.

So you go, frail as you are, to that tomb, to that last place where you saw him. And I want to make a tangential application. I know from talking with you what some of your prayer lives are like, what some of your relationships with Jesus are like. Some of you can remember a time when you prayed to Jesus and it felt so alive. You felt so alive. You felt like, man, he's listening to me.

You saw him moving mountains, and you couldn't wait to get into your prayer closet, because that's where you would find the risen, living Lord. Lately you're not sure he's listening at all. Lately your prayers are more like, where are you, Jesus? Lately you have very little expectation that he's going to move at all. So you go back into that prayer closet, but this time with a very different heart. This time, like Mary, you're going just to be in the place where you last saw him.

You know he's done something before. You're not expecting something today, but you just want to be where you last saw him. For some of you, that's what it's like to come to church on Sunday. You used to come in here, and you can remember a time. You can remember some camp, some experience, where you just felt so full, so alive in that place.

I was with him. I saw him. I felt him. And now you're coming in the side door, thinking, I just want to be in the place where I last saw him, where I last experienced him. I have very little expectation today, but I want to be here because I don't know where else to go.

Jesus is not waiting for you to increase in faith before he shows himself to be alive to you. It was beautiful that she went to the tomb in her despair, and point number two from these first two verses is that it is beautiful that in her despair she fled to the brothers. Where do I go? I run to Peter and John. My brothers. They know him.

What was she expecting to get from them? What she got from them was a shared load. She told them the news that the tomb was empty. And what was their response? We'll read it in a second.

They panic and run to the tomb, not expectantly, not thinking, we're going to find it just like he said. No. They need to see for themselves.

He's really gone? She gathers mourners to herself. She invites her brothers into her panic, into her despair, into her freak-out moment. She says, brothers, he's gone! And so they go. Verse 3: "So Peter went out with the other disciple, and they were going toward the tomb.

"Both of them were running together, but the other disciple outran Peter and reached the tomb first. And stooping to look in, he saw the linen cloths lying there, but he did not go in. Then Simon Peter came, following him, and went into the tomb. He saw the linen cloths lying there, and the face cloth, which had been on Jesus' head, not lying with the linen cloths but folded up in a place by itself. Then the other disciple," John, "who had reached the tomb first, also went in, and he saw and believed; for as yet they did not understand the Scripture, that he must rise from the dead. Then the disciples went back to their homes."

You may have heard it preached that where it says he saw and believed, it means he believed that Christ had risen. But that's not what it says. Just finish the sentence: "for as yet they did not understand the Scripture, that he must rise from the dead." What it means is that they hadn't believed Mary, that the stone was rolled away and Jesus' body had been taken and was missing. They had to see it for themselves.

They peek in, they see the linen cloths lying there, and they say, she's right! They took him! He's gone! And they return to their homes. So Mary has spread her panic.

And again, you don't hear me using a tone of condemnation for Mary's approach here. Mary did exactly what she was supposed to do. She went to the brothers, and they bore the burden with her. They went and saw for themselves, and they felt it. They felt it right next to her.

And they went back to their homes, somber. The sub-point for you this morning is this: the empty tomb is clearly not the same thing as the risen Christ. The empty tomb is clearly not the same thing as the risen Christ. When they saw the empty tomb, I don't see any rejoicing here yet. I don't see anything good yet.

I see panic and despair, because an empty tomb is not the same thing as the risen Christ. Some of us never get around to looking at the risen Christ. We stay down in the empty tomb, talking to the wall. We remain in this place: this is the last place I saw him, so I just want to stay right here.

And we hope to manufacture an experience with Jesus as if he's not alive, as if you can't still meet with him, as if he's not still standing today. Do you understand that Jesus rose bodily? Or do you look to the doctrine to comfort you rather than the person? Doctrine is truth about a person, and that person is alive.

You are not going to find comfort in the doctrine of the empty tomb. You're going to find comfort in the person who's not in that tomb. Part of this is the difference between saying, and this is a loose application, I know Jesus doesn't not love me, and saying, I know Jesus loves me. It's the difference between saying, I know Christ won't abandon me, and saying, I know Christ is with me.

Saying truths about Jesus and speaking from your relationship with Jesus are just not the same thing, although they feed each other. The empty tomb by itself was not enough to comfort them. They needed to see him, and they haven't yet. So where have they gone? They've gone back to their homes.

Verse 11: "But Mary stood weeping outside the tomb." She didn't go back to her home. She stayed, and she stood weeping outside the tomb, "and as she wept she stooped to look into the tomb. She turned and said to him in Aramaic, 'Rabboni!'"

The other Gospel accounts describe the angels in the tomb as men in white. She is deeply confused about what is happening here. Don't think Mary sat there realizing she was talking to angels. She was very casual about it, wasn't she? And she didn't realize she was talking to Jesus, and she was very casual about that too. Where did you take him?

She's talking to him, and her eyes are not yet opened to see. And yet, even as she continues in her faithlessness, even as she continues in her grief, even as she stays blind to what is going on, completely blinded by grief, Jesus and the angels of heaven meet with her. And what do they say? "Woman, why are you weeping?"

Jesus says it again: "Woman, why are you weeping? Whom are you seeking?" And then he says her name. What some of us need to be reminded of, as we read a passage like this, is that Jesus is interested in our tears.

Jesus is interested in your tears. I think we sometimes read that like a rhetorical question, like he's setting up a gotcha moment. No. He asks her sincerely, twice, through the angels and then directly: Woman, why are you weeping? Give it to me.

Some of you are struggling to relate to the resurrected Jesus because you won't bring your death to him, because you won't bring your despair to him, because you won't bring the truth of your doubt and your fear and your faithlessness to him, because you won't say it. Who cares about those tears? He cares. He cares about that distress. He would have you tell him: why are you weeping? Whom are you seeking? And so she answers, and take note of what she's still looking for.
