# The Empty Tomb (John 20:1-10)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 20:1-10

---

So here we are. We've been working our way through the Gospel of John for about fifteen to eighteen months now, slowly walking through all the events leading up to this text. We have seen Jesus beaten, mocked, scorned, crucified and killed. We saw a spear pierce his side.

We saw blood and water pour out. We saw the Son of God declare, "It is finished." And we saw him dead dead. We saw him taken off the cross and carried to a tomb cut from the rock, Joseph of Arimathea's tomb. We saw the stone rolled across that tomb, and then we saw it go quiet.

Last week we spent significant time on what was happening on the earth and what was happening underneath the earth, as we declared that Jesus Christ was putting death to death by his own death. Here, in this morning's passage, we pick up with his disciples wandering back to the tomb to mourn. If you were with us while we preached the death of Lazarus, you started to see what the Jewish mourning ritual was like. You would go back and forth, from your home to the tomb and from the tomb to your home, in many cases for weeks. You would go to the tomb to weep, and then return to your home.

And that's what's happening here. Mary Magdalene goes to the tomb to cry, to be near the body of her dead Lord. But when she gets there, she sees that the stone has been taken away and the body is missing. So she runs and gets Peter and John, and tells them, "They have taken the Lord out of the tomb, and we do not know where they have laid him." Her only logical conclusion is that somebody has removed the body.

We preached it last week: this was Joseph's tomb. So maybe Joseph moved the body. Maybe they found a different spot for him. Maybe the soldiers moved the body.

Maybe somebody took it. I don't know what happened, but the body is not there. And I just wanted to be near the body so that I might mourn, and now I don't even know where his body lies. This is one more grief on top of all the others. The disciples have scattered. Very few of them were even there to see him crucified, they were so afraid when he was betrayed. And now we don't even know where his body lies, so that we can mourn near it, so that we can cry near it. So Peter and John run to the tomb. John outran Peter and reached the tomb first, but he didn't go in. Maybe he was afraid. He peeked, but he wouldn't enter.

Peter walks right in. Verse 6: he "went into the tomb. He saw the linen cloths lying there, and the face cloth, which had been on Jesus' head, not lying with the linen cloths but folded up in a place by itself." Then John goes in, and "he saw and believed." We'll talk about that phrase in a second. The face cloth had been removed, folded up and placed in a different spot from the linen cloths that wrapped the body.

Something stood out, even in this moment, about the orderliness of the tomb. Whatever happened here didn't happen in a rush. Someone took care to fold up this face cloth and set it in a place by itself, while the linen cloths stayed where the body had lain. This called to mind a beautiful picture for me, and I want you to picture it with me: the dead body of Christ, steeped in seventy-five pounds of myrrh and aloes, its eyes opening three days later, sitting up in the tomb in a glorified body, removing the face cloth, folding it up nicely, setting it aside, and strolling out of the tomb. Peter doesn't yet understand how this face cloth came to be folded up and set off to the side.

But the grace with which our Lord reentered the earth is just marvelous to me, and I thought I'd hold out that tidbit to you. Then it says that when John entered, he saw and believed. We might be tempted to think it means he saw and believed that Jesus had risen, as he said he would, but the rest of the sentence takes that away from us: "for as yet they did not understand the Scripture, that he must rise from the dead."

So it was on entering and seeing with his own eyes that the body was missing that he believed Mary's account: they had taken Jesus' body and moved it somewhere else. That's what he believed. He's not here. He's not here. "For as yet they did not understand the Scripture, that he must rise from the dead. Then the disciples went back to their homes."

Here's what I want to preach to you all this morning: an empty tomb is not the same as a risen Savior. The disciples saw that the tomb was empty, and their response was to go back to their homes, because they did not yet understand the risen Christ. An empty tomb brought them into further despair, so that all they could say was, we'll go back home. We'll return to our homes.

There's nothing here to mourn. But the risen Christ ends the mourning altogether and brings you instead to joyful celebration. I pray we'll find our way into that celebration as we behold the risen Christ this morning, and not merely an empty tomb. The early church understood this. They understood that he died. They understood that he was buried.

But they understood this too: that he was raised on the third day. Paul writes in 1 Corinthians 15, "For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received: that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve. Then he appeared to more than five hundred brothers at one time, most of whom are still alive," so you can go and ask them, "though some have fallen asleep. Then he appeared to James, then to all the apostles. Last of all, as to one untimely born, he appeared also to me." Paul delivered to them as of first importance that Christ died for our sins, was buried, was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures, and appeared to a great cloud of witnesses, not only the disciples. The bodily resurrection of Jesus is of first importance for the Christian.

And Paul says what follows if it isn't true: "And if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile and you are still in your sins." Verse 19 says, "If in Christ we have hope in this life only, we are of all people most to be pitied."

"But in fact Christ has been raised from the dead." And that's what we've gathered here this morning to discuss. In Acts 5, Gamaliel, the honored teacher of the law, tells the council about a man named Theudas: "For before these days Theudas rose up, claiming to be somebody, and a number of men, about four hundred, joined him.

"He was killed, and all who followed him were dispersed and came to nothing. After him Judas the Galilean rose up in the days of the census and drew away some of the people after him. He too perished, and all who followed him were scattered." Up to this point in our story, we see a Jesus who looks a lot like Theudas and Judas the Galilean. He drew a following, and then he perished, and his following came to nothing.

They scattered. And there would be nothing more to talk about unless there's a resurrection. Verse 9 says, "for as yet they did not understand the Scripture, that he must rise from the dead." I think that as Christians we're pretty good at talking about why Jesus had to die. Why did he have to rise? There are many reasons. In Luke 24:25-26, Jesus says, "O foolish ones, and slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have spoken! Was it not necessary that the Christ should suffer these things and enter into his glory?"

And later in that chapter he says that "everything written about me in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms must be fulfilled," and "Thus it is written, that the Christ should suffer and on the third day rise from the dead." Peter says it in his sermon at Pentecost in Acts 2: "this Jesus, delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God, you crucified and killed by the hands of lawless men. God raised him up, loosing the pangs of death, because it was not possible for him to be held by it." He must rise.

He must rise. Why? I'll give you five reasons this morning.

First, Jesus Christ must rise from the dead to fulfill the promises of God to mankind, so that God is not made a liar. At the center of those promises is a king from the line of David who will reign eternally over a people who will be found right in the sight of God. This eternal reign was the hope of the ancient people, and it is our hope today, that we have a Lord who will stand in for us eternally, a perfect, spotless one, a King who will reign forever. If Jesus Christ dies and stays dead, then he makes God a liar, or he is not the Messiah.

He must live in order to reign eternally in righteousness with you and me. Number two: he must rise in order to vindicate his sinless life. What do I mean by this? I mean that Jesus claimed to be without sin.

He says, "Which one of you convicts me of sin?" His claim was to be sinless, and we needed him to be sinless, didn't we? Because the great offer of Christianity, and if you are not a Christian this is what I need you to hear, the offer God extends to be received by faith in his Son, Jesus, is that Jesus would take your sin, all of your failure to obey the commands of God and to honor him with all the honor and glory he is due, and take it upon himself.

He would take it into the grave, rise from the grave and leave those things behind, so they stay dead while he rises victorious. And in its place he would impute to you, or give to you, his perfect righteousness, so that when you stand before the judgment seat, the deeds of Christ are read over you, because it's his record and not yours that you hold in your hands as you go before him. If he wasn't sinless, this is no good promise. He cannot make good on this offer. You need a perfectly spotless record, and Jesus claims to be able to give that to you.

But was he sinless? He must rise in order to vindicate his sinless life, to certify and prove that he was in fact sinless. I take this not only from Peter's sermon at Pentecost, where we read that "God raised him up, loosing the pangs of death, because it was not possible for him to be held by it." I take it also from Colossians 2:8. I'll read it to you.

Paul writes, "See to it that no one takes you captive by philosophy and empty deceit, according to human tradition, according to the elemental spirits of the world, and not according to Christ. For in him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily." There is no higher statement of perfection ever made about a person. The fullness of deity dwells bodily in him.

Verse 10: "and you have been filled in him, who is the head of all rule and authority. In him also you were circumcised with a circumcision made without hands, by putting off the body of the flesh, by the circumcision of Christ, having been buried with him in baptism, in which you were also raised with him through faith in the powerful working of God, who raised him from the dead." Listen: "And you, who were dead in your trespasses and the uncircumcision of your flesh, God made alive together with him, having forgiven us all our trespasses, by canceling the record of debt that stood against us with its legal demands. This he set aside, nailing it to the cross." Verse 15.

Listen: "He disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him."

Death had no claim on him, because there was no sin in him. It's as if Jesus says to death, you have no power to hold me. Your hook has no barb, Satan. There is no charge for you to bring against me. I go willingly, and only willingly, into this place, and I walk out when I please. As Paul writes to Timothy, "He was manifested in the flesh, vindicated by the Spirit, seen by angels, proclaimed among the nations, believed on in the world, taken up in glory."

To put your faith in one who has no power to offer you a perfect record of righteousness is to put your faith in someone who cannot save you. You need perfection if you are going to stand before the judgment seat of God. Christ alone has a spotless life, and his resurrection testifies to it. Luke was obsessed with this idea. In his account of the passion he says it five times: this man was innocent.

This man, there was no guilt found in him. This man was innocent. There was no guilt found in him. And his resurrection from the dead testifies that it was true. Number three: he must rise to prove that his death was effective to put our death to death. Death was powerful enough to kill him, and if he stays dead, death has won.

If he stays dead, we have no confidence, and we are of all people most to be pitied. In Romans 4:25 Paul wrote that Jesus "was delivered up for our trespasses." His death was sufficient, and the proof is that death can't hold him. So Paul finishes the doctrine with the back half of his sentence.

Jesus "was delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification." There is no justification before God without the resurrection. He must rise. Jesus died for you so that you might not die, and rose for you so that you might live.

Fourth, he must rise to unite us to himself. The reason we say it's by faith and not by works that we inherit eternal life is that by faith we are united with the one Lord Jesus. Our union with Christ is the central doctrine of Christianity: you and he are made one. The Holy Spirit indwells you, producing faith, so that by that faith you would be united with the one risen Christ. What good is it at all, church, for you to be united to a dead man? The point of our hope is that we would escape our death, and if we are united to the dead, then we have no hope at all. Our hope is to be united to him who lives.

This is what John saw in Revelation 1, when he was given a vision into heaven and saw Jesus. It says this: "When I saw him, I fell at his feet as though dead. But he laid his right hand on me, saying, 'Fear not, I am the first and the last, and the living one. I died, and behold I am alive forevermore, and I have the keys of Death and Hades.'" Amen? When you fall on your face as one dead before Christ, he puts his right hand on you, lifts your chin and says, behold, I died, but I live forevermore.

Stand, rise, live forevermore with me. And lastly, Jesus Christ had to rise to be our King and our delight. Listen. In the picture we sometimes paint of the soldier who jumps on the grenade, he dies so that we might go on living, but he's gone. That isn't the salvation we were promised.

We were promised that our deep need, our deep soul need, the very thing we were created for, would be met: that we would dwell bodily with our Maker, our Creator, our triune God, that we would walk face to face and reign in eternity in the kingdom with our Messiah, with the Christ. That is the promise. It's not enough to be welcomed onto golden roads and into a room in the Father's house and all that. To be without the Savior, without the Christ, is to be without the thing you were made for. To dwell bodily forever in a place without your Lord is no salvation at all. That is not what we were promised. We were made for him.

We were made for him. So, church, Christ had to rise to fulfill the promises of God; to prove his sinlessness, so that we can have confidence when he offers his righteousness to us; to prove that his death was effective to put our death to death; to unite us to himself, the living God; and to be our King forever. When we say, "He is risen! He is risen indeed!" we are declaring the highest truth of the Christian life.

We don't merely behold an empty tomb. We behold our risen Christ. He has gone before us. And if you do not know him, come to him today for the first time. Confess your sin to him, receive his payment for the penalty of your sin, and have his perfect, righteous life applied to you, the eternal life he lives bodily. Talk to me at any point after the service today.

I would love to work through all of that with you. But if this is your Lord, we must learn to behold him in his risen state. We must learn to worship the God who lives, the God who reigns, the God who evermore will be. It changes everything about how you engage this life. That's my only application for you today. If he died for you, was buried for you and rose for you, then there is an eternity ahead of you, which means everything you do in this life is of eternal consequence. You're not merely waiting out this life so that you can enter the next one.

This life you are living now will endure beyond the grave. All that you do in this life will count and matter in the kingdom economy of God. My theology on this changed. Maybe nobody else has ever thought this, and I had really bad theology, but I used to think that in heaven we wouldn't really know one another.

We wouldn't remember this life. How could we possibly be fully spared from all the pain and suffering of this life if we had to remember it? So I used to think we'd get that Men in Black memory wipe. We'd just forget it all, enter the kingdom and start a new life.

Life 2.0. But eternity is something you're already living in. You will never cease to exist. You will dwell bodily and forever in either heaven or hell, and this life will all be part of the story.

All of it. What I say to my wife is, I'm looking forward to those days in heaven when we'll look back on the days when we walked by faith and not by sight, and we'll say, I knew it. Or, remember when we doubted? Our worship will be perfected on account of the lives we live here.

It will change the way we behold the risen Christ when we see him face to face. This life matters, but this life is not all there is, and that should change the way we walk through each and every day. Let's pray.
