# The Light of the World (John 8:12-59)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 8:12-59

---

This is a long passage, nearly fifty verses, so I'm not going to preach it one sentence at a time as I normally do. Instead I want to walk through it with you by way of ten central statements Jesus makes as this back-and-forth with the Jews unfolds.

First, I want to give you a testimony. I grew up in a little New England town of about 2,500 people, and it's my heaven on earth. My mother's family has lived there for five generations, and I lived in the home where they grew up. In that little farmhouse, my uncle grew up in the bedroom that became mine, and his uncle grew up in it before him, and so on. What's interesting about living in a home with that kind of family history is that the fingerprints of the generations are all over it.

My sister's bedroom, for example, used to be my aunt's, and carved into the windowsill are things like "I love so-and-so," scratched out, then "I love so-and-so," scratched out. It's a shrine to every boy the girls in that room fell for in grade school, going back a hundred years. Once we went out in the woods and found the remains of a tree fort a family member had built around 1960, and we rebuilt it.

But my favorite discovery in that home was a loose floorboard in my bedroom. I slid my dresser over and found that a big old floorboard underneath was loose, and I wiggled it free. Underneath was a cache of forgotten treasure: a bag of marbles, some army men, a trophy, and a Bible. I wasn't raised Christian. It was a King James Version that must have seemed old already when it was given to my uncle, who stuck it under that floorboard.

To me it was ancient. I was maybe nine or ten. I took that Bible up to my top bunk, kept it under my pillow, and read it. Where I grew up, the religious scene was an earthy, ancestral spirituality. I believed in fairies. One Halloween I held a séance and tried to summon Babe Ruth. That was my upbringing.

This was the first thing I'd ever read in King James English. It wasn't a holy book to me, but it was a spiritual book. It was neat, it was old, and I'd found it in a hole in my floor. So I had about as high a view of that book as you can have without being born again.

But I want you to hear this: when I read it, nothing happened. It felt really cool to hold, but it might as well have been a book of incantations. It was just a book, and nothing happened when I read it. The reason is that my heart was a heart of stone.

I was also ten, and it was in King James English. But it was because my heart was a heart of stone. Years went by. At sixteen or seventeen we had moved away from that town, I could drive, and I took myself to a church. I was seeking something.

I started to hear the Bible preached, to spend time with Christians and to share life with them, and I was drawn to them because the way they lived and treated one another was distinctly different from the way my friends treated each other. I was drawn to Christians well before I knew Christ, the God who had made them the way they were. So I started calling myself a Christian to fit in with that group. But even sitting under the teaching of the Bible, nothing was happening. Nothing was getting into me.

The first time a girl wanted to kiss me, I traded in Christianity for that. But I'll never forget when Christ got hold of me. The first prayer I ever really prayed to the Lord was a shaken-fist prayer. The scales had come off my eyes. I had become aware that something was wrong with me, that I lived in darkness, that I was a fractured sinner.

On the outside people knew me as one thing, but I knew the things my mind could think. I spent several years of high school suicidal. In middle school, if I wasn't talking, I was thinking about death. I bullied my brother to feel tough, I guess. I wasn't a protector of my sisters.

I had hatred in my spirit toward my parents. And I remember cursing God in that prayer: whatever is wrong with me, God, surely it's your fault, because you made me, and supposedly you're all-powerful. So whatever I am is your problem, not mine. I just wrote him off.

But the Lord was doing something in that moment, because later that year he ransomed me from death to life and made me aware that he was making me his child. When he did, I bought a Bible and brought it to work. I was working overnights, and in about three months I read my Bible cover to cover. For the first time it came alive to me. Every word was food.

Every word was spiritual nourishment, and for the first time it did its work on me. And here's a testimony from just this week. As I read this passage to get ready to preach it, I was as fired up as if I were watching an action film, because every word is true, and this is my Jesus.

That's entirely different from the nine- or ten-year-old boy with his mystical book in his top bunk. It's entirely different from putting on appearances and reciting the words to look like something. The words have come alive. The truth is, I was a glory-seeking liar who walked in darkness, who put myself on the judgment seat, who was deaf to the words of God and dead in my sin, until something happened.

That's what Jesus talks about in this passage. You've heard it read. To save time, I want you to follow ten statements Jesus makes in it, so that you can trace the line of what he's teaching, see how it applies to the testimony I've just given you, and see how it describes your testimony too, if you are found in Christ.

The first major claim Jesus makes is to be the light of the world. Verse 12: "I am the light of the world. Whoever follows me will not walk in darkness, but will have the light of life." The second follows soon after, in verse 19: "If you knew me, you would know my Father also."

We've already seen a great deal of the light of the world in this Gospel. John has been helping us see a very high view of God: the one who came before all things, who shone light into the darkness, who gives life in the creation story and in our renewal. But here Jesus speaks of a light of the world that rescues. The light in the beginning, when God said, "Let there be light," before sin, is a little different from the light of the world that comes after sin. Because once darkness is inhabited by sinners, light exposes sin.

It exposes darkness. What was hidden is revealed. He says that whoever follows him will not walk in darkness but will have the light of what? Of life. So the first invitation I need to make as we look at these ten statements is this: whatever you're keeping in the darkness is pulling you toward death.

Darkness and death go hand in hand. I was telling a friend last night that I could be content if this year of ministry were about nothing but fostering two things: a deep dependence on prayer, our vertical relationship with God, and confession and repentance. What if we, as a church, dragged out of the darkness the things we don't really believe the gospel is big enough for, that Jesus is big enough for, that his grace and mercy are big enough to cover, that his blood is sufficient to cover? What if we could put our finger on the things we won't say among one another? Recently I opened a gathering of our church's leaders by saying, I don't know what to ask, so here's the question.

What's the thing that, if nobody asked you, you'd never volunteer? Can we talk about that? And man, did we. That's what I want for us this year. Not a moment in time.

I want a culture of taking the things that are in the darkness and dragging them into the light, in faith that the light slays the darkness and brings dead things to life. Jesus claims to be that light, and he says those who follow him will have the light of life. So first, I know you're hiding sin. I know you are, because I am.

All of us struggle to believe the gospel reaches every corner of our sin. We say, I didn't do anything. But you are sinful. Unless I'm uniquely broken, I know your own mind betrays you and your own flesh betrays you. You judge yourself by your best intentions and everyone else by their actions. You excuse your sin: that's not who I really am.

I didn't mean it. It's really because of something that happened to me, not something I did. That's walking in darkness: making friends with your sin, making excuses for it, buddying up to it, trying to tame it and keep it in the room with you. But rather than walking in darkness, Jesus says, if you follow him you will have the light of life. And he says, if you knew me, you would know my Father also.

I won't press this one too hard, because it seems he makes this claim every week, and so you need to hear it every week. Jesus is God. Jesus is God. Jesus and the Father are one.

Again and again Jesus has said it: I and the Father are one. To know me is to know the Father. To know the Father is to know me. We are one.

If you knew me, you would know him. So Jesus is making a high claim: to walk with him is to have the light of the world, the light of life. And that's because he and the Father are one. To know him is to know God, and God is the Father of lights, from whom every good gift comes.

The third statement is that his judgment is true, and that it is not he alone who judges, but he and the Father who sent him. Verses 15 and 16: "You judge according to the flesh; I judge no one. Yet even if I do judge, my judgment is true, for it is not I alone who judge, but I and the Father who sent me." This is a theme all through John. Jesus has said that "the Father judges no one, but has given all judgment to the Son," and then he defers that authority back to the Father. Listen, even though I have the authority to judge, I defer to the Father. My judgments are as he would judge.

And the Father defers to me. His judgments are as I would judge. Jesus is claiming unity, oneness, with the Father in his judgments, and claiming that his judgment is true. If Jesus has the authority to judge, if Jesus and the Father are one, if Jesus is God and God is judge and his judgment is true, then when he looks at you and says you're living in darkness, you can trust that he's right. When he calls something evil, when he calls something sin, when he goes so far as to tell these men, "You are of your father the devil," his judgment is true.

It's not a mere suggestion. He's talking about the difference between death and life, and he gets to define it. His judgment is true, and for his judgment to fall on us is a horrifying thought if we're standing on our own merit, because in this sparring with the Jews he has gone to great lengths to show them that their righteousness pales beside what they actually need to stand upright before him.

So the first thing the church needs to remember and hear is that we walked in darkness, marked by our sin. We desperately needed light to invade that darkness, and we couldn't drag ourselves into the light. The light had to come for us. And it came in the Son of God, Jesus Christ, one with the Father, so that he might pour out his judgment on the earth. So what is his judgment? The fourth statement, verse 24: "Unless you believe that I am he you will die in your sins."

Unless you believe that I am he, you will die in your sins. I think we want to soften this, because it's so hard to look at and believe. We don't even really want to believe we're in sin, let alone that we could die in it. But what Jesus is saying is that to be brought out of death in your sins into new life, into the light of life, you need only believe that he is he. So my question for the church is: who do we believe he is?

Who do you believe Jesus is, as he relates to you? Is he your advice-giver? A great prophet? A great teacher? A good man?

Is he God? Is he the judge? He has declared, and claimed the right to declare, that he is the judge, and he has judged that we will die in our sins unless we believe that he is who he says he is, the Son of Man, one with the Father. But it isn't enough simply to believe that, because Satan knows who Jesus is. He can describe Christ correctly.

So we're certainly not talking about mere academic assent to theological truths. We're talking about a knowing that sounds like tasting and seeing, a relational indwelling. Because in the fifth statement, verse 28, Jesus says, "When you have lifted up the Son of Man, then you will know that I am he." By "lifted up" he means crucified. Unless you believe that I am he, you will die in your sins.

And when you have crucified me, you will know that I am he. Jesus knows that the very people he's talking to are going to crucify him. When you have crucified me, he says, you will know it was me. And it literally happens. As soon as he breathes his last, there's the first convert after the crucifixion. The centurion standing in front of the cross says, "Truly this man was the Son of God!"

One minute too late, you'd think. Unless he can rise from the dead. And if he can rise from the dead, maybe he can raise you from the dead.

I think some of this can feel like basic Christian doctrine to us, until you actually look at your life. Then maybe it isn't so basic. We've been walking together. The reason I care so much about believers really knowing one another is this.

The most common thing we see in our relationships is that we fail to believe the gospel in the actual corners of our lives, which is why we hit on this every single week. We don't really believe it. How do we know whether we're believing it? When we sin, where do we run?

If we run to God, that's evidence that we believe the gospel. If we run away from him, we're believing a false gospel of righteousness by good works, or by hiding. When we run to darkness when we fail, when we run to darkness when we sin, we're choosing to keep living apart from the Son, apart from the light of life. But he says, if you knew me, you would know my Father.

He has judged that you will die in your sins unless you believe that he is he. And if you believe that he is he, you'll know that he is the one who has the light of life. Sometimes I think we see him lifted up, crucified, giving up his life and rising again, and that helps us agree that he's God, but we still struggle to believe the sixth statement, verses 31 and 32: "If you abide in my word, you are truly my disciples, and you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free." I see two kinds of slavery within the church.

I know we'll see it in the world. That's why we need to go to our unreached neighbors with the gospel: we see people who have never heard it, who don't know Jesus, who are truly enslaved, either to the good works of a false righteousness or to guilt, shame and condemnation for all the things they know they aren't. We want to give them this message of life. But it doesn't only exist out there. It exists right in here.

As I walk with you, I know you're like me. We keep going back to this heart posture, this spiritual low of guilt, shame and condemnation, which we then try to medicate with works of righteousness and good deeds. We try to clean ourselves up to be better than we are, or at least to pretend we're better than we are, and we go into hiding. We act as if this isn't a test of whether we're believing the gospel, and we don't go deeper with it ourselves before we presume to bring it to someone else. The world doesn't need the message we carry to them if we aren't believing it ourselves. It starts here. This isn't an indictment of one church. I'm talking about the church everywhere, in every age.

The work of every church is to believe the gospel more fully together before we pretend to carry it out in its fullness to other people. We're to believe it for ourselves, together, not once for all as if we've arrived, but daily, in relationship, with one another. I can tell whether you're believing the gospel by whether you'll volunteer the ugly things to me.

If you're talking about the things you hate most about yourself, the things you're most ashamed of, if you're confessing the things that really plague your spirit, the things you wish you could be washed white as snow from, I know you're doing it because you believe you have been. But if you don't believe you've been thoroughly washed by the blood of Christ, that before your Father you stand purified by his life, death and resurrection, then you have to hide. You have to lie, and so you will. So it isn't enough just to show up among other believers. If you've been gathering with other believers for a year or two and no one really knows you, and you seem like someone who has it all together and no real need for Christ, you're likely not believing the gospel.

I know you're not, because I know what it's like in that place. I can go years without talking about the things I really need to talk about, if you let me. So don't let me. But Jesus promises, if you abide in my word, you are truly my disciples, and you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free.

The seventh statement, verse 47: "Whoever is of God hears the words of God." This is where I was going with my opening story. If you're of God, you'll hear his words. If you're not of God, you won't. And that bears on evangelism, on bringing the gospel to our neighbors.

Generally speaking, I'm not an apologetics guy. I know apologetics has its place. But people can't reason their way into the truths of the gospel by academic assent alone.

These words jump off the page with joy as the Holy Spirit communes with your spirit when you're brought from death to life. Agreeing with the truth because of a good argument isn't the same thing as spiritual regeneration, taking a heart of stone and making it a heart of flesh. That doesn't happen through apologetics so much as through the Holy Spirit entering the picture and bringing dead people to life.

And that happens when we preach the gospel. I think a lot of times what cripples the church in bringing this good news to others is that we don't know how to present it so that we can win a sparring match with people who might have good challenges to it. But the truth is, it starts with the fact that we don't really believe it for ourselves. When we do, sharing it is just the natural overflow of talking about what we've tasted and seen. That's just what we do.

You don't plan ahead what you're going to say about the great steak you had last night. You just start talking about it because it was a great steak, and you know it was great because you tasted it. You want other people to taste it too, so you offer it to them.

You should try this. And they say, that looks terrible, and you're crazy. And you say, more for me. You don't go online and look up all the ingredients so you can list them off and build a compelling case.

You share what you've tasted and found satisfying. And if someone is of God, he will hear those words. And if you abide in those words, you are truly his disciples, and you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free. Of course, they challenge him on this. "We are offspring of Abraham and have never been enslaved to anyone."

We don't know what you're talking about. And Jesus answers, "The slave does not remain in the house forever; the son remains forever. So if the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed." That's our great assurance. Your sin, which clings so closely, you have already been forgiven in Christ. The Father looks at you today and declares you spotless, because payment has been made on your behalf in Christ Jesus. That's true.

But it feels like I'm lying to you, because you say, yes, but I still sin. I thought freedom would mean I don't sin anymore. You can't call something true when I know it isn't.

But that objection is pride. Do you know what makes something true? That God says it's true. He doesn't require that you understand it or agree with it first. When he says something is true, it's true. It's done. He is truth.

He is light. When he speaks, life springs up. Everything was created by the power of his word. When he says something is, it is. And when he says something isn't, it isn't.

So when he says something and you say, I don't see it, the problem is with you, not with him. From his vantage point it's already done, and his vantage point is the only one that matters. Of course the question is, will I ever shed my sin for good experientially, so that it isn't only true from his eternal perspective, but I experience it as true? And the answer is yes.

Yes. Even now, in a given moment, the Spirit can have the victory in you. Once you were a slave to your sinful nature and could do nothing pleasing to God, because you were utterly dead in the trespasses in which you once walked. But the Spirit alive in you is able, in any given moment on this side of eternity, to make you obedient to God, because his Spirit within you only ever wants to do the will of the Father. That means that in a given moment, you can say no to sin and you can honor Christ. That's just true.

We often think of sanctification, becoming conformed to the image of Christ, like a graph with time along the bottom and holiness up the side, and a line climbing steadily upward. The longer I walk with the Lord, the more holy I become, and that's sanctification.

And that's just not true, because I know you. And you know me. Sanctification isn't a function of time. In any given moment, walk in the Spirit or you walk in the flesh. So the line jumps up and down: in one moment the Holy Spirit, through you, glorifies the Father and the Son and does exactly what he wills. The good news is that the Son gets his glory even when, rather than honoring him, you instead demonstrate your need for his grace, mercy, and forgiveness.

This is what got me so fired up as I prepared. The fact that I read this and it does anything in me is my proof that the Spirit bears witness with my spirit that I am a child of God. The fact that these words mean anything to you is proof that you're walking as a child, not as an outsider walking in darkness. When you read it and then do it, that's the Spirit bringing the goodness of God into the world. That's him accomplishing his good purposes in creation through you, a broken vessel.

And then sometimes you sin. Until one day, at Christ's return or when you go to be with him, the line levels out at perfect holiness, and you shed your sin like a snake sheds its skin, never again to know suffering or mourning or death or sin of any kind. That's the big claim in the eighth statement, verse 51: "If anyone keeps my word, he will never see death." Of course they're outraged, because Abraham died.

The prophets died. "Who do you make yourself out to be?" God. He's claiming to be God. He will never see death.

But Jesus has already told us that we are walking in darkness and death. When he says we will die in our sins if we don't believe in him, he means that you are already dead in your sins, and you will die in that state. It's not that you're on the scales and the verdict comes at death: sinner, not sinner. You are a sinner. That's already true. And as a sinner, you are dead even while you live in your sin, until something happens for you.

The ninth statement, verse 50: "Yet I do not seek my own glory; there is One who seeks it, and he is the judge." This was and is central to my understanding of what it means to walk with Jesus, and it's something I want us to believe as a church: everything the Father is doing in his church, everything he's doing in you, is for the glory of the Son. What your Father in heaven is about is glorifying Jesus.

Do you know how you can be sure he'll have the victory in you? Because God the Father will not withhold any glory from Jesus. He does it for your joy too. The drumbeat of my life is this: we exist for the glory of our King and the joy of his people. For the glory of our King and the joy of his people.

But we focus on the joy more than the glory, and we find we have less joy, don't we? Because joy flows out of glorifying Christ. The most selfish thing you can do in the pursuit of your joy is to seek to glorify Jesus. That's the path to joy. And Jesus says he isn't seeking his own glory.

He makes so little defense of himself. He says, there is One who seeks it, and he is the judge. I'm so grateful, and you should be too, that God the Father, the judge, seeks the glory of the Son. Because it's out of his seeking the Son's glory that he pours out the Holy Spirit on a church, that he offers the Son a remnant, a people, ransomed from darkness, from sin and death, from the power of sin's slavery in your life.

The reason you were plucked out and given to the Son is so that the Father could glorify him. You are the Father's love offering to his Son. And the Father gives only the most precious and beautiful things to his Son. He will purify you. He will conform you to his image.

He will root sin out of every corner of your heart, moment by moment as you walk with him, and out of the world on that final day. Finally, the tenth statement: "Before Abraham was, I am." I want to read this one straight from the text, starting at verse 52: "The Jews said to him, 'Now we know that you have a demon! Abraham died, as did the prophets, yet you say, "If anyone keeps my word, he will never taste death." Are you greater than our father Abraham, who died? And the prophets died! Who do you make yourself out to be?' Jesus answered, 'If I glorify myself, my glory is nothing. It is my Father who glorifies me, of whom you say, "He is our God." But you have not known him. I know him. If I were to say that I do not know him, I would be a liar like you, but I do know him and I keep his word. Your father Abraham rejoiced that he would see my day. He saw it and was glad.' So the Jews said to him, 'You are not yet fifty years old, and have you seen Abraham?' Jesus said to them, 'Truly, truly, I say to you, before Abraham was, I am.' So they picked up stones to throw at him, but Jesus hid himself and went out of the temple."

In this passage they've held Jesus up against who they need him to be, who they expect him to be, and our culture does the same. Surely he can't be greater than Abraham. Surely he can't be greater than the prophets. At our best, in our sin, we'll let him stand on equal footing with them. But he can't be I AM.

He says Abraham rejoiced that he would see his day, and he saw it and was glad. Jesus is telling us how Abraham responded when he saw him. When Abraham died, he didn't die.

When Abraham died in this life, by the faith that was counted to him as righteousness he beheld the glory of the Son he had rejoiced to one day see. Jesus is saying, let me tell you about the time I met Abraham. He rejoiced that he would see my day, and he saw it and was glad. And they say, when did you meet Abraham? You, a man in his thirties?

Before Abraham was, I am. "I am" is the very name of God that he gave Moses at the burning bush. When Moses asked his name, God said, "I AM WHO I AM." And if you wonder whether that's what Jesus meant, notice that they picked up stones to throw at him.

So many words are spoken in this passage, and they all say the same thing. He's God. We're not. He's good. We're not.

He's light. We're darkness. He's justice. We're injustice. He's life.

We're death. That's the drumbeat of Jesus' words here. And to the very people who will crucify him, he says, when you have lifted me up, you will know. And if you believe, this is the way out. This is the door, and it stands open.

In this time before his return, the invitation still stands: to come in, to come into the light and let it pierce the darkness, to forsake and renounce your sin, to repent and confess, to walk with Jesus so that we no longer walk in darkness, to receive forgiveness for our sin so that we might walk with him as sons, perfected, so that our ears might be opened to his word, and so that we might share it with others. This is the chief end of the church. It's why we're not with him yet. We're still here, like the man Jesus freed from the demons in the region of the Gerasenes.

Jesus casts out his demons and saves him, and the man begs to go with him. And Jesus says no: "Go home to your friends and tell them how much the Lord has done for you, and how he has had mercy on you." He's still doing it.

That's the great hope. We're still here because he's still doing it. He's not done saving. He wants to bring more dead people back to life. He wants to bring more people who are living in darkness into the light. And he's not done with you either.

So we're still here, that we might walk with him more fully and invite others to the party. As I close, I want to call us to respond. I could make a hundred charges to myself and to you from statements like these, but I'll stick to the one that burns in my heart, keeps me up at night and fills my prayers when I look at the future of our church. If we get nothing else from what Jesus says here, let it be this: he is good, and you can bring your stuff to him. Drag it out and put it in front of him, because he shines light on it, puts death to death and brings us into new life.

So, two charges. Believe that more fully for yourself. And believe it with one another, and the rest will follow. Would you pray for that with me, for me and for us?
