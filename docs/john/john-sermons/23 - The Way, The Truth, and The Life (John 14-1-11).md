# The Way, The Truth, and The Life (John 14:1-11)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 14:1-11

---

This opening sentence will be our compass for the whole morning, so let's look at it together quickly: "Let not your hearts be troubled." Let not your hearts be troubled.

This sentence, spoken by our Lord Jesus to his closest friends, is the heart, the posture, from which the rest of our text this morning flows. Jesus Christ in the flesh looks at his beloved and speaks over them: let not your hearts be troubled. Then he says a great deal more toward the end of achieving what he's commanding. He wants to show compassion and tender care, and to encourage troubled hearts, with everything we're going to read this morning. So to get the most out of time spent in this conversation with Jesus, I think we need to bring him our troubled hearts and let him speak these truths over us, the truths he considered fit to speak to the troubled hearts of his dearest friends. Let these words wash over us.

I'll confess that yesterday afternoon I conducted a funeral, and it was a hard one. As a pastor, there are funerals you almost look forward to: the hundred-year-old woman who dies in her rocking chair with a Bible in her lap. We don't get many of those.

This one was especially hard. The woman who died was young, in her twenties, and her life had been very hard. She grew up faster than she should have had to, and her life ended in an overdose. A funeral like that draws a certain kind of crowd. The room yesterday, as I preached, was really two rooms: those who had run with her in her addiction, and those who desperately loved her and longed to see her pulled out of it, and in the end didn't get to. I talked with a friend who planted a church in rural North Carolina and does about twenty opioid-overdose funerals a year for people connected to his church.

I called him and said, help me, man. I've never done this before. He said, preach to the believers first. Remind them of their hope in Christ. Then turn to those who could be next and make sure they hear the gospel. So I did.

I spent twenty-two minutes or so preaching the gospel to a room of addicts, and I watched the Lord doing something in a handful of them that I pray they'll carry home, and that he'll keep working there. I'm praying. I bring it up because I'd been preparing this sermon for today, and this text ended up at the center of my sermon yesterday. So I'm preaching it again, to a different room, for a different reason. But if this text was enough to comfort that room yesterday, it's enough to comfort you today.

Maybe the difference between the two rooms is that in that room there was no denying the brokenness. The tears were already flowing. Self-awareness was high. There was no work to do up front to bring someone to the awareness that they need hope and encouragement before speaking hope and encouragement over them. But some of us came in here this morning hardened.

Maybe you don't feel you need encouragement this morning, so you won't be able to receive it from me, because you think it's for someone else. I want you to hear it squarely up front: I'm preaching to you. And my prayer is that as we work through how Jesus works this encouragement into the hearts of his beloved, you'll see how it's for you. He starts with "Let not your hearts be troubled." Why does he say this?

We've been working straight through this book for some time, so we know why. Go back. He has just said Peter will deny him three times. He has just said one of the twelve will betray him, without telling them who.

In the chapters before, a plot is moving forward among the Jewish leaders to kill Jesus after he raises Lazarus. And he has told them he means to die, and how: by crucifixion. So if you're in Jesus' inner circle, you've walked with him for three years, you've staked everything on him, and this week, on this day, you're hearing that one of us will betray you, Peter will deny you, and you're going to be crucified? Talking about crucifixion, Jesus? Your heart is going to be troubled.

He's not talking about general anxiety or agitation. He's talking about a very rational fear that sizes up the situation, measures our ability in the flesh, sees it doesn't stack up, and rightly feels an agitated, troubled heart. At the funeral yesterday we talked about the brokenness of the world, and what I hope you'll see here is that we're talking about evil advancing on the earth: evil in your own life, evil around you, evil in the news. The disciples were seeing evil advance with their own eyes: persecution heating up, people hunting Jesus to kill him.

And it had made its way into their inner circle. One of them would be the betrayer. So it wasn't evil out there; it was evil right here. Peter would deny him. This is very personal.

In the same way, you look at the world and at your life and see evil advancing in different corners of it. New forms of evil taking root in the culture. New kinds of sin cropping up in your family. New stories of brokenness and hurt, until we're asking, Lord, is the plan still good?

Is it still in play? Are you still in control? Are you still the Messiah? Are you still King? Are you still doing all you promised?

Have we got this wrong? And Jesus says, let not your hearts be troubled, in light of all I've just said: Peter denying me, Judas betraying me, my being crucified.

Let not your hearts be troubled. Why? "Believe in God; believe also in me." We use this word "believe," believing in God, believing in Jesus, all the time in Christian circles.

I believe in Jesus. But I don't mean, and Jesus doesn't mean, believing in Jesus the way a child believes in Santa Claus, or as if something becomes true because you believe in it. We're talking about something so true that it's worthy of your belief. It's a belief that's been earned. Think of a game of tug-of-war. You look at the two sides pulling, size them up, and decide which one is bigger, has the better grip, the better stance.

I believe in that guy, because every sign tells me he's the one to bet on. But this is a belief that goes one step further. It puts Jesus on one side of the rope and everything else on the other and says, believe in me. It doesn't look at your circumstances to decide how strong your faith in him can be right now. It looks to him as the way, the truth and the life, which we'll get to. Because every day of the Christian walk, all of us are inclined to do one of two things.

We're inclined either to look at our circumstances and let them tell us who God is and what he's doing or not doing, or to look at God and let him tell us who he is and what he's doing or not doing. Either we look to God to make sense of our circumstances, or we look to our circumstances to make sense of God. And Jesus calls his people here: let not your hearts be troubled. Believe in me.

Believe in God; believe also in me. Stake everything on me. Pin your hope on me. Believe in me.

I can do it. I've got this. Believe in me. I won't fail you.

Verse 2: "In my Father's house are many rooms. If it were not so, would I have told you that I go to prepare a place for you?" So now he builds on this "believe in me." Believe in me for what? For your seat at the table.

For your room in my Father's house. Remember the parable Jesus told: the kingdom of heaven is like treasure hidden in a field, which a man found and covered up. Then in his joy he goes and sells all that he has and buys that field, because he knows what's there. He counts everything else as loss for the surpassing worth of that treasure: the kingdom of God, that seat at the table, that room in the Father's house. Jesus is saying, all these things agitating your heart, everything that troubles you when you look at the world and see brokenness and darkness and evil and death: count it all as loss.

Believe in me. He's going through a cross, into a grave, and to the right hand of the Father, where he reigns forever. We're talking about a Jesus who didn't just say, wait here while I go over there and work on it. Jesus left that place, came here, passed through all the things we fear, and then entered into the thing we want most, so that we could join him in it. This matters, because if he identifies with us in our weakness, we can identify with him in his power.

"In my Father's house are many rooms." I want you to picture them as vacant rooms. Vacant rooms in the Father's house. Picture a palace, a beautiful place.

The Father's house, with many rooms. As we get to the famous line in a moment about Jesus being the way, we're talking about Jesus as a door, a key, a passage, everything needed to take up residence in the Father's house. Many rooms.

And he says not only that he goes to prepare it, but that he is the one who prepares it. That's my first major point this morning: when Jesus says that he goes to prepare a place for you, it means you don't prepare the place for yourself. In bringing peace to his people, let not your hearts be troubled, Jesus is going to speak directly to a series of doubts and fears and insecurities, and to great desires we're afraid won't be fulfilled. The first one he speaks to is: there's a place for you. There's room for you. Number one.

Number two: I go to prepare it for you. I don't know which of these speaks to which of you this morning, so you'll tell me with eye contact. But the first agitation is this: is there a room for me at all? Is there space for me?

This one speaks to my soul. I'll be careful, because I want to honor my father. But my parents split when I was young. My dad was a local delivery driver, and on his own he couldn't provide what they had been able to provide as a two-income family. So when they split, both homes fell into poverty. My father had less of a support system, and when we went to stay with him, he had moved into a one-bedroom apartment in a run-down neighborhood. My three siblings and I would all crash in sleeping bags on the living-room floor.

Even then, as a kid, I understood poverty well enough to know this wasn't a sign that my father didn't care. But at some level it told my child's mind that my father wasn't able to make room for me. With the family fractured, my parents couldn't make room for me, and in some way we weren't okay. There was no safety, no security, no assurance of a place to lay your head.

It took some time for that to change, so it was a real thing for me. How much more when some of us think about the Father's house? Some of us wouldn't say God isn't willing, but maybe he isn't able. Maybe God can't make room for me, either because I'm too far gone, once I take a really good look at my sin,

or because he's simply powerless, a God unable to do what he wills. Or maybe he doesn't know. We start ascribing all sorts of attributes to God that plant the seed of doubt in us that there's room for us at this table, that he can make room for us, that he has made room for us. So Jesus leads with it.

In my Father's house there is room. That's the first thing, full stop. "If it were not so, would I have told you that I go to prepare a place for you?" That's the second thing he appeals to: you are not responsible for preparing your place in that house.

A good friend of mine used to give an illustration that I use a lot now, because I think of it all the time. He says sometimes we Christians are like passengers on a plane to Hawaii who, halfway through the flight, forget we're going somewhere and start believing the plane is our home. So we start jockeying for more legroom and fighting over extra packets of peanuts.

We start behaving as if we've arrived, this is home now, and I've got to make a place for myself. I've got to prepare this spot for me. You can see it in a lot of behaviors if you think about your life: the way we try to expand our footprint. I was literally taught this in the business world.

In my former career I sat at some negotiating tables, and I was told that when you sit down at a table in a business meeting, you should take up as much physical space as possible to assert dominance over the people you're negotiating with. We do that with our lives. We expand our footprint and our influence, try to win people's approval, try to gain the stuff of the earth. Whether it's through creature comforts or, what's the word, materialism and vanity, we surround ourselves with comfort because this, ultimately, is my home.

Either I don't think I have a room in the Father's house, or I don't think it will be that great. I won't like the way it was prepared for me. So I'll take this instead, which is to prefer slop to a banquet. The place prepared for us in the Father's house has been prepared by the hand of Jesus, so we don't need to prepare it for ourselves. This only occurred to me this morning, so it isn't a well-thought-out point.

If it starts a debate, that's okay; I'm happy to talk about it more. But I want to say it anyway. I realized that if we do this in our immediate sphere, it will carry over to a grander scale. We start thinking not only about how to prepare a place for ourselves in our home and our immediate surroundings, but about the world.

We start saying, this is all there is, so it's of ultimate importance to make the world a more comfortable home for us. It starts in your town, then your nation, then the globe. If everybody would just see things the way I see them, or do things the way I'd do them, I'd be more comfortable.

I'm not veering into end-times theology here. Some of you may hold a view that says it's the church's responsibility to roll out the red carpet for Jesus' return, that we're supposed to remake this earth to better reflect heaven. There's room for you at my table, though I think you're wrong. But in conversation, I don't often find that view is what drives the urge to improve things in my town, my nation and my world.

What I find instead is a view that appeals to my default position, which is anxiety about the way things are, the state of the earth. Things are broken, and we don't like it. Now, are we called to do good here? Yes, we are called.

We're called to fix our eyes on the ways of the Lord, walk in those ways on earth, and pray that he would use that activity to create little pockets that point to heaven. That's very much true. But I don't find that this is often the heart posture behind all the busy activity we see. It isn't, I've fixed my eyes on the Lord and been so comforted that I want to bring that comfort to others. It's, I'm terrified and angry, and I want to strong-arm people into making spaces I enjoy living in.

And Jesus says, let not your hearts be troubled. Believe in God; believe also in me. Those of us who function the way I've just described are very much like the original disciples. They wanted to see Jesus raised up on an earthly throne. They wanted this side of heaven simply made better.

They would have settled for a Messiah who gave them a more comfortable existence, but he was going further than that. Verses 3 to 6: "And if I go and prepare a place for you, I will come again and will take you to myself, that where I am you may be also. And you know the way to where I am going." Thomas said to him, "Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way?" Jesus said to him, "I am the way, and the truth, and the life."

"No one comes to the Father except through me." Here's another illustration: picture the Father's house on Google Maps. Jesus is saying, if you're anxious that there may not be a place, I assure you there's a place. It's called My Father's House, Nevada, and I built it.

So you don't have to worry about preparing it. That's great, Jesus, but I don't know the way. Jesus says, I'll show you the way. I am the way. Then the next anxiety follows: okay, there's a place, there's room, I have a map, but I don't have a car.

I can't get myself there. And he goes one step further: "I will come again and will take you to myself." We see Jesus giving layer upon layer of clarity. The Father's house: my construction. Your place in it: I've prepared it. The way to it: through me. The vehicle: I'll get you there. I'll literally carry you there.

Some of us are right there. We believe all of it right up to the point where he says he'll come and get us. We see it. We believe it. We want it.

We'll even take the map. But we're printing out the directions, and he's saying, I'll call you a ride. I'll take you there. We picture a Jesus who, when he says follow me, is like a Kenyan marathon runner we're struggling to keep up with, constantly terrified we'll lose him in the distance and have to figure it out from there. But he means follow me the way railcars follow a freight-train engine.

"I am the vine; you are the branches." We follow him, but it's more "hold on" than "keep up." Lord, we do not know where you are going. How can we know the way? "Jesus said to him, 'I am the way, and the truth, and the life.'"

"No one comes to the Father except through me. If you had known me, you would have known my Father also. From now on you do know him and have seen him." This brings me back to the funeral yesterday. I said to that room of addicts, if this is all there is, if we get really honest about how broken it all is and this is all there is, then I understand why opiates make sense to you. You're making a rational decision.

I want out. I want better. I want higher. I want out. If this is all there is, if we're making our own way, if the airplane is the destination and the peanuts are running out, despair makes sense. But my appeal to that room was this: if there is more, if there is better, if there is One, then you owe it to yourself to find out. And I simply invited them to come and behold him with me.

He says, "If you had known me, you would have known my Father also. From now on you do know him and have seen him." Verses 8 and 9: "Philip said to him, 'Lord, show us the Father, and it is enough for us.' Jesus said to him, 'Have I been with you so long, and you still do not know me, Philip?'"

If you've been with us through this Gospel, there's no way you haven't gathered by now that Jesus is God, that Jesus and the Father are one. It's been the loudest message of John's account, and this is one of my favorite passages for saying it concisely. Show us the Father. How long have you been with me, and you still don't know me?

Jesus and the Father are one. He is the way. He is the truth. He is eternal life. Let me pause briefly on what it means that Jesus is the truth.

He means it in any number of ways that could each be a sermon, but the main way I want you to hear it this morning is that Jesus is the truth about God. Your mind and your enemy and the world will tell you all kinds of things about God, but Jesus is the truth about him. And sometimes, when you don't have a well-developed theology on a given matter, and you have plenty of those and I have plenty, the starting point is: does this sound like Jesus? Does this look like Jesus? It isn't foolproof, because Jesus is complex.

But when something new or strange reaches your ear, something that agitates or unsettles your heart, and people claim it's Christian, that it's godly, and you can't make it fit with who you know Jesus to be, it's worth going back to the Scriptures and exploring further. I'll go that far. He is the truth. If you've seen him, you've seen the Father.

What I love about John is that it's a very repetitive Gospel. Jesus had a repetitive ministry because he had a single message to spread, and John records these conversations. So I borrow from earlier sermons a lot, because he's building a case. Here Jesus is saying that he and the Father are one.

If you've seen me, you've seen the Father. He's urging us to let him refine our understanding of the Father, and remember, his audience was mostly Jewish. He wants to refine the understanding of the Father they had from the Law and the Old Testament, to clarify it. They were getting the heart of God wrong even as they observed the laws of God.

So Jesus came to clarify some things about the truth, above all. I bring it up to us, mostly non-Jewish, because I know one of the main ways we feel we have to earn our place in the Father's house is by obeying the law. It's a self-made righteousness: through my good works, my achievements, forsaking sin, feeding the poor and whatever else, I believe that one day I'll stand before God and give an account of my life and be let into the Father's house on the basis of what I've done. At the very least, I try to be better than someone else, so that if there's only one room left, he'll pick me.

It's a scarcity mindset and a works-based mindset, and it leads to the pit of hell. There are many rooms in the Father's house, room enough for you, on the basis of what? The way: the merits of Christ alone, the life lived for you, the death died for you, the resurrection that went before you, so that you might join him in his death and resurrection. And Jesus is reigning at the right hand of the Father today. He's on the throne.

He doesn't just speak truth like a professor. He is the truth. All things bow to him. All things yield to him. He has total authority over all things, including the fractured corners of your life.

So when your heart is troubled, he says, look at me. Behold me on my throne. Behold me, the risen Christ. Behold me, the one who could use a Roman cross to save the world. I know that if you could write the story, you'd leave out all the best parts.

You would. So would I. We'd write the story so that everything was cozy all the time, and God got no glory.

But in the story he has written, he goes down into the depths in order to carry us to the heights, and he is the only way. Verses 10 and 11: "Do you not believe that I am in the Father and the Father is in me? The words that I say to you I do not speak on my own authority, but the Father who dwells in me does his works. Believe me that I am in the Father and the Father is in me, or else believe on account of the works themselves." What works? His signs, and for us, looking back, Calvary.

He says, I go to prepare a place for you. You don't believe it? Look at the works. Look at the cross.

Look at the empty tomb. How often are these the things we set before ourselves in daily life when our hearts are troubled? Do we say "Jesus has got this" as a slogan? Or do we see King Jesus on the throne, risen from the dead, and say, Jesus has got this? Jesus says the answer for our troubled hearts is to look to him and understand that he and the Father are one, and that he has made a way.

I pray that as we've worked through this, the Lord used one of these, even just one, to put his finger on your tendency toward a troubled heart. Each of us, in certain seasons of life, struggles with doubts like these: Is there a place? Is there room for me in it? Is the way it's been prepared actually better than this life? How do I get there?

Can I get there? Will he bring me? And to every one of these anxieties Jesus says, look to the works, and let not your hearts be troubled. Let's pray.
