# The Bread of Life (John 6:22-59)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 6:22-59

---

We're continuing our long journey through the Gospel of John, reading the whole book in order. I try to break it up so that each week you can receive the passage on its own, but the truth is that you'll receive each passage in its fullness as you encounter the whole account from start to finish. I won't give too much recap, because that passage alone took a long time to read. But last week we saw Jesus perform an incredible miracle.

I've broken this section into three parts: the miracle itself, which we preached last week; the crowd's response to it, which we'll see this week; and next week, the disciples' response along with Jesus' teaching about it. First, though, we have to remember the miracle, because this teaching comes straight from Jesus' mouth to the crowd that experienced it. Last week we saw Jesus lead a crowd into a desolate place, take a spot up on a mountain and teach them. The need for food arose out there in the wilderness, and Jesus took a boy's sack lunch and multiplied bread and fish to feed the multitude. The disciples kept reaching into the basket, and every time there was more. Jesus showed himself to be a God of abundance and fed the multitudes.

Then he withdrew, and that night his disciples set out across the sea toward Capernaum. Jesus walked on the water while they rowed against the wind, got into the boat with them, and immediately they reached the other side. This morning the crowd is following him. It's the same group of people from last week. They've just eaten multiplied bread, and they're looking for Jesus because they want to stay with him, talk to him and ask him questions.

Last week I left some themes unpreached, because I wanted to let Jesus address them himself this week. Last week's passage ended in verse 15, where Jesus perceived that the crowd was about to take him by force to make him king, and so he withdrew by himself to be with the Father. The crowd wanted to grab him, so Jesus left, and now the crowd hunts him down on the other side of the sea. We need that context to understand his teaching today.

There are also themes we touched on briefly last week that the people themselves see this week, so let me set them up. The multiplication of the bread happened at Passover. That's how the passage opens: "Now the Passover, the feast of the Jews, was at hand." You know the Passover from Exodus. God's people, the Hebrews, were enslaved in Egypt, and God did a great work to free them. In the last plague he sent the destroyer through the land of Egypt to bring judgment on the people who held his people captive. The only way his people would be set free was to obey God, who told them to take a spotless lamb, kill it, eat it, and put its blood on the doorposts of their homes. When the destroyer came through and saw the blood of a substitute, he passed over that home. Then, as God led them out of Egypt, they came up against the Red Sea and couldn't escape on their own. So God did another miracle by the staff of Moses: he parted the Red Sea, and they walked through on dry ground. Then he led them for forty years through the wilderness while he prepared a place for them, a promised land where they could live and prosper. For forty years they followed the pillar of cloud and fire through the wilderness, and in the desert there were times they had nothing to eat. So God made manna fall from heaven to feed them.

Why does this matter in John? Because at Passover, while the people are looking back in remembrance on the sacrificial lamb that caused the destroyer to pass over them, on that meal, on walking through the Red Sea on dry ground, on being fed from heaven, Jesus reenacts all of it. He does it on purpose. He leads a crowd through the wilderness to a desolate place with no food and then makes food appear miraculously. Then he crosses the sea in a miraculous way, just as the people crossed the sea in a miraculous way. He is reenacting the Exodus to show himself to be the God of the Exodus. He's doing it specifically for the Jewish people, at Passover, on purpose. He set all of this up to create the context for what he says this morning. You'll probably want to spend more than these few minutes thinking through all the parallels Jesus went to such effort to create, to show the people that he is the very God of the Passover they were celebrating.

Then the teaching begins. Verses 22 to 25: "On the next day the crowd that remained on the other side of the sea saw that there had been only one boat there, and that Jesus had not entered the boat with his disciples, but that his disciples had gone away alone. Other boats from Tiberias came near the place where they had eaten the bread after the Lord had given thanks. So when the crowd saw that Jesus was not there, nor his disciples, they themselves got into the boats and went to Capernaum, seeking Jesus. When they found him on the other side of the sea, they said to him, 'Rabbi, when did you come here?'"

So here's the setup. The crowd saw there was only one boat. Jesus fed them all, then the disciples got on the only boat there was to cross to Capernaum, and the crowd saw that Jesus wasn't with them. They think, when the next boats come from Tiberias, we'll get on them and follow the disciples. Jesus wasn't with them, so he must be going on foot, walking around the shore. If we take these boats, we'll beat him there. That would be the natural conclusion. So when they arrive and find Jesus already there, they're amazed: "Rabbi, when did you come here?" They are getting a glimpse of a miracle the disciples, the inner circle, saw firsthand. They didn't see Jesus walk on the water. All they know is that he somehow beat them across the sea without ever getting into a boat. They're marveling, so they ask, Jesus, when did you get here?

Verse 26: "Jesus answered them, 'Truly, truly, I say to you, you are seeking me, not because you saw signs, but because you ate your fill of the loaves.'" The crowd ate its fill, crossed the sea, found Jesus in Capernaum, and now they're marveling: how did you do this, man? We saw the boat. This is so cool. Jesus won't be hyped up that way. Instead of answering their question, he says, you're not seeking me because you saw signs. You're seeking me because you ate your fill of the loaves.

When I read that sentence, the first major point of this morning's message came to me: there is a big difference between seeking Jesus as your treasure and seeking Jesus to get some lesser pleasure. Jesus is telling the crowd, you're not seeking me for my sake. It's not because you've seen who I am and are marveling at that. It's because you've seen what I can do and you're marveling at that. Remember, last week ended with them wanting to seize Jesus. Now he says to the crowd, you just want more bread.

My wife has been away for several days, and I've been solo-parenting, so forgive me if some of today's illustrations come from children's stories. Do you know the story of Rumpelstiltskin? This is what I picture. A king hears that a miller's daughter can spin straw into gold, so he locks her in a room full of straw and orders her to spin. He doesn't want her. He wants the gold. The crowd sees that Jesus can multiply loaves and fish and immediately thinks, this is useful. Let's grab hold of this man. They wanted to take him by force and make him king, not because they saw him as God but because he could multiply food, and if you can multiply food, you'd make a very useful king. They wanted to lock Jesus in the room and make him spin, to make him a king who represented them and could feed them forever. A man who can make bread would be very useful.

Remember too that they lived under Roman oppression. For a mob of five thousand to decide to seize a man and make him king was to invite the emperor's wrath. They did see that this man had power and could stand up for them if that happened. But they were putting their hope in this: if we make him our king, he'll do our bidding against the Romans. He'll feed us. He's powerful, so let's make him king. And Jesus says, you don't make me king. I am a king. That's where we ended last week, and this week he says it again: you're not seeking me because you saw the signs, but because you ate your fill of the loaves.

This morning we have to reckon with whether Jesus is speaking to us. When we seek Jesus, are we seeking him for the loaves, or for who he is? We all know the right answer, but I think we need to take a real accounting of our lives and how we relate to Jesus to give an honest and full answer. Do we seek Jesus for the loaves, because we ate our fill? There are a lot of benefits to Jesus. There's a lot to gain from associating with power, from being near a man who can work miracles.

I don't want to be petty, and I've pointed this out before, but it symbolizes a mindset in our culture. You know the signs we hang in our kitchens: "A little bit of coffee and a whole lot of Jesus." By itself it's fine. It's catchy. But it reflects a widespread mindset that Jesus is one more item on the list of things we need for comfort. As long as I've got coffee and Jesus, or bread and the one who makes the bread, I'm good. As long as I've got comfort and the one who can give me comfort when I'm uncomfortable, I'm good. If I need Jesus plus something, then what I really need Jesus for is to protect or provide the something else. We reduce Jesus to useful. We reduce him to the one who can give us what we really treasure.

We do it all the time, in many ways. We come to Jesus only after we've tried everything else to get what we really want, and even then we don't call on him to receive him. We call on him as a last-ditch effort to get the thing: God, I've tried everything to get this promotion, and I was passed over. Jesus, please give me this promotion. Our prayer life starts to look as if Jesus were subservient to our will. You're welcome to bring God the desires of your heart. I'm not saying you shouldn't. But hear what I am saying: we think his main function in our lives is to hear our will and carry it out, that he's useful because he has the power to give us what we can't get by our own will. And Jesus says, I won't be approached that way. You are seeking me, not because you saw signs, but because you ate your fill of the loaves.

Regarding signs, he has already rebuked crowds in this Gospel for pursuing him because of them. Some came to him because they saw the signs, and he said, you're only following me because of the signs. Now he says to these people, you're not even coming for the signs; you're coming because you had your fill of the loaves. So we don't want to read this as, come to him for the signs instead. But he goes on to say that the signs do serve a purpose.

Verse 27: "Do not work for the food that perishes, but for the food that endures to eternal life, which the Son of Man will give to you. For on him God the Father has set his seal." We should hear "God the Father has set his seal" as a stamp of approval. Jesus is saying, my signs signify and certify that I am the one sent from God, that I am the Messiah. When the Father sets his seal on me, all these signs and wonders and miracles serve one chief purpose: to testify to who I am. I am the one the Lord has sent into the world. I am the Savior. I am the Messiah.

So hear this: when Jesus talks about the Father's seal on him, he gives us a framework for all our favorite things about him. When we love the stories where he shows himself to be the provider who multiplies the loaves, when we see him heal, raise the dead, give sight to the blind and hearing to the deaf, when we see any of his signs and wonders, they all serve a purpose. They are the Father's seal on him, the stamp that says this is in fact the Son of God. It's tragic if we marvel at the wonderful things about Jesus and never trace the line back to the one doing them, to what they mean and what they tell us about him.

A smaller point: notice how flat it reads when you change the central character from Jesus to the people who received his good works. Verse 23 says the boats from Tiberias came near "the place where they had eaten the bread." Last week it was the place where Jesus multiplied the bread. Now it's the place where they ate bread, and what made the bread miraculous was that they ate it in a place where there was no bread to eat. When we tell the story of Jesus' work with ourselves as the main character, it becomes "I ate," not "Jesus multiplied bread miraculously." "I got the promotion," not "Jesus provided lavishly." It matters how we understand what Jesus is doing and what he is showing us about himself when he does it.

Now listen closely. "Do not work for the food that perishes." They did work for it. This crowd commandeered some boats from Tiberias, well, not literally hijacked them, but they took boats across the sea looking for Jesus. They're working for food that perishes. They've set their sights on a man who can make bread. They're chasing a baker. They've reduced Jesus to an amazing baker, and they're working for bread. And he says, "Do not work for the food that perishes, but for the food that endures to eternal life, which the Son of Man will" what? "Give to you."

This is huge. Jesus tells the crowd, you're working for what perishes, when what will never perish, eternal life, is available to you, and the Son of Man will give it to you. It's a gift, not earned, not worked for. Titus 3 says it: "But when the goodness and loving kindness of God our Savior appeared, he saved us, not because of works done by us in righteousness, but according to his own mercy." What you really need, he says, you don't have to work for at all. The Son of Man will give it to you, the food that endures to eternal life.

That eternal life is a gift is probably the hardest thing for anyone to really believe, and we see it in their response. Jesus says, the Son of Man will give you the food that endures to eternal life. And they answer, "What must we do, to be doing the works of God?" He has just told them, in no uncertain terms, I give it to you, and the next thing they say is, what must we do? No matter how many times I preach it, no matter how many times we talk about it with each other or read it in our Bibles, every time we run up against the radical truth that Jesus Christ, out of his own mercy, simply gives us eternal life as a free gift, our instinctive next response is, all right, so what do I have to do?

There's a reason for that. From birth our whole lives are oriented the other way, and that's why Jesus will be so tender about it in a moment. In almost every other relationship we have, at least in part, we receive approval and acceptance, we are brought in rather than cast out, on the basis of merit: as long as I'm useful, helpful, efficient, attractive, powerful, as long as I have something to contribute, as long as I'm needed in some way. So when Jesus says eternal life is a gift from the Son of Man, it's an entirely new category for his hearers, and their instinctive response is, "What must we do, to be doing the works of God?"

Verse 29: "Jesus answered them, 'This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he has sent.'" They ask, what must we do to be doing the works of God? He answers, the work of God is that you believe in him whom he has sent. He moves the work off the people and puts it back where it belongs, on God. The work of God is that you would not work, but believe. Faith itself is a gift. "For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God."

But listen to their response. Verses 30 and 31: "So they said to him, 'Then what sign do you do, that we may see and believe you? What work do you perform? Our fathers ate the manna in the wilderness; as it is written, "He gave them bread from heaven to eat."'" Remember who's talking. This is the crowd that just got their sign. He just multiplied the bread for them. And he has already told them why they came: you are seeking me not because of the signs, but because you ate your fill of the loaves. They've come back wanting bread again. Don't miss it.

Here's the next children's story. We've been watching Winnie the Pooh. In the beginning of the first movie, Pooh sees a beehive at the top of a tree. He wants the honey, so he tries to climb the tree and fails. Then Christopher Robin comes along with a balloon, and Pooh asks, oh so casually, whether Christopher Robin might happen to have something like a balloon. And of course he does, and Pooh floats up to the honey. That's who I see in the crowd this morning. "What sign do you do, that we may see and believe you? What work do you perform? Our fathers ate the manna in the wilderness; as it is written, 'He gave them bread from heaven to eat.'" They're telling him which miracle they'd like him to do to prove himself. That's all they're doing. They're hungry again.

I'll get to what Jesus says in a second, but see what they're holding out to him like a carrot. They think, and we tend to think it too, that he needs to show himself or prove himself to them in the way they want. They hold out their faith as a gift they can offer him. And they completely fail to see that their faith is a gift given to them by him, and that he is not their trick pony. They are saying exactly what he said they came for: you came because you ate your fill of the loaves. Show us a sign. Give us more bread.

Verses 32 and 33: "Jesus then said to them, 'Truly, truly, I say to you, it was not Moses who gave you the bread from heaven, but my Father gives you the true bread from heaven. For the bread of God is he who comes down from heaven and gives life to the world.'"

When my father was alive, he was a truck driver, and he and I were mostly estranged for most of my life and lived in different states. One morning in my early twenties I was driving to work and saw a beautiful sunrise, and I thought, by the Spirit, that my father, out on the road somewhere, was probably seeing the same sunrise. So I texted him, something like: Saw the sunrise this morning. It's incredible. I'm pretty sure you can see it too right now. Take a look if you get a second. What I'm hearing in my head as I drive to work is that I need only look at my own hand to know I am fearfully and wonderfully made. I pray that as you look at this sunrise, you'll see the goodness of God.

He called me back thirty seconds later. We hadn't spoken in a long time. He was crying, and he said, ten minutes ago I bought a lottery ticket, and I said to God, if you make these the winning numbers, I will know you are real, and I'll be yours and follow you the rest of my life. But if you don't, then once and for all I'll know you're not real and I'm free to put you out of my mind. And he said, I can hear him laughing at me right now. I thought I needed winning lottery numbers from him, and he knew all I needed was a text from my son and a beautiful sunrise. He was crying. It was a beautiful moment.

Later that year, in the middle of his second divorce, wrecked by it, he called me and said, I remembered the text you sent me, and that all I needed was to see a sunrise. I need you to remind me. And I said, Dad, you didn't need to see a sunrise. You needed the risen Son. And I talked to him about Jesus, and he was broken.

The crowd is testing Jesus: we need bread. If you do the bread thing, you can have our faith. As if their faith were something to dangle in front of Jesus to make him earn it. And Jesus says, I don't need your faith. You need me. In fact, you're counting on me for your faith. So he doesn't play along when they bring up the manna. He says, it wasn't Moses who gave you that bread from heaven. My Father gives you the true bread from heaven. The bread of God is he who comes down from heaven and gives life to the world.

Verse 34: "They said to him, 'Sir, give us this bread always.'" That sounds sincere. He has told them the manna didn't come from Moses but from his Father, that his Father gives true bread that will nourish them forever, and they want that bread. They're hungry. Give us this bread always. But they're still thinking in terms of seizing him to be their king. The whole point was to track Jesus down because he can make bread, and now he says his Father can give bread always, and they're still thinking of a bread factory: he can literally just feed us bread forever.

Verses 35 to 40: "Jesus said to them, 'I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger, and whoever believes in me shall never thirst. But I said to you that you have seen me and yet do not believe. All that the Father gives me will come to me, and whoever comes to me I will never cast out. For I have come down from heaven, not to do my own will but the will of him who sent me. And this is the will of him who sent me, that I should lose nothing of all that he has given me, but raise it up on the last day. For this is the will of my Father, that everyone who looks on the Son and believes in him should have eternal life, and I will raise him up on the last day.'" Verse 41: "So the Jews grumbled about him, because he said, 'I am the bread that came down from heaven.'"

In one paragraph they go from "Sir, give us this bread always" to grumbling, once they realize the bread he means is himself. That's often what faith looks like when it sprouts quickly and then fizzles out. You go to Jesus wanting him to be something you define, but not wanting him to be who he is or to offer what he actually offers. They say, give us this bread. He says, it's me. They say, we don't want that. We want the barley loaves.

Hear this: to prefer Moses is to prefer death. To want Jesus merely to repeat the old miracles that all pointed ahead to him is to prefer death. To say, Jesus, I'd settle for you being a prophet, or I'd rather you just be a miracle worker, is to prefer death. He's about to tell them, your fathers who ate the manna in the wilderness died. I'm offering you bread that gives the breath of life.

So why won't they come to him? He answers the question himself: "I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger, and whoever believes in me shall never thirst. But I said to you that you have seen me and yet do not believe. All that the Father gives me will come to me, and whoever comes to me I will never cast out." That is a hard truth and a deeply comforting one. The bread of life stands in front of a hungry people, and they won't take and eat. And he tells them why: you've seen me and do not believe, but all that the Father gives me will come to me.

Your ability to come to Christ, as we've already seen this morning, is given to you by the Father. All that the Father gives to the Son will come to him. The church debates this, and people like to debate it when it comes from the mouths of the apostles, which carries just as much authority. But set that aside for a minute, because this is Jesus speaking about himself, and we have to let him speak for himself. You're looking at me and you won't believe, he says, but all whom the Father has given me will come to me, and whoever comes to me I will never cast out.

That was a central truth for my father. He was emancipated at fifteen. He had a hard life. Like many of us, he'd had people promise they would always be there and then leave, until he concluded that the only person you can really depend on is yourself. So he was fiercely independent and built his life so he wouldn't need anyone. That's why being truly known in community matters so much. We know how easy it is in our culture to run from community and cling to independence, because we don't want to be truly vulnerable. The world has told us that to open yourself up is to open yourself to being wounded, because people fail you. The Lord doesn't. My father believed the world's version, and he needed to see a God who would never cast him out.

This morning I think we all need to see a Jesus who will never cast us out. But we can't really believe in the eternal security of our place with Jesus until we understand how we became his in the first place. If you earned it, you have to keep earning it. If there was something about you that made you worthy of adoption into God's family, you'd need to stay worthy. If he needed some skill of yours for his kingdom and you became less useful, he might drop you. We have to understand that the whole way you were brought to Jesus is that the Father gave you to him as a gift. In our tradition we say that regeneration precedes faith. You were dead in the trespasses in which you once walked, and the Father made you alive, turned your heart of stone into a heart of flesh, placed the gift of faith in you, and you came to Jesus, necessarily. That's what Jesus is saying here: all that the Father gives me will come to me.

So when they say, what sign will you do? Do this and we'll believe you, obey us and we'll believe you, he doesn't even bother answering. I'm not worried about that, he says. Everyone the Father gives me will come to me, and whoever comes to me I will never cast out. Those go together. Your security in the hand of Christ comes from the fact that you didn't earn your standing with him in the first place. You were bought with a price. You were the Father's gift to him.

"For I have come down from heaven, not to do my own will but the will of him who sent me. And this is the will of him who sent me, that I should lose nothing of all that he has given me, but raise it up on the last day." Some of us are still on the outside looking in, standing opposed to God because we haven't received grace or faith, trying to earn the Father's favor by our good works. Others of us have been adopted, but we don't believe we're adopted. We think some combination of things brought us into God's favor, so we're still wearing ourselves out as if it were our job to hold on to our place with God. That's nonsense.

I adopted my oldest son. In the early days of our relationship he really wrestled with this. We're doing real talk this morning. He had lived a life where men didn't stick around, so a piece of paper didn't mean much to him. He was a kid. He didn't just need legal proof that he belonged to me now, and that it didn't depend on his performance. He needed to see the tender, unwavering love of a father. But one of my favorite things about adoption is that when it's finalized, a new birth certificate comes in the mail. I didn't know that when I adopted him. They send a new birth certificate, and his new name is on it from the day he was born, as if it had always been there. That's what it means to be brought into a brand-new identity purchased for you. It isn't only, from now on you belong to me. It's as if you have always belonged to me. The document isn't what helped him believe it, but it certainly helps to look at it and say, it's like the old me doesn't even exist anymore. That's what Jesus invites us into.

And yet they grumbled. Verses 42 to 44: "They said, 'Is not this Jesus, the son of Joseph, whose father and mother we know? How does he now say, "I have come down from heaven"?' Jesus answered them, 'Do not grumble among yourselves. No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him. And I will raise him up on the last day.'"

He doubles down. Everyone who comes to me I will not cast out. Everyone who comes to me will receive eternal life. Who will come? Those the Father gives me. They will come. He says it as a declaration: those the Father gives me will come, and they will not be cast out. But on the other side of the coin: "No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him."

"Draws" doesn't carry the force of the word. The Greek word means to draw or drag, and it was used of mules drawing a chariot and of oxen drawing the plough through a field. John uses it later of the disciples hauling in a net full of fish. No one comes to me unless the Father drags him. We want it to sound as if the Father woos or entices. But an ox doesn't woo the plough behind it, and a horse doesn't lure the carriage hitched to it. It hauls it. It takes the load on itself and carries it from one place to another. You don't call down a well, come up to me, water. You drop in the bucket and haul it out. No one comes to me, Jesus says, unless the Father reaches in, takes the dead you, and drags you into new life. And whenever he does, you will have new life.

Verses 46 to 59: "Not that anyone has seen the Father except he who is from God; he has seen the Father. Truly, truly, I say to you, whoever believes has eternal life. I am the bread of life. Your fathers ate the manna in the wilderness, and they died. This is the bread that comes down from heaven, so that one may eat of it and not die. I am the living bread that came down from heaven. If anyone eats of this bread, he will live forever. And the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh.' The Jews then disputed among themselves, saying, 'How can this man give us his flesh to eat?' So Jesus said to them, 'Truly, truly, I say to you, unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you. Whoever feeds on my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life, and I will raise him up on the last day. For my flesh is true food, and my blood is true drink. Whoever feeds on my flesh and drinks my blood abides in me, and I in him. As the living Father sent me, and I live because of the Father, so whoever feeds on me, he also will live because of me. This is the bread that came down from heaven, not like the bread the fathers ate, and died. Whoever feeds on this bread will live forever.' Jesus said these things in the synagogue, as he taught at Capernaum."

I'll save the implications of those words for next week. But I'll say this: if this is your first time hearing this, if you're new to Christianity, you might not come back. Next week we'll see how the disciples respond, and they'll say, "This is a hard saying; who can listen to it?" And in the next passage we'll find that many people stopped following him after he said this. I'm not blind to what I just quoted Jesus saying. He said, you have to eat me and drink me, and the people responded the way you might expect.

But the fact that you're hearing me quote this and you aren't walking out right now is a testimony to what Jesus just said: all whom the Father has given him will come to him. That you can hear this, bear it and understand what he's saying is a gift of grace to you. It's a sign that you've been given the grace of faith. And see that Jesus never calls us into blind faith. He calls us into a faith we experience. He doesn't say believing is something we only do in our heads. He says, you must eat of me and drink of me. He offers himself as the seal and substance of the new covenant. Jesus Christ was going to let himself be crucified on a Roman cross and buried, and three days later take up his life again, to seal the covenant he bought with his own body and blood. That's what he's talking about. He is the bread of life.

Many of us spend a lot of time thinking about Jesus but not tasting and seeing Jesus. We act as if our faith were something done mostly in our heads, purely academic: I read this and try to understand it. But Jesus says, take and eat. You must take me in. That is entirely different from just thinking about him. And he says that those who do, when he is in us and we are in him, are invited into eternal life.

If we drift from this, and we do, avoiding passages like this one, we can turn Christianity into a club, a set of things we do. We show up at church, we go to our small group, and when we talk about Jesus, we talk about him mainly as a bread maker, someone who can meet some needs. He wants none of that. Jesus knows what he's saying here will thin the crowd. He knows that all who will be left are those the Father gave him. But he doesn't mince words, because he knows that everyone the Lord is bringing to life will respond to this gospel.

I know this isn't the main point, but it's heavy on my heart. If we as a church drift from this, if we won't talk to others in these terms, then we aren't even doing the work of the church, which is to spread the gospel. The message of Jesus Christ is not mainly that he can improve your life. It's not mainly that he's a good teacher, or a miracle worker, or a good addition to the other good things in your life. It's none of that. It's that those who eat his flesh and drink his blood have eternal life. However you put it, we're sometimes terrified to share the gospel because we won't have the right words: if I say it the wrong way, people will leave and won't come. Jesus said it in about the strangest and most offensive way possible, with no concern for whether the crowd shrank or grew, because he was confident that by the will of the Father, those who heard would receive new life. So you can go say it awkwardly, is what I'm saying. You can say hard things, if you believe that people don't merely assent to these truths in their heads, but that God the Father gives people to his Son as a gift.

In conclusion: next week we'll see how the disciples respond. Today we see how the crowd responds. Only God really knows whether you are among the disciples or among the crowd. But here is a good test. Is your security with God, your standing before him, based on your merit, or on the merit of the body broken and the blood shed for you in Christ? This is the charge Jesus gives the crowd, so it's the charge I give you this morning from his own mouth: you cannot be saved, you cannot come to him, you cannot respond to him, unless the Father puts new life in you. And if he does, you will. If that's what he's doing in you this morning, then you can, and you will. I invite you into it.

And to those of you among the disciples, who know these are the words of life, I invite you into what Jesus assures you of this morning. It's what I needed to hear most, so I trust someone else needed it too: you have eternal security in the hand of God. Hear me. He will not lose a single one. You're not going to mess this up. You cannot wriggle free of your adoption. He has renamed you. He has set his seal on you. He has made you his own. You are his possession, and he will not let you go. That is your promise if you belong to him. And if you don't, the invitation Jesus makes is this: what work must you do? Look on the Son and believe.

Let's take a moment to pray and to come to terms with who we are as God speaks to us in this passage. Then let's take whatever he says to us and give it away, to one another and then to the people around us. Let's pray.
