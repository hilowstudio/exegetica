# They Shall Inherit the Earth (John 3:22-30)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 3:22-30

---

This sermon belongs to a series on the Beatitudes, and this morning's beatitude is "Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth." If the series has felt disorienting so far, you're in good company. When Jesus speaks, he turns the universe upside down. He usually speaks so against the grain of our culture that our heads spin as we try to make sense of what he says, and the Beatitudes are no different.

So let me recap what a beatitude is. The Beatitudes are a series of statements Jesus made to his disciples at the opening of the Sermon on the Mount. As he began to teach his followers, he opened with these two-part statements. In the first part he names a heart posture or characteristic of the ideal disciple, the ideal follower of Jesus. He is describing the people of God, the children of God, laying out the right heart, the right position, the right thinking of a disciple. Then to each description he attaches a blessing, a here-and-now blessing: "Blessed are."

And when he says "blessed are," he means blessed are. When we read the Beatitudes, we're tempted to treat them as wisdom literature, as if we were in Proverbs, or as law, as if they were commands. Jesus says, "Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth," and we want to hear, you should become meek so that you can inherit the earth. That's making it law. Or we hear it as wisdom: it would be good to be meek, because there's a reward for it, so we should all try to be meeker. Both of these rob something from what Jesus is actually doing. He is making proclamations of truth, simple declarative statements that are true right now: the meek are blessed. It was true as he spoke it, and it is still true today. If we turn it into wisdom literature or law, we'll misunderstand it, and it will push us to pursue something we can't actually reach in our own strength. I'll talk about that this morning.

But first we have to get a right sense of what the word meek even means. Meekness is so countercultural, especially in the West, that when I began studying for this sermon I had to look it up. I don't walk around talking about meekness. I don't pursue meekness. I don't know many people I would accurately describe as meek, because meekness often looks like weakness, and nothing is more countercultural than wanting to be weak.

So let me lay it out. Meekness is what you think it is: humility, lowliness, gentleness, quietness. Meekness is a heart posture of humility. But there are many ways to arrive at humility, and meekness gets there in a very specific way. You surely know humble people. I've even been called humble at times, which blows my mind, because you can arrive at humility by different roads.

Some people are humble because they have nothing to brag about. They enter a conversation, recognize they have nothing to bring to it, and keep quiet. I'll step in quietly here and be a learner, a supporting voice, because I don't bring much to the table. We can arrive at humility by assessing ourselves and deciding this isn't an area where pride is appropriate. But when we step into an area where we're the experts, where we've accomplished something, suddenly we're no longer humble. Now we want to be the authority. We want to be assertive.

Meekness draws its humility from somewhere else entirely. It doesn't come from low self-esteem or a low view of yourself. It is born out of confidence, confidence that my status, my name, my security and my approval are secure, so secure that I don't need anything from you. I'm not trying to draw your approval, impress you or get anything from you. So I'm free to lay myself down before you and make myself even lower than I am, because I don't need anything from you. My position is more secure than anything you could offer me.

If Jesus was meek, and he was, then meekness can't be the result of a lack of power or a lack of wisdom, because Jesus is all-wise and all-powerful, and yet he was meek. What makes meekness a unique kind of humility is that it is born of complete confidence in your standing, and what Jesus was teaching is your standing before God.

Jesus is the perfect example of meekness. Though he was in the form of God, he did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men, and humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross. And now he is highly exalted at the right hand of the Father. That is Jesus. He showed us perfect meekness: the ability to lower himself, to humble himself so far that he could give himself away for the good of others. And he was able to do that because of his sure confidence in the Father, in his position with the Father, in his eternal place in the Trinity with the Father and the Holy Spirit.

So Jesus gives us this amazing picture of meekness, and in the Beatitudes he teaches us that the meek are surely blessed, because they will inherit the earth. And many of us will respond, I want the earth. I want to inherit the earth, so I'll pursue meekness. But the minute you pursue meekness for its benefits to yourself, it isn't meekness anymore. By definition, meekness lowers itself for the benefit of others, not for its own benefit. I lay this out because, as you try to wrap your mind around meekness, I want to demolish any idea that this is something you achieve and strive for in order to get the blessings Jesus describes. It doesn't work that way.

What Jesus is saying is that those who are meek are surely blessed and will inherit the earth. It's simply a true statement. It is already true, and it is true of the meek. Some of you are reeling right now: but I'm not meek. I wouldn't describe myself as meek. Here's what I'd say. Jesus is describing the ideal state of his disciples, the heart posture and character of his children. And these things come as a gift of the Holy Spirit. As the Spirit takes up residence in the children of God, we are conformed to the image of Christ. That is a sure thing, and we don't achieve it in our own power. He does it for us. So each beatitude should send us to the Lord, calling on the Holy Spirit: make this true. You've said it's true, so make it true. That's part of walking with Jesus.

As we talk about meekness, it helps to look at something more relatable than trying to be Jesus. I've said Jesus is the perfect example of meekness, and it would be a tall order to tell you, so go and be like Jesus, even though that is what we're called to do. Instead, I want to draw from the story of John the Baptist in John 3:22–30.

"After this Jesus and his disciples went into the Judean countryside, and he remained there with them and was baptizing. John also was baptizing at Aenon near Salim, because water was plentiful there, and people were coming and being baptized (for John had not yet been put in prison). Now a discussion arose between some of John's disciples and a Jew over purification. And they came to John and said to him, 'Rabbi, he who was with you across the Jordan, to whom you bore witness—look, he is baptizing, and all are going to him.'"

Let me set the scene. John is the one who baptized Jesus. The Gospel of Mark opens that way. John had gone ahead of Jesus, before Jesus began his public ministry, crying out in the wilderness that the Messiah, the Christ, had come into the world, and calling people to prepare themselves, repent and be baptized. He preached a message of repentance and baptism. The Bible literally calls him John the Baptist, because baptizing is what he did. His public ministry was to draw crowds, call them to repentance and baptize them. And the place God gave him put him in a position to baptize Jesus himself. So he was a big deal. He was the voice crying out in the wilderness.

After he baptized Jesus, he kept on with his mission, calling people to repent and be baptized. That's what he's doing here at Aenon near Salim. But now there's been a shift: Jesus has taken his disciples into the Judean countryside, and they are baptizing too. So you have two camps baptizing in the same general region at the same time, preaching the same message of repentance, confession and purification. And that creates some tension.

Verse 25 says a discussion arose between some of John's disciples and a Jew over purification. We aren't told exactly what they were discussing. But whatever it was, it sends John's disciples to him: "Rabbi, he who was with you across the Jordan, to whom you bore witness—look, he is baptizing, and all are going to him." Everyone is going to him. Intentionally or not, John's disciples are tempting him to lean into his role and his reputation, because they have just named some important facts: John, remember the man you baptized in the Jordan, the one you bore witness to? Everyone is going to him now. And now we're being questioned by this man about purification, surely not for the first time. Something in that discussion drove them to John: why is everyone going over to Jesus, the one you testified about?

You can imagine the tension. I'd guess the purification debate went something like this. You've been baptizing for a long time, calling people to repent, confess and be baptized to prepare the way for the Christ. You've had a massive following. When you show up at a body of water, the crowds come, because they've heard about your preaching and your baptisms. Yet the crowds are getting smaller and smaller, and even some you've baptized are going over to Jesus to be baptized by his disciples. So what gives? Does your baptism not work? Is something wrong with your purification, so that people need to be baptized a second time? Is his baptism more effective than yours? Your crowd is shrinking and his is growing. What's going on?

I can't emphasize enough who John really is, so let's flip back to John 1. Verses 6 to 8: "There was a man sent from God, whose name was John. He came as a witness, to bear witness about the light, that all might believe through him. He was not the light, but came to bear witness about the light." Then verses 19 to 27: "And this is the testimony of John, when the Jews sent priests and Levites from Jerusalem to ask him, 'Who are you?' He confessed, and did not deny, but confessed, 'I am not the Christ.' And they asked him, 'What then? Are you Elijah?' He said, 'I am not.' 'Are you the Prophet?' And he answered, 'No.' So they said to him, 'Who are you? We need to give an answer to those who sent us. What do you say about yourself?' He said, 'I am the voice of one crying out in the wilderness, "Make straight the way of the Lord," as the prophet Isaiah said.' (Now they had been sent from the Pharisees.) They asked him, 'Then why are you baptizing, if you are neither the Christ, nor Elijah, nor the Prophet?' John answered them, 'I baptize with water, but among you stands one you do not know, even he who comes after me, the strap of whose sandal I am not worthy to untie.'"

John the Baptist is the opening character of John's Gospel. In this testimony we read that he came to roll out the red carpet for Jesus. At no point does he claim any fame for himself. He doesn't claim to be Elijah, the Prophet or the Christ. He even says he isn't worthy to untie Jesus' sandal strap. And when they ask, so who do you say you are, he says, I am the voice crying out in the wilderness.

So when you think about John the Baptist, think John the voice. When John's voice cried out, the people came in crowds and received this preparatory baptism with water, getting ready to receive the Christ. And now the voice that had been crying out is losing his following as people go over to a different voice. So the discussion arises, the debate about purification, and John's disciples question him and tempt him to defend himself: "Rabbi, he who was with you across the Jordan, to whom you bore witness—look, he is baptizing, and all are going to him." This is what they made of the purification debate. John, you baptized him. You're the one who baptized Jesus, and now Jesus is baptizing and people are going to him as if he's the real thing. Whatever makes Jesus such a big deal is only because of you. You taught about him, you baptized him, you told everyone how great he is. But look what it's costing you. Everyone is going over to him.

In an aside at the beginning of chapter four, John tells us that "Jesus himself did not baptize, but only his disciples." You can almost imagine that Jesus, knowing how this would go, didn't want anyone to be able to claim, I got the real thing: I was baptized by Jesus, and you were only baptized by John the Baptist, or only by one of Jesus' disciples. Jesus let his disciples carry out baptism while he was on earth, and I think that's a beautiful posture of humility in Christ as well.

John had every opportunity to say, look, Jesus is great, but I'm the one who baptized Jesus. That's not what he does. Instead, starting in verse 27, John answers, "A person cannot receive even one thing unless it is given him from heaven. You yourselves bear me witness, that I said, 'I am not the Christ, but I have been sent before him.' The one who has the bride is the bridegroom. The friend of the bridegroom, who stands and hears him, rejoices greatly at the bridegroom's voice. Therefore this joy of mine is now complete. He must increase, but I must decrease."

This response is astonishing. John is watching his crowds and his following leave. He had given up his life in sacrificial obedience to the Lord to lay down this red carpet for Jesus, and instead of being carried along into greater fame, he is losing his following to Jesus.

First John says, "A person cannot receive even one thing unless it is given him from heaven." He's talking about two things. First, his own sphere of influence: whatever influence he has had up to now, he credits to God. Anything I've received so far was given to me by the hand of God. But more importantly, he's saying that the people moving over to follow Jesus, leaving John's camp to see what this Jesus is about, are also the work of God. Jesus is not receiving a single person apart from the hand of God. If people are responding to the Christ, God is moving. John knew it was impossible for the following of Christ to grow unless God was giving people to him. He knew he had come to be a voice in the wilderness, but that God would have to change hearts if he was going to give anyone to the Christ. John knew he couldn't do that. So his first response is celebration: a person cannot receive even one thing unless it is given him from heaven. He rejoices that the people he baptized are being given to Jesus as a gift from heaven.

Think about everything in your life, everything you've pursued and gained in your own strength. You won't hold any of it forever. It is all perishing. Yet everything in the Lord's hand is kept safe.

In verse 28 John reminds his disciples and anyone in earshot: "You yourselves bear me witness, that I said, 'I am not the Christ, but I have been sent before him.'" He repeats his humble posture. You want to make me more than I am, more than the lot God has given me? I am the one who goes before the Christ, and nothing more.

But it isn't only humility. In verse 29 he exalts Christ: "The one who has the bride is the bridegroom." This is beautiful, because John begins calling the people he baptized the bride. Not that his baptism made them the bride. As they came in humility, confession and repentance, and God gave these children of God over to the Christ, John rightly identifies everyone who leaves his camp to join Christ's as the bride. The bride is an image that will grow as the Gospels continue, and as the Epistles are written we'll get a clearer view of what it means. But here John rightly sees the people leaving him for Jesus as the bride, and he rightly identifies Jesus as the bridegroom. The one who has the bride is the bridegroom, and I don't have the bride. The bride is leaving me. I am not the bridegroom. But the bride knows the bridegroom's voice, so she must find her way to him.

"The friend of the bridegroom, who stands and hears him, rejoices greatly at the bridegroom's voice." Don't think of me as the Christ, John says. Don't think of me as the bridegroom. My security, my identity, my status and my joy are wrapped up in this eternal position: I am a friend of the bridegroom. And not only a friend, but one who rejoices greatly when he hears the bridegroom's voice.

I don't think it's an accident that John speaks of the bridegroom's voice here. He had called himself the voice crying out in the wilderness. John the voice is making way for the bridegroom's voice. He steps aside and lets his own voice be muted, quieted, lowered. He doesn't assert his voice, his accomplishments, his status or his position. He quiets his voice and says instead, I delight in the voice of the bridegroom, for I am the bridegroom's friend.

Everything we have that is worth anything is wrapped up and secured in the fact that we are friends of the bridegroom. That's the whole story. The whole point is that we, as friends of the bridegroom, enjoy all that the bridegroom has to offer, and the bride knows the bridegroom's voice and must make her way to him. John is glad to play his part in the redemptive history of the world, but he knows that as long as you stay with me, the bridegroom's friend, and not with the bridegroom, you're in dangerous territory. These are not the waters you want to be standing in. His water is far sweeter. That water gives eternal life. So John joyfully turns his following, his life's work, over to Christ, the bridegroom. It's gorgeous.

Why does he do it? "Therefore this joy of mine is now complete." It was for joy that John the Baptist emptied himself of his following. It was for joy that he emptied himself of status, stature and reputation, for joy that he watched crowds of his followers go over to Jesus, for joy that he endured the charges and mockery of those who said, doesn't your baptism work? When they asked him about purification, he was one of the first to grasp the difference between what he was doing, baptizing with water, and what Christ was doing, baptizing with the Spirit.

He doesn't answer the purification question directly at first. The conversation started with purification and then twisted into why people are leaving you for Jesus. But John loops back around and answers the question about purification: the bride must go to the bridegroom. It makes me think of Ephesians 5. Paul will use language like this to help us understand the bride and the bridegroom. The bride of Christ is the church of Christ, the children of God, purchased by the blood of the cross and made pure by the covering of Christ. They are made pure by the bridegroom. John couldn't do that. He couldn't make anyone pure. But the one he bore witness to could. So it was his joy to see the bride united with the bridegroom, because the bridegroom makes the bride pure. That's why Paul told husbands to love their wives sacrificially, as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her, that he might sanctify her.

So in this poetic way John hears an argument about purification, is asked a very different question, turns the question aside, exalts Christ, rests in his joy, and declares that purification is found in Jesus. And his joy in doing it is complete.

He finishes, "He must increase, but I must decrease." Let's stay on the word must. Not, he should increase and I should decrease. He must. John understood that from the foundation of the world it was the perfect will of God that Christ must increase and we must decrease. This is the natural order of things. As we are humbled to the point of submitting to the lordship of Christ, as we see him rightly and bask in his glory as his dominion over this world and over our lives increases, we are brought to the fulfillment of what? Joy. Our joy is complete.

So when we try to understand how John the Baptist could be so meek, willingly and joyfully lowering himself, his status, his achievements, even his voice, the answer is that he had total confidence and security in the bridegroom, the greater voice, the one who truly purifies, the one who must increase. Knowing he must increase filled John's heart with delight as he watched the Lord accomplish in the world what he had said he would. He was watching God keep his promises in the Christ, and it brought him deep and complete joy.

John didn't then retire quietly. His life would end with a beheading. He would be called to humble himself to the point of the final silencing of his voice. Yet even then, with his eyes fixed on the bridegroom, John understood what Jesus teaches in this beatitude: "Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth." There was nothing on this side of eternity that John needed to fight for, cling to or protect. He could joyfully lay it all down in service to the work that must be done, because on the other side of it is the inheritance of the earth.

So as we wrap up, a word about inheriting the earth. This earth will be redeemed and restored. Christ will return. John knew it. I know it. You know it. That is the central hope of the Christian life, and it's why we can be meek. We can lower ourselves, humble ourselves and let go of whatever we try to cling to in this life, because we have a greater hope: the bridegroom returns for his bride, makes all things new and restores all things to their right order, so that we will be citizens of an earth we belong to. We will receive an inheritance, because we are joined to the bridegroom and the bridegroom calls us his friends. We share his inheritance as his brothers and sisters, with a reserved seat at the King's table, belonging at every meal, citizens of a greater kingdom on a restored earth. John knew that so deeply that he could give up his life for it.

You and I wrestle with meekness because it's costly. It will cost us reputation. It will cost us the ability to push and manipulate people into doing what serves our interests. In our pride we want to assert ourselves: our wisdom, our ideas, our status, our achievements, our money and our power. What we're really trying to do is carve out an inheritance for ourselves on this side of eternity, to make this world our permanent home. That is what keeps us from being meek: our hope isn't in anything greater than what we can achieve by our own might, strength and strategy. But John saw something more beautiful, and he could empty himself of everything he had gained because he believed what was coming was so much better.

Now listen. I don't think this is something you can strive for. I would say you can't. You and I can't simply decide in our own strength that the economy of the kingdom of God is better than the economy of man, because the economy of man is all around us all the time. This world is constantly telling us what success, status, security, safety and joy look like. It is the work of the Spirit alone that lets us tune out all those lesser voices and hear the voice of the bridegroom clearly, so that as his friends we rejoice greatly at his voice and find our satisfaction there, sure that in our meekness, our status being nothing more than friends of the bridegroom, we will inherit the earth.

So take an account of your life. I don't want you to ask, am I meek? I want you to ask, where is my joy found? Where is my rest? Where is my satisfaction? Whose approval do I care about? Does your standing before God, who looks on you with delight because he finds you spotless and purified by the bridegroom, fill you with complete joy? Are you fulfilled as you see the work of God done in your life and in the lives of the people around you? Are you letting the church be the place where you get the clearest picture of the reality you were made for, or are you looking out there, in the world? You will find yourself either devastated or full of complete joy depending on where you look. So I invite you into the satisfaction of being covered in Christ, which makes meekness possible. It frees you to stop fighting for what is already perfectly yours in the economy of heaven. Let's pray that the Lord would do that in us.
