# The Savior’s Power (John 6:1-21)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 6:1-21

---

Think back to last week. We walked through the verbal sparring between Jesus and the Jews in which he declared himself, in no uncertain terms, to be God. The Jews challenged him. They were frustrated and angry, even seeking to kill him, because he made himself equal with God by claiming the right to work on the Sabbath. And Jesus doubled down. He claimed to be not only the God of the Sabbath but equal with God in glory and honor, in will, in majesty and in power. He even said he is the God who by the power of his word will raise the dead, the one who will judge all people and raise them either to the resurrection of life or to the resurrection of judgment. Last week Jesus claimed, in no uncertain terms, to be God.

If we stay there for a while, we see Jesus rightly lifting himself to his proper place, the place where John's Gospel began: in the beginning with God, himself God. It was a big day in his earthly ministry. In many ways he was moving forward the timeline on which these people would crucify him for those claims. But as we lift our eyes and see Jesus as the eternal God of the universe, the God who will bring judgment on all people, the God by whose word we are saved, suddenly he feels like God. And as mere men, when we see him in his rightful place, we become aware of a great gap between us, and the question sets in: is this great Jesus approachable?

The result, often, is that many in the church will trust Jesus for salvation: he's clearly the Jesus who can give me a future after this life. But surely this God isn't interested in my today. I can count on Jesus to bring me into eternity with him, but where can I look for today's meal? So I think it's precious that John leads us next into the feeding of the five thousand. My hope this morning is that we'll see how he lowers himself and humbles himself to be the God and Lord of our daily meal.

Let's go verse by verse. John 6:1: "After this Jesus went away to the other side of the Sea of Galilee, which is the Sea of Tiberias."

Every preacher has to make decisions. When I sat down with the whole book of John, I had to decide how to break it up, where to stop, what a preachable amount is. I don't want to stop in a way that disrupts the flow of what the author is trying to communicate or takes verses out of context. I want to make those decisions mindfully before the Lord, to preach this the way the author meant it to be received.

And when you preach the Gospels there's an added burden: there are four of them. Matthew, Mark, Luke and John often overlap and tell the same story from different vantage points. There's a lot we can learn about the feeding of the five thousand from the other Gospel writers. In fact, it's the only miracle of Jesus' ministry told in all four Gospels. So you have to decide: am I going to preach four Gospels, or what John said, since we're in John?

Here's why it matters. They were all there. These are eyewitness accounts. So what each writer includes is as telling as what he leaves out. When an author leaves out something he saw, it tells us what stood out to him as a priority. John is showing us, from his perspective, the most important details. So here's my decision. On the front end I'll tell you the whole story, compiling the four accounts. Then we'll stay with what really made an impression on John, what he shared and what he left out.

All four accounts tell the core of the story: there's a crowd, there's no food, Jesus multiplies food and distributes it until everyone's need is met, and then they gather the leftovers, twelve baskets. All four tell how Jesus took the bread, blessed it and broke it, gave it to be distributed, and the disciples ate. We'll get to that.

But Mark gives us details John doesn't. He says Jesus ended up in this desolate place because he was trying to get away from the crowds. The crowds were pressing in so hard that the disciples didn't even have a minute to eat. So Jesus was taking them away to a desolate place to escape the crowds. And Mark says that when Jesus looked out at the crowd, "he had compassion on them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd." That detail is unique to Mark. Mark also says Jesus taught them many things there, which John leaves out. Mark records the disciples' sarcasm, which John also leaves out. Mark is known for that. He talks about the disciples in the most honest way, showing how they answered Jesus back: what are we supposed to do, go spend two hundred denarii and feed all these people? Mark also points out that there were women and children besides the five thousand men, so we see just how many people we're talking about.

Luke tells us that when the crowd found Jesus, he welcomed them. That's unique to Luke: Jesus takes on the role of host. And Luke tells us what Jesus taught: he spoke to them of the kingdom of God.

Then there are John's unique details. John says the crowd followed him because of the signs. He names the place, the Sea of Tiberias, not just the Sea of Galilee. He says it was up on a mountain, telling us where that desolate place was. In John, it's Jesus who raises the problem of food, whereas in the other accounts the disciples are the ones fretting about it. John names the disciples, showing each one's part, where the others speak of them generally. It's in John that we learn the loaves came from a boy and that they were barley loaves. It's in John that the people respond by calling him the Prophet who is to come. And it's John who connects this miracle to the walking on the water.

So the core is the same, but each Gospel writer carried a different impression of the events and told them with different emphases. I want to pay attention to why John points out what he does and why he might leave out what he leaves out, hoping we'll see what John saw: a deeply tender Jesus who is at the center of it all.

"After this Jesus went away to the other side of the Sea of Galilee, which is the Sea of Tiberias. And a large crowd was following him, because they saw the signs that he was doing on the sick. Jesus went up on the mountain, and there he sat down with his disciples. Now the Passover, the feast of the Jews, was at hand. Lifting up his eyes, then, and seeing that a large crowd was coming toward him, Jesus said to Philip, 'Where are we to buy bread, so that these people may eat?' He said this to test him, for he himself knew what he would do. Philip answered him, 'Two hundred denarii worth of bread would not be enough for each of them to get a little.' One of his disciples, Andrew, Simon Peter's brother, said to him, 'There is a boy here who has five barley loaves and two fish, but what are they for so many?' Jesus said, 'Have the people sit down.'"

As we focus on this early part of the story, pray with me that we'll see what John saw. Father, you have lifted our eyes to see you as King of it all, Lord of it all, God of the universe. And here you are about to take care of a single meal for a single group of people on a single day in history. May that strike us this morning with as much awe as lifting our eyes upward. Show us a fuller and more complete picture of who you are, to invite us into your rest and comfort and joy. Invite us into deeper dependence on you. Help me preach by the power of your Spirit, who loves nothing more than to glorify the name of your Son. By his power, show us, lift our hearts, stir our hearts to be your children. Testify to us that you are Abba, Father. Lord, do it, because you can. In your name. Amen.

The first thing I want you to see this morning is that Jesus sees you before you see him, and he has compassion on you. Remember Mark: Jesus had compassion on the crowd and saw them as sheep without a shepherd. Suddenly the story takes on a different tone. So think about a time in your own life. I'll tell you one from mine, because maybe you need to hear it.

When my wife was pregnant with our second child, she was very sick. It was a high-risk pregnancy. She was having kidney problems, with a total blockage in one kidney, and she needed a nephrostomy tube out of her side to drain urine because she couldn't pass it naturally while she was pregnant. When they placed the tube, a staph infection was introduced into the kidney on the operating table, and she went septic. The sepsis reached the placenta and threatened the life of our baby.

I remember being in the room as a young dad while two specialists argued with each other in front of us about the best course of action. One said, we need to treat the infection and leave the baby in as long as possible, because at this point every day matters. The other said, it doesn't matter how long the baby stays in if he's swimming in a staph infection; we need to take him out. The doctors were all but looking at us to make the call, but we didn't go to medical school. We just wanted the baby to live. So in that moment my wife and I called on the name of our God, in front of the doctors and with each other, holding hands, not knowing what to do, and we said, let's induce. Take him out.

We thought the Lord had given us a miracle. The baby was born and cried out, and they handed him to my wife for some contact time, and he started turning blue. Blue beyond blue. He stopped breathing. They grabbed him and rushed him off to the NICU, where he would spend about two weeks. He's fine now.

I remember looking at that whole situation, really the whole pregnancy. It was a pregnancy of anger with God. It felt like I was running toward God and God was running away from me. I wasn't getting any sense that he had foreseen where I was going, that he cared, or that he would meet my need in that season. And here was this glimpse of hope: we called on you, God, we said let's induce, and here's the baby, and the baby is okay. And then they took the baby away. It was the scariest moment of my life. We didn't know what was going on. No one was explaining anything. They just grabbed him and left the room. Lord, I'm pursuing you, and it feels like you're walking away from me.

I don't want to project too much of myself onto this story. But when you compare this account with the other Gospels, what you see is a crowd that wants to get to Jesus. They had seen him heal people, and that's why they're following him. They don't want him to go away. They can see him leaving, so they follow. And he is literally trying to get to a desolate place where he can be alone. You have to wonder what they were thinking. And Jesus saw them as sheep without a shepherd. Suddenly certain details in the story change completely.

Before I point them out, think of a time in your life that's still unresolved, a time you've decided not to think about, when you called on the name of the Lord and he seemed to walk away, or not to hear you, or when you pursued him and he didn't seem to see you.

So Jesus, who has had compassion on them as sheep without a shepherd, departs to this desolate place and goes up the mountain. Then he takes the initiative with Philip: "Where are we to buy bread, so that these people may eat?" The crowd followed Jesus out into the wilderness, either desperate because they were sick and willing to skip a meal, or simply not thinking about what they were doing. Leaving the towns to follow Jesus to a desolate place, they're about to spend a day out there with no way to get food. Either they've put it out of their minds because they're seeking Jesus, or they're genuinely worried about it but chasing him anyway.

And here is what I love about John's account when we compare the Gospels. John points out that Jesus is the one who raises the question, not the disciples asking, what are we going to eat, what are they going to eat? It shows that Jesus is thinking ahead and planning in advance to meet the needs of the crowd following him. That's the first thing.

The second is that his awareness of them means he is orchestrating the whole setting. Last week we saw Jesus set everything up by telling the paralyzed man to pick up his bed and walk on the Sabbath, breaking the Sabbath rules on purpose so that he could reveal his glory and majesty. It's the same here. By his actions Jesus is setting up a situation in which the people are led out into the wilderness so that he can feed them. We should notice the echoes. He leads sheep without a shepherd to a place where, verse 10 says, "Jesus said, 'Have the people sit down.' Now there was much grass in the place. So the men sat down, about five thousand in number."

Why did John include the grass when he left out so much else? "Now there was much grass in the place." Sheep without a shepherd. And what does a shepherd do? He leads the sheep to green pastures. Jesus, knowing what he was going to do, has them wandering after him while he picks the perfect spot on a mountainside, where he can see the crowd, teach them, speak to them, distribute bread to them and give them a comfortable place to sit and have a picnic in the park.

And Jesus raises the problem before anyone asks him: "Where are we to buy bread, so that these people may eat?" He says it to test Philip, because he knows what he will do, and Philip answers, in effect, we can't. Then Andrew, Simon Peter's brother, says, "There is a boy here who has five barley loaves and two fish, but what are they for so many?"

That's typical of Andrew. He seems to be the one who mixes with people on the outer edge. Some of the Twelve huddled close to Jesus, and some were out shaking hands with the people who came to meet him. It's fitting that John tells us it was Andrew who knew about the boy, who had been talking with him and knew he had a lunch. But I imagine Andrew felt a little silly even bringing it up. Jesus says, where are we to buy bread? Philip says, it can't be done. Andrew says, there's a boy with a sack lunch. And Jesus says, good enough. Have the people sit down.

So Jesus not only sees you before you see him and has compassion on you. He not only looks on sheep who need a shepherd and takes up the call to be our shepherd. He also knows our need before we ask. The people didn't ask him. And he is willing to deny himself to meet that need. In fact, he plans ahead to do it. The other Gospels show that Jesus and his disciples were hungry themselves. The crowds followed, Jesus had compassion on them, knew their hunger, and decided he wouldn't only feed himself and his disciples. He would feed them too.

So go back to that first question. Think about a time when you felt you were pursuing Jesus and he was walking away from you. Here is what I want you to remember. What he is doing in this story, he is always doing in ours. Wherever he is going, he wants us following him and calling on him, and wherever he stops, he looks you in the eye and says, I see you. Before you even asked, I knew what you needed, and I creatively planned ahead to meet that need, so much so that I'm willing to deny myself to meet it. He is showing you something about himself that is meant to draw you into deeper dependence on him and deeper joy in him.

He could have taught whatever he was teaching about the kingdom of God back in town. He didn't need to lead anyone into the wilderness to do it. But leading the people into the wilderness and then miraculously multiplying bread would certainly have shown them something about what he had just claimed about himself. Those of us who know the Exodus story should remember God's people following the pillar of cloud and fire through the wilderness to desolate places where there was no bread, and God, by a miracle, sending manna from heaven to feed them. Jesus is calling up that Old Testament story to show the people who he is.

Likewise, we need to see the ways that, when we call on him, we want him to be someone we can predict and control. We want to tell him what we need and have him give us what we asked for. Tim Keller put it this way: God will either give us what we ask for or give us what we would have asked for if we knew everything he knows. He has seen our need and thought ahead creatively about how to meet it, and that's exactly what he does.

"Jesus then took the loaves, and when he had given thanks, he distributed them to those who were seated. So also the fish, as much as they wanted." Here we should pay attention again to what John leaves out. We've looked at what he uniquely includes: the grass, and the fact that Jesus raised the question, not the disciples. That mattered to him. Now what does he leave out?

John says Jesus distributed the food to those who were seated. The other Gospels say the disciples distributed it: Jesus multiplied the loaves and fish and gave them to the disciples to hand out. John says Jesus took the loaves, gave thanks and distributed them to those who received them. He conspicuously leaves himself and the other disciples out of the distribution altogether. Why? Why wouldn't John mention that he, an insider, was one of the ones called to hand out the bread?

There are a few things I'd say. First, and I know this might be awkward, this is for every pastor, for me, and for anyone who ever sets out to carry out obediently what the Lord calls them to do, anyone who takes up a call of Christ or a cause of Christ. Hear this: we are mere distributors of the bread. We are not multipliers of bread. We don't multiply it. We only hand it out. Our role is to come to Jesus with empty hands, take what he gives us, and give it to whomever he tells us to give it. That is the role of everyone in Christian ministry. When you take up a cause of Christ to love anyone, you receive from Jesus and then give it away. Receive from Jesus and give it away.

This is a high point for me this morning, and I hope for you: Jesus will never call his people to give what they have not first received from him. He will never call his people to hand out what they have not first tasted. We'll come back to that. But hear it: the Christian life is receiving from Jesus and then giving it away. And the reason you can give it away is your confidence that Jesus will give to you again. It is by the hand of Jesus that my cup is filled, so I can pour out my cup for others. So John leaves himself out of the story altogether. He doesn't want to be seen as the bread guy. He wants Jesus to be seen as the bread guy.

"And when they had eaten their fill, he told his disciples, 'Gather up the leftover fragments, that nothing may be lost.'" This is a second point: Jesus always gives us our fill. "Their fill" is important because of what it doesn't mean. It means no one was still hungry after the meal. But it also means no one packed a to-go bag. They ate their fill, and Jesus didn't fill their storehouses so they wouldn't need him tomorrow. He met their need today so they would need him again tomorrow. That's how we receive from Christ.

Early on, as I was being trained for ministry, a wise man told me that the people you minister to never need what you can dig out of the back-seat cushions of the car. You know how you go out for a great meal, bring home leftovers, the kids spill some, and it ends up in the seat cushions? People don't need you to look back on that great meal, say, oh, I think I have some left over, dig around in the back seat and offer it to them as if it will be as good for them as it was for you. The Christian life of ministry is actively receiving from the Lord and giving people what he is actively giving you.

You'll see this a lot in Christian ministry, and you'll do it a lot in your own lives. Even this morning, when I wanted to tell you about God's faithfulness in my life, I told you a story that's nearly ten years old. My son is almost ten now. That's a pattern we follow often. He does want us to look back and remember his past faithfulness, but never only that. He also wants us to see how he is actively loving us and our souls today, so that we can call others into experiencing him the way we are. So Jesus fed them their fill. Nothing less, but nothing more. No to-go bags. He wants to be our daily fill.

We also see what Jesus never does to those who follow him, pursue him and seek him: he never neglects those who are neglecting themselves in that pursuit. Jesus does call us, not in this passage but several times in this Gospel, to take up our cross and deny ourselves if we would follow him. And when we deny ourselves, take up our crosses and follow him, neglecting our own needs as we trust him, he never neglects to meet our needs. The people who followed him into the wilderness with no plan for eating didn't need to factor that in, because Jesus did it for them. That's important to remember, because there will be times when Jesus says, follow me into a place where you can't see how following me will provide your daily bread. John wants us to see in this story that when we neglect ourselves to follow him, he never neglects to meet our needs.

After everyone has eaten their fill, after Jesus has multiplied a boy's lunch into bread and fish for more than five thousand people, he tells his disciples in verse 12, "Gather up the leftover fragments, that nothing may be lost." "So they gathered them up and filled twelve baskets with fragments from the five barley loaves left by those who had eaten." Pop quiz. How many disciples were there? Twelve. How many baskets of leftovers? Twelve.

This is it. This detail is in all four Gospels. They all loved this part. Jesus hadn't forgotten that they went to that place in the first place because the disciples were hungry. He says, I've got a basket for each of you. I haven't used up my provision, my grace or my miracles in having you serve them. As I just said, I don't call you to hand out to others what I'm not also offering to you. I don't tell you to go tell people to take and eat what I'm not inviting you to take and eat. Twelve baskets left over, so that they could sit down and enjoy a meal with Jesus.

How many of us are running ourselves ragged telling dry stories about a Jesus we haven't tasted and seen for ourselves? Telling old tales we've grown far from, that we don't really believe anymore because we haven't sought his face? Holding on to the times we felt we pursued him and he turned his back on us, pushing them out of mind so we can just be good Christians and move on? Christ gives us a place to lay that down, to seek his face and see him in that lowly place, so that he can minister to us today. And often, what people need most is for you to identify with their hunger. That's what Jesus invites us into.

Isn't it interesting that Jesus didn't feed the disciples until after he fed the crowd? I'd suggest, though the text doesn't say it, that the reason John left himself out of the story as a distributor of the bread is that he didn't see himself on Jesus' side of the miracle. He identified with the crowd. He was one of the hungry people too, and with each loaf and each fish he handed out, his faith grew that his own need would be met.

I point that out because in the Christian life things don't always happen in the order we think they should. I said Jesus never calls us to hand out what we haven't tasted, and yet here he let them taste it after they had handed it out. Sometimes obedience comes before faith, and sometimes faith comes before obedience. Sometimes Jesus says, do what I tell you. I know you don't understand. I know you're hungry. Do it anyway. And in doing it he gives you faith as a gift, so you can see him more fully. Other times he shows you first, so that you go out excitedly and tell everyone else. Either way, he is good.

Verse 14: "When the people saw the sign that he had done, they said, 'This is indeed the Prophet who is to come into the world!'" That comes from Deuteronomy 18:15, where Moses says, "The LORD your God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among you." Because Jesus fed them, the people see this sign. Remember, they were following him in the first place because they had seen him heal people. But he fed them bread, and they say, this is the Prophet who is to come into the world. They see him rightly. And what do they do? This is huge.

Verse 15: "Perceiving then that they were about to come and take him by force to make him king, Jesus withdrew again to the mountain by himself." This is the radical nature of Jesus. Last week we saw a Jesus who made himself so big, so obviously God, displaying himself so mightily, that no one would think you could take him by force. Today he hands out bread and fish to a crowd, making himself so approachable that he seems like the kind of God you could take by force. Think how humbly Jesus must have presented himself for them to want to seize him and make him king.

I'll save the careful exposition of this for next week. After Jesus walks on the water, the people follow him to the other side of the lake, and he preaches a sermon to the people whose bellies he filled and clears up what's going on here. I want to let him do that. But for this week, know this: Jesus made himself so low, so relatable, so caring, so compassionate, so touchable, so punchable, so human, and yet so fully God, that the crowd thought, let's grab him.

Does Jesus ever feel seizable to you? I'm not encouraging you to sin here. But does his love ever feel so close, does he ever feel so near, that it seems you could just grab hold of him? He put himself in that position. He orchestrated the whole thing. He will correct them where they were wrong, but they were starting to see something true.

Verses 16 to 21: "When evening came, his disciples went down to the sea, got into a boat, and started across the sea to Capernaum. It was now dark, and Jesus had not yet come to them. The sea became rough because a strong wind was blowing. When they had rowed about three or four miles, they saw Jesus walking on the sea and coming near the boat, and they were frightened. But he said to them, 'It is I; do not be afraid.' Then they were glad to take him into the boat, and immediately the boat was at the land to which they were going."

I said at the start that a preacher has to decide where to preach things, and John conspicuously puts this in the middle of the bread story: first the miracle, then the walking on water, then the sermon about the bread. He wanted it in the middle. I could preach it by itself, now or next week. I'm preaching it now because it happened the same day, that evening. The disciples watched Jesus withdraw by himself after the miracle, and they set out across the sea without him.

As they cross toward Capernaum, it gets dark. Jesus still hasn't come to them. The sea gets rough. A strong wind is blowing. They have rowed three or four miles. The lake is somewhere between seven and twelve miles across, so they're far from shore, about halfway. And they see Jesus walking on the sea, coming near the boat, and they are frightened.

Here again John conspicuously leaves something out. Matthew and Mark tell us that when Jesus got into the boat, the wind ceased, and Matthew tells of Peter walking out on the water to meet him. They highlight Jesus as Lord over nature. John says nothing about any of that. Why? What did he want us to see?

They were frightened, but he said to them, "It is I; do not be afraid." Then they took him into the boat, and the story is over. What did John see that the other disciples didn't emphasize? For John, the story ended when Jesus got into the boat. The miracle wasn't that Jesus changed the circumstances that frightened them. The miracle was that Jesus did whatever he had to do to get into the boat with them. That's the last point of the sermon this week: Jesus will do whatever he needs to do to get to you in your hour of need. And in that hour, the greater thing is that he gets into the circumstances with you, not that he changes them.

I'd need more Bible to preach that fully, but this morning I want you to see that for John, the story was finished when Jesus got into the boat. One of my favorite preachers, John Piper, describes the glory of Jesus something like this. Picture Jesus as the radiating source of glory in the universe, like the sun. His glory comes down on us like a beam, the way the sun's heat reaches the earth, and plants grow and your skin soaks up vitamin D. It would be a fool who looks at his skin and says, I love it, looks at the plants and says, I love it, and never lifts his eyes to the source of the glory and goodness that produces it all.

Next week we'll see the people chase Jesus down because they want more bread. And in the other Gospels the disciples marvel that the wind stops. But John rightly sees the source of it all: Jesus getting into the boat with them.

I want you to be encouraged this morning. The wind did cease when Jesus got into the boat. John just didn't mention it, so I won't focus on it. Often when Jesus shows up, our circumstances change radically for the better. But often he simply enters into them with us. Often the circumstances don't get better, and yet Jesus walks step by step with us.

My fear is that in the American church, when Jesus makes himself approachable, when he shows us that he cares and is interested in our day-to-day, we so easily shrink him again, stripping away his kingship and his deity, and treat him as merely useful. We start to treat him like an ATM or a genie: rub the lamp when we need something, because his main job is to give me bread. Jesus doesn't allow that. What Jesus offers is himself.

And here is how I know that he often enters our circumstances without giving us exactly what we asked for. What the people wanted when they tried to seize him and make him king, and what even the disciples wanted until the very end, still confused about it, was for him to establish an earthly reign: a king who would fill their bellies every day, fight off their oppressors and give them earthly prosperity. He said no to all of that. Instead he laid down his life and let the oppressors crucify him, because he was after a far greater throne. Many of us would settle for a Jesus who takes a smaller throne, a throne we build for him. Jesus won't allow it. Instead John shows us a Jesus who, not because of what he can do, not because of the bread or the sea and the waves, simply says, it is I; do not be afraid, and gets into the boat. For John, that finishes the story.

That's why I ended on this story this week rather than holding it for next week. It felt like the right end to the story of a Jesus who has just fed the crowd: be with me. Taste and see. Be full. Have your rest. Have your lunch with me. That's what he shows us today, and that's what I want to invite us into. This week, don't put Jesus so far away that you can't relate to him, and don't bring him so low that you think he needs to obey you. See that he places himself exactly where he ought to be, which is the best place for us. He is only ever after our good.

We don't make Jesus king. Jesus is the King. I'll say it again: we don't make Jesus a king. He is the King. When the people started clamoring to make him anything, he withdrew by himself. He doesn't need our help to be who he is. He is who he is. His invitation to us is not to make him anything or partner with him in becoming anything, but simply to receive him for who he is. And in receiving him for who he is, our needs are met, and more. If you feel hungry this morning, if you feel you're chasing him and he's going places you don't understand, and you're getting tired of following him through the wilderness, know that wherever he lands, it will be a place with much grass. The perfect spot.

So, in conclusion, there's really one charge this morning. There is a real, tangible need in your life today. I don't want you to go back ten years, unless ten years ago there was something you decided never to revisit because it damaged your walk with Jesus. If you need to do that, do it. But I want you to think about today: your hungry belly, or, like the crowd chasing Jesus, your need for healing, some area of your life where you've felt, my God can't be bothered with this, because he is the God of kingdoms, the God of the universe, the great I AM. I want you to see a Jesus who looks at sheep without a shepherd, leads them to green pastures and shows himself to be their great provider. Talk to him today. Seek your provision from him today. And trust that whatever his answer, he saw your need before you asked, he saw you before you sought him, and he has creatively planned to meet your need in exactly the way you need. Then turn to someone near you and pray over them for the same.
