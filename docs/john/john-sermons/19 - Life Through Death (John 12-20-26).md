# Life Through Death (John 12:20-26)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 12:20-26

---

This morning's passage, John 12, begins with a detail you're tempted to skip, frankly, because as I wrote this sermon I broke the text down into four central statements of Jesus. When Jesus speaks, he does all the work for me. As a preacher, I can't add to what he says.

I just come up here and say, and he said this. So you'd like to skip ahead, because he makes four big statements. Let me give them to you up front. First, he says, "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified."

Second, "Unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit." Third, "Whoever loves his life loses it, and whoever hates his life in this world will keep it for eternal life." And last, "If anyone serves me, he must follow me."

And where he is, there his servant will be also. That gives us plenty to preach this morning. But what we can't skip is that he says these things in response to something. Something has happened that brings these statements to Jesus' lips. And my prayer as we look at his response is that we'll see three things this morning. First, and above all, why Jesus died.

Number one, why Jesus died. Number two, for whom he died. And last, how he calls us to respond to those first two truths. Let's look at our text together.

Verse 20: "Now among those who went up to worship at the feast were some Greeks." As we saw last week, the feast is the Passover. And coming up to worship at this feast are some Greeks. The Passover is a distinctly Jewish feast.

Yet some Greeks are coming up to worship at it. Because that's unusual, people have speculated all sorts of things about who these Greeks were. Much of it is wrong and some of it is right, and I'll tell you what's right. The Greeks here are, wait for it, Greeks.

That should go without saying. But because they're coming up to worship and want to observe the Passover, some have suggested they were Hellenistic Jews: Jews heavily influenced by Greek culture who followed Greek customs but were really Jews. Others say they were Greeks who had been circumcised and fully converted to Judaism. But there are different words for "Greeks" in the Greek. John's word here, Hellēnes, is the word the New Testament uses for Gentiles, people who were not Jews. It's not the word for Greek-speaking Jews.

These are Gentiles, and they've come up to worship at the feast. The reason I'd push back a little on the idea that they were circumcised converts who had fully taken on Jewish tradition is whom they look for when they arrive. Verse 21: "So these came to Philip, who was from Bethsaida in Galilee, and asked him, 'Sir, we wish to see Jesus.'" They came up to the very Passover feast at which the authorities were plotting to kill Jesus.

And these Greeks come looking for him gladly. They're there to see him. This is unusual, and it's magnificent. If you've been with us for a while, you know the great theme of our message: it has been the will of the Father from eternity past to bring all things under the rule of Jesus Christ, so that every knee will bow and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord.

That's what he's ultimately doing as he purchases a people for himself and then scatters them among the peoples of the earth to spread the gospel: he is gathering for himself a people from every tribe, tongue and nation. And here, before Jesus has even gone to the cross, some Greeks make their way to the Passover feast looking for Jesus. They want to see him. So they find Philip and say, "Sir, we wish to see Jesus."

Verse 22: "Philip went and told Andrew." Maybe I'm reading too much into it, but it seems to show us this was unusual. Philip didn't know what to do with it, so he goes and tells Andrew: Andrew, there are these Greeks who've come up to worship, and they want to see Jesus.

What should we do? Philip doesn't want to go alone, and Andrew doesn't want to go alone, so they both go to Jesus. "Andrew and Philip went and told Jesus." The Greeks go to Philip, Philip goes to Andrew, and Philip and Andrew go to Jesus. Jesus, some Greeks have come up to worship, and they're asking for you. And Jesus answers with these four statements.

I set up this context because what we're about to hear from Jesus is his response to the fact that the nations have started to come. People of Greek birth have come up to the Jewish feast seeking Jesus. These are the earliest stirrings of the church among the Gentiles. You are Gentiles. This is the first movement toward your salvation and mine, toward the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ reaching beyond the Jewish city center. And this is what he says.

First, verse 23: "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified." Jesus, there are these Greeks who've come up to worship, and they're asking to see you. And Jesus answers, it's time. It's time.

The time has come. "The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified." That's his response. The Greeks are here? It's time.

Connect it. My ministry is starting to draw the nations to me. It's time. This is what I came for. It's time for the Son to be glorified.

Notice that Jesus uses the word "glorified," because he's going to make it very clear what he means. When Jesus teaches, I generally try to preach the whole teaching as one sermon, because that's how the original hearers would have received it. I didn't do that this time.

So I'll borrow from next week. In this same conversation, verses 31 to 33, he says, "Now is the judgment of this world; now will the ruler of this world be cast out. And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself." And John adds, "He said this to show by what kind of death he was going to die." So within this conversation he makes it very clear what he means when he says, it's time for me to be glorified. He's saying, it's time for me to be crucified.

It's time for the Son to be lifted up. It's time to die. The hour of my death has arrived. And yet he calls it his glorification: crucifixion, death, as the way he is glorified.

I'm going to cross-reference Philippians 2, and I'll go back and forth between our passage and that one several times, so track with me. Paul writes, starting in verse 3: "Do nothing from selfish ambition or conceit, but in humility count others more significant than yourselves. Let each of you look not only to his own interests, but also to the interests of others. Have this mind among yourselves, which is yours in Christ Jesus, who, though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men."

"And being found in human form, he humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross. Therefore God has highly exalted him and bestowed on him the name that is above every name, so that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father." That's what Jesus means when he says the hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified: he would be raised up and highly exalted above every name that is named, so that every knee would bow and every tongue confess.

He's talking about his glorification through death. His glorification through death. How? Verse 24: "Truly, truly, I say to you, unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone." Pause there.

He's talking about himself. He is the grain of wheat. Unless I go into the earth and die, I remain alone. I remain alone. But we know Jesus is never alone. He and the Father are one.

So alone in what, Jesus? Alone in what? Unless he goes into the earth and dies, he remains alone in his righteousness before the Father. He remains the only human being who has lived a perfect life, who is credited with perfectly glorifying the Father and with perfect union with the Father, who has the fullness of the righteousness of God on him, the full approval of the Father, a seat at the table with the Father in the kingdom of heaven.

Unless I die, he says, unless I go into the earth, I remain alone. You can't come with me unless I die. That's what Jesus is saying. So why did he die? Why did he die?

That's question number one. He died so that he would not remain alone in the ways I've just described, but would instead bear much fruit: "but if it dies, it bears much fruit." So who is the fruit? That was the second thing we wanted to answer. For whom did he die?

It's you. It's you. You are the fruit. When the Greeks were seeking him, he said, I have to go and die now, because what they're seeking they can't have unless I die. And what everyone before those Greeks was seeking, they could not have unless he died.

To bear fruit, a church, a people wrapped up in his death, he had to go first. He had to go first. Why did he die? To bear fruit. Who is the fruit? You are the fruit.

He died for you. "Truly, truly, I say to you, unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit." Here we need to pause, because in verse 25 he takes a turn. He starts talking about what his fruit looks like.

What his fruit looks like. What Jesus describes in verse 25 and following is what the fruit looks like. It's a description. He is not describing how you become fruit. He has just told you how he bears fruit.

Jesus dies. He dies. Jesus goes into the grave and gives up his life as a ransom for many, who by faith alone, in him alone, are joined with him in his death and then joined with him in his resurrection. That's how you come to be fruit. You become fruit because of what someone else has done for you.

This is the great message of Christianity: you don't achieve right standing with the Father, but on the merits of Christ alone you can stand boldly before the throne of grace, because of what another has done for you that you could not do by your own merit or in your own strength. This is the message of Christianity, and you need to hear it first. If Jesus hadn't died for you, he would remain alone. Nobody joins his company of saints on account of their works.

They're joined to him on account of his death. Full stop. So once he has done it, what does it look like? Verses 25 and 26: "Whoever loves his life loses it, and whoever hates his life in this world will keep it for eternal life."

"If anyone serves me, he must follow me; and where I am, there will my servant be also. If anyone serves me, the Father will honor him." So on what basis does Jesus move from saying, what I do, I do to bear fruit, to ransom a church, a people, a remnant for myself,

to talking about how you tell the fruit on the vine from the fruit rotting on the ground? What he's describing here is how we work out our salvation. That's why I brought in Philippians 2. Let's look at it again.

From verse 1, Paul writes, "So if there is any encouragement in Christ, any comfort from love, any participation in the Spirit, any affection and sympathy, complete my joy by being of the same mind, having the same love, being in full accord and of one mind." And then he calls us to do nothing from selfish ambition or conceit, but in humility to count others more significant than ourselves, having the mind of Christ Jesus, who did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped and held onto, but emptied himself, took the form of a servant, and humbled himself to the point of death, even death on a cross. So, flipping back to John: when Jesus says that if anyone serves him, he must follow him, and he has made it completely clear in this very conversation where he's going, he's saying, follow me into death.

Follow me into death. It's a much easier message for me to stand up here and give you the other half of this truth: Jesus died so that you might live. Yes and amen. Jesus died so that you might live. But Jesus died so that you might die, so that you might live.

Jesus died so that you might die, so that you might live. Jesus lives so that you might live. It's those who join him in his death who join him in his resurrection. I'm not talking about your physical death. The hour of that is already written.

Jesus is talking about the death of the flesh, the death of yourself. As Paul says, "It is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me." The death Christ died, he died to sin, once for all, and the life he lives, he lives to God. Because Jesus on the cross is not a symbol, a model for you to look to, an encouragement.

Jesus was nailed to a cross not only to absorb the wrath of God; he absorbed the wrath of God with your sin on him. And your sin is your very nature. You inherited it from Adam. You were born into sin. Your very self was nailed to the cross with Christ, and the wrath of God you were due was poured out on Christ on the cross, so that the old you, sinful you, was nailed to the cross with Jesus and the wrath of God was poured out on it.

You've been judged, and you didn't make it. The judgment of God was poured out on you, and you didn't make it. Praise God. "The old has passed away; behold, the new has come." You've been raised to new life by the Holy Spirit, who has taken up residence in you.

So Jesus starts to talk about what it looks like. How do you know you're fruit? Because you follow him. But where is he going? To a cross.

So I'm asking you: are you carrying your cross? Because if the way to everlasting life is through death, and Jesus has said so in no uncertain terms, then you don't get to bring your old life into the new life. It isn't both. We don't add Jesus to our life. We lay down our life, and then he takes it up again.

And when he does, he takes control. For Jesus to be your Savior, Jesus must be your Lord. And if he's Lord, he's the one directing your life. That's what death means. It means you aren't the god of you anymore; you yield to Christ as your God.

So, Philippians 2:9: "Therefore God has highly exalted him." And then verses 12 and 13: "Therefore, my beloved, as you have always obeyed, so now, not only as in my presence but much more in my absence, work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in you, both to will and to work for his good pleasure." Work out your salvation with fear and trembling. Adam, I thought you just said that I'm reconciled to God by the merits of Jesus Christ's life, death and resurrection alone, that I'm made right by the merits of another. That's what you just said.

Now you're telling me to work out my salvation with what? Fear and trembling? What are we talking about? We're talking about death. If the way to life is death, and death isn't causing you fear and trembling, you're probably not dying.

Working out your salvation with fear and trembling looks like taking stock of your life and offering it to Christ in worship, laying it down so that he might take lordship of your life. You might remember the story of the rich young ruler from Mark 10, who runs up to Jesus eagerly and says, "Good Teacher, what must I do to inherit eternal life?" And Jesus says to him, first of all, "Why do you call me good? No one is good except God alone."

That's the first thing he says. And if only God is good, then you have no business calling me good unless you're ready to call me God. So I don't do the good teacher thing. It's good God.

But that aside, he says, you know the commandments. And the man gets excited: I've kept them since my youth. "And Jesus, looking at him, loved him, and said to him, 'You lack one thing: go, sell all that you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me.'" And the man went away sorrowful, for he had great possessions.

So you can come eagerly to Jesus. You can ask him, I want eternal life; tell me how. He can give you the commandments, and you can say, I'll follow the rules.

But when he demands it all, when he puts his finger on the one thing you won't let go into the grave, you walk away sorrowful at the final hour while Jesus looks at you with love, because you counted the cost and decided it was too great. So here in Paul we see a picture of working out your salvation with fear and trembling. It made me think of a number of passages. Because your perfect, holy God, who by the power of his word brought all things into existence and sustains them by the word of his power, this God who is sovereign over all, who made you and has numbered the hairs on your head, who claims lordship over your life, is at work.
