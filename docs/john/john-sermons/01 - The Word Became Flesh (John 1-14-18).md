# The Word Became Flesh (John 1:14-18)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 1:14-18

---

We're still in chapter one of John, and this morning we finish the prologue, verses 14 through 18. We've been taking a slow walk through it. Before John gets into the history, the details of the life, death and resurrection of Jesus, he writes this prologue, a beautiful, poetic, soaring piece of worship. We'll spend the better part of a year and a half walking through the details of this book, but John stopped before the specifics to fix his eyes on who Jesus really is and why he bothers to write any of this down. That's why we're taking our time.

Here is where the prologue has brought us. In the very beginning was the Word, Jesus, and the Word was with God, and the Word was himself God. He was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him, and without him nothing was made that was made. In him was life, and the life was the light of men. That light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it. Then came a man sent from God, named John, as a witness to bear witness about the light, so that all might believe through him. He was not the light. The true light, who gives light to everyone, was coming into the world. He was in the world, and the world was made through him, yet the world did not know him. He came to his own, and his own people did not receive him.

This is beautiful, high, exalting language, and it fixes our eyes on a Jesus who was there in the beginning. John paints a picture of Jesus Christ present at creation, speaking into the void over the face of the deep, creating by his very word. All things were created by him, through him and for him. When we think about the God of creation, the image that should come to mind is Jesus, who is himself the image of the invisible God. In him is life, and that very life is the light of men. The picture keeps growing: Jesus is the source of all things.

The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it. That's an important place to start. When the light came into the world he had made, the world did not know him, and his own people rejected him. Until the light and life of Christ shine on our hearts, we are mere darkness to be overcome. But the promise that the darkness has not overcome the light tells us that Jesus was not plan B. He wasn't written into the story two thousand years ago because the first plan went wrong and God had to call an audible and send Jesus in to fix things. From the beginning, Jesus Christ was speaking this whole story. There is one plan of the one God, and he has always been the one writing it.

And if Jesus is writing it, the darkness cannot overcome him. From all eternity the purpose of God has been to bring all things to bow at the name of Jesus Christ, to magnify his glory by magnifying his grace through the gospel. If that is the purpose of all things, then not even the darkness can overcome it. Jesus, writing this story as plan A, makes all things, sin, darkness and even death, bow and serve that chief end: that he would be glorified, that Christ would be magnified.

But the prologue leaves a hard truth hanging in the air. We are the darkness the light has to pierce. Apart from the regeneration Christ gives, we are the rebels and the enemies. So when he entered the story and we try to see him rightly, we have to say: Jesus, you are God. Not only the God who spoke at creation, but the God who walked with Adam and Eve in the garden in the cool of the day. You are the God from whom they hid among the trees when they sinned, and the God who promised them that a Savior would come and trample the head of the serpent. You are the God who sent them out of the garden. You are the God to whom the blood of Abel cried out for justice when his brother Cain struck him down. Jesus, you are this God. You are the God of the flood, the God who made covenants with Noah and Abraham and David. And yours is the blood of the new covenant. Jesus, you are the God of the Bible, the true light who gives light to everyone. But when you came into the story, we did not know you. We rejected you. We were mere darkness to be overcome.

Then comes the promise of verses 12 and 13: "But to all who did receive him, who believed in his name, he gave the right to become children of God, who were born, not of blood nor of the will of the flesh nor of the will of man, but of God." Jesus said, "Unless one is born again he cannot see the kingdom of God." Right here in the prologue, John lays the gospel over us. By the time we finish this morning, everything you need to understand to be brought from death to life, from darkness into light, will have been preached to you. We'll spend the next year and a half walking through how it came to be and how it was lived out, but everything you need is here.

So I want you to hear it now. If the light of Christ has not shone on you, if you have not received him and believed in his name, then you are darkness to be overcome. That is where you stand today. But to all who receive him and believe in his name, he gives the right to become children of God. I know a call to faith at the start of a sermon is unusual. Let's do it anyway. Paul says he is not ashamed of the gospel, because it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, and yet to those who are perishing it is a fragrance of death. The gospel has just been preached to you. Let it be the power of salvation for you. All who believe in his name become children of God, receive new birth by the Spirit, and become new creations. If that is not you yet, hear me: nothing stands in your way. You can receive Jesus Christ, be made a child of God and be brought out of darkness into light. And if this is the ten-thousandth time you've heard it, I pray you're encouraged to fix your eyes on Jesus once more for all the promises we're about to receive.

Let's work through the passage. I'll read the whole thing, and then we'll go verse by verse. "And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth. (John bore witness about him, and cried out, 'This was he of whom I said, "He who comes after me ranks before me, because he was before me."') For from his fullness we have all received, grace upon grace. For the law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ. No one has ever seen God; God the only Son, who is at the Father's side, he has made him known."

Back to verse 14, sentence by sentence. "And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us." The Word, the God of creation I've just described, the eternal God of the Bible, became flesh. And don't skip what John said one verse earlier: those who believe are born "not ... of the will of the flesh." He entered into the very thing he was rescuing us from. He became flesh.

This is hard, so stay with me. I don't know whether you get a Men in Black picture in your head the way I did while I was preparing this. When we think about Jesus becoming flesh, something works against us. Maybe you wouldn't put it this way, but I think as we talk you'll find you're with me. We say, yes, God became flesh, but we mean he became humanoid, human-ish, sort of human. He looked like us and walked among us. He could certainly fool you into thinking he was human, but inside that human-looking body, something else is working the controls, like the alien in the movie. Human in form, sure, but not really human. Maybe you've never had that exact thought. I'm artistic, so I think in pictures. But every time you approach God as though he can't relate to you or understand you, every time you act as if you can't draw near because he couldn't really know what it's like, you're missing this central doctrine: he really did become fully human. He became flesh.

How can we know that? Because Jesus, in his goodness and kindness, knew we would doubt. He knew we would wonder what we'd find if we cracked him open. So he let us crack him open, and we discovered that he bleeds red blood. When he cries, he cries salty tears. When he sweats, it's salty sweat. When you punch him, he bruises. When the Word took on flesh, he became punchable. He cried. He bruised. He died. In every way, Jesus Christ became human.

There's a second challenge. We may not say it in these words, but work it out with me. We say, God, you don't really know what it's like to be human. We put ourselves back in the garden: yes, you're the architect, you had a design, and you walked with us when everything was as it should be. But I remember how it went. Once we sinned, you sent us out. You said we couldn't dwell with you anymore, and as we move through the Scriptures, you set up all these hoops to jump through before we can come near you. Surely you don't know what it's like to be us. So surely I'm justified in taking things into my own hands to chase comfort, satisfaction, joy, fulfillment, righteousness, whatever.

Go back to the garden with me, and remember that it was Jesus Christ, the Word himself, speaking all things into existence. In creating man, God breathed the breath of life into him and said, "Let us make man in our image." Humankind was created for a purpose. Our chief end is to image God. What is an image for? To image. We are mirrors, made to reflect the grace and glory and character of our God. But when sin corrupted the image, we no longer did perfectly what we were designed to do. So does the original designer know better than we do what it means to be human? Absolutely. Yet now that things are broken, we charge him with not understanding.

So what did he do? He entered the story again, this time in human form. This is hard, so track with me. Jesus Christ didn't take on human form temporarily. He didn't dwell with us for a while to earn some credibility. He has chosen to exist forever as a human being. Today he is seated bodily at the right hand of the Father, always representing the case of humankind. We have a great high priest seated at the right hand of God.

Here's why it matters. When we say that Jesus Christ took on flesh, we aren't talking about a one-time mission. We're talking about God identifying himself with humankind once again. So when we say, Jesus, you couldn't possibly know what it's like to be human, he can answer: no, you don't know what it means to be human. In taking on flesh and living a life that perfectly honored the Father, Jesus has become the highest definition of what it means to be human. Think how often he said it: I don't do my own will, but the will of the one who sent me. I don't speak my own words, but the words of the Father who sent me. We hear those lines and think he's describing his divine nature. He's describing his human nature. He is doing what humans were created to do: image God, reflect his glory and grace, and carry out his purposes in creation perfectly. Jesus in the flesh is the summit of humanity. We don't get to say to him, you're the alien, you don't know what it means to be human. He says to us, you don't know what it means to be human. Look to me.

He took on flesh and identified with us, and he didn't only become flesh. He dwelt among us. As God walked with Adam and Eve in the garden in the cool of the day, Jesus dwelt among us. That is no small doctrine. It means that everything you experience, he experienced. Not only in the wilderness during the forty days of temptation, but day after day: mockery, jeering and betrayal, hunger, fatigue and thirst. He knew laughter and friendship. He knew the death of friends and people he loved. He lived in this actual world and walked on this actual ground. So when you talk to God as though he isn't relatable, isn't approachable, doesn't know you and doesn't really understand, you have these doctrines wrong. The Word became flesh and dwelt among us.

That ought to be amazing news. But by dwelling among us, what did he open himself to? We didn't want him. By dwelling among us, he put himself willingly into the hands of sinners to be crucified for our transgressions. He couldn't be crucified for us unless he entered all the way in, and he did. He made himself low, taking the form of a servant, obedient to the point of death on a cross. So when Hebrews says, "For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin," we know who is at the Father's right hand today. It is Jesus, dwelling bodily. He lived the perfect human life, the life we were designed to live. He died at our hands the death we deserved for failing to live it. And he pleads for us forever at the Father's right hand, fully able to sympathize with us in our weakness.

Then John writes, "and we have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth." We have seen his glory? Remember Moses. He says to God, show me your glory. God answers that he will make all his goodness pass before Moses and proclaim his name over him, but Moses cannot see his face, for no one can see God's face and live. So God hides Moses in the cleft of the rock, passes by, speaks his name over him, and lets him see his back but not his face. And here John says, we have seen his glory.

What Moses was told he could not see, John says he has seen. Matthew, Mark and Luke tell the story of the Mount of Transfiguration, where John, with James and Peter, saw Jesus transfigured, saw him in his glorified state. And standing there with him were Elijah and Moses. So when John says "we have seen his glory," he means it as literally as you can take it. He saw Jesus glorified, standing with Moses and Elijah, with his friends James and Peter beside him. And notice who was there: Moses, the man God told that no one could see his face and live, now looking on the glory of Jesus. That's part of what John meant.

But in case you feel you're missing out, that's not all he meant. He also meant it of all of us together. In taking on flesh and dwelling among us, Jesus showed us his glory. When we see him kneel and wash the disciples' feet, including the feet of Judas, his betrayer, we have seen his glory. When we see him give sight to the blind and hearing to the deaf, heal the sick and raise the dead; when we see him multiply loaves and fish to feed the crowds; when we see him speak to the storm and it goes still, and walk on the water; when he is crucified at Calvary and rises victorious three days later; when he appears for forty days to a great cloud of witnesses and teaches them how the Law, the Prophets and the Psalms all testify about him; when they watch him ascend to the right hand of the Father: we have seen his glory. We have seen his glory.

"John bore witness about him, and cried out, 'This was he of whom I said, "He who comes after me ranks before me, because he was before me."'" We met John the Baptist earlier in the prologue. John was conceived about six months before Jesus. His mother, Elizabeth, was a relative of Mary, and their sons were born about six months apart. John and Jesus were given different purposes. John's was to roll out the red carpet for Jesus: to prepare the way in the wilderness, as the prophet said, to announce that the Messiah was coming, and to call people to repent and be baptized for the forgiveness of sins.

But John came first. He was conceived first, his ministry started first, and he built a following first, all before Jesus. Yet here John cries out, "He who comes after me ranks before me, because he was before me." If Jesus wasn't conceived before you, born before you or in ministry before you, John, what do you mean, he was before you? He means what Jesus himself would say to the Jews: "Truly, truly, I say to you, before Abraham was, I am." And they picked up stones to throw at him. By the Spirit, John was allowed to see the truth about Jesus: this is the eternal God, who was there from the very beginning.

Paul says it this way in Colossians 1: "He is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation. For by him all things were created, in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible, whether thrones or dominions or rulers or authorities—all things were created through him and for him. And he is before all things, and in him all things hold together. And he is the head of the body, the church. He is the beginning, the firstborn from the dead, that in everything he might be preeminent. For in him all the fullness of God was pleased to dwell, and through him to reconcile to himself all things, whether on earth or in heaven, making peace by the blood of his cross." He who comes after me ranks before me, because he was before me. This is John's early declaration that Jesus Christ is himself God.

Verse 16: "For from his fullness we have all received, grace upon grace." This is one of my favorite verses in all of Scripture. When I first came to Christ, the Gospel of John was the first book of the Bible I ever read, and it took me forever to get through the prologue. Maybe that's why I'm so passionate about it. This verse struck me then: it is from his fullness that we have all received, grace upon grace. Fullness in Christ is an idea a lot of us need to fix our eyes on. I did when I first read John, and I do every time I come back here.

Some of our prayers don't make sense if this is true. When you say, Lord, if you'll just do this one thing for me, I won't ask for anything else, you're treating him like the keeper of a storehouse, rationing out grain and trying to stretch it to cover everyone's needs. You're not understanding that it's from his fullness we have all received. When you come to God like a beggar, saying, I don't deserve it, I know other people have much bigger problems, I'm just a lowly this or that, you're not seeing that it's from his fullness we all receive. And when unbelief about his fullness keeps you from praying at all and you start looking elsewhere, you'll find that nothing else offers fullness. There is one spring of living water that never runs dry, one cup that overflows with grace upon grace, and no matter how much you drink from it, it never runs out.

That exists only in Jesus. If we don't believe it, we'll settle for other things. We drink from filthy cisterns, or from wells that do run dry: people, status, sex, substances, money, power, esteem, reputation, whatever we hope will fill us, because we want to be full. The fullness of Christ comes from this: he has existed eternally, coequal in majesty and glory with God the Father and God the Spirit. The triune God of creation lives in shalom: fullness, wholeness, unity, true oneness. From that fullness we receive grace upon grace.

Then we have to get to the word "received." Maybe we start to believe that God in Christ is full and that everything we need is found in him, but we don't understand how we get it. We simply receive it, but we want to turn that word into "earned": from his fullness we have all earned grace upon grace. So you go to God and say, I've been good. I've followed the rules. Look at what I've done. You think you can leverage the gifts of God as if they weren't free and overflowing from his fullness. Or you think the storehouse is locked until you've done X, Y and Z: yes, he's full, but not for me. The fridge is full, but it's locked. Then we miss it. From his fullness we have all received, grace upon grace.

Notice too that verse 12 doesn't say "to all who did accept him." It says "to all who did receive him." It's a different word, and it matters. Think about Christmas or your birthday, when someone simply wants to love you. I think of a time someone wanted to care for my family in a season of need and dropped an anonymous envelope in our mailbox. It was a beautiful gesture. We didn't ask for it, didn't earn it, didn't even accept it. We received it. That is the nature of the grace of God and of the light of Christ. It shines in the darkness, and the darkness does not overcome it. This is what we call irresistible grace. We receive grace upon grace. So open your hands and receive it. Sit under the waterfall of his grace and believe in the name of Jesus.

Verses 17 and 18: "For the law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ. No one has ever seen God; God the only Son, who is at the Father's side, he has made him known." Notice the semicolon after "No one has ever seen God." End of thought. Then: God the only Son, at the Father's side, has made him known. Jesus says in John 14, "Whoever has seen me has seen the Father." The reason John can say we have seen his glory is that we have seen the face of Christ. Jesus Christ is God.

That is the sentence we need to grapple with in every area of life. Is he God or not? When I look on the face of Christ, do I see the glory of God or not? Is the image of the invisible God revealed to me in him, the exact imprint of his nature, or not? No one has ever seen God, but God the only Son, at the Father's side, has made him known. That is the claim of this passage, and it's the claim of Christianity. Everything we do rests on it. Is Jesus Christ God or not? That's what it all comes down to.

So back to the invitation I gave at the start. In the beginning was the Word, Jesus Christ. All things were made through him, and without him nothing was made. In him is life, and his life is the light of men, and his light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it. To all who receive him and believe in his name, he gives the right to become children of God, born not of the flesh but of God, a spiritual rebirth. In him we see his glory. The image of the invisible God is revealed to us, and we can relate to our God and know him, because he entered into flesh for us, dwelt among us and paid for us.

So what could I possibly charge you with after a declaration that high? After saying, receive Christ, what could I tell you to go and do? Nothing. Instead, let me read you the end of the high priestly prayer. It has stayed on my heart for weeks. Jesus prayed this just before he was betrayed to a Roman cross: "I do not ask for these only, but also for those who will believe in me through their word, that they may all be one, just as you, Father, are in me, and I in you, that they also may be in us, so that the world may believe that you have sent me. The glory that you have given me I have given to them, that they may be one even as we are one, I in them and you in me, that they may become perfectly one, so that the world may know that you sent me and loved them even as you loved me. Father, I desire that they also, whom you have given me, may be with me where I am, to see my glory that you have given me because you loved me before the foundation of the world. O righteous Father, even though the world does not know you, I know you, and these know that you have sent me. I made known to them your name, and I will continue to make it known, that the love with which you have loved me may be in them, and I in them."

What John begins at the start of his Gospel, Jesus doubles down on, a thousand times over, in his prayer at the end of his life. John describes a triune God, Father, Son and Spirit, one God in three persons, at work in a story that from eternity past was about magnifying his glory and his grace by redeeming a people through the blood of the cross. When we receive Christ, receive new life and new birth and become new creations, we are no longer our own. We are wrapped up in oneness with him. That's the fullness of Christ we have all received. We don't come to him on our own to collect our portion. We are wrapped up in his fullness, one with him: he in us, and we in him. That was the great prayer of the one who came for us.

So my charge to you is simply to be. To receive. Let me close with Paul's prayer in Ephesians 3. As I read it, bow your heart before the Father: "For this reason I bow my knees before the Father, from whom every family in heaven and on earth is named, that according to the riches of his glory he may grant you to be strengthened with power through his Spirit in your inner being, so that Christ may dwell in your hearts through faith—that you, being rooted and grounded in love, may have strength to comprehend with all the saints what is the breadth and length and height and depth, and to know the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge, that you may be filled with all the fullness of God." Now talk to the Lord, who offers you his fullness, and ask him to fill you with all the fullness of God by his Spirit in your inner being.
