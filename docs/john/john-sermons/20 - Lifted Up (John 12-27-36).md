In a picture, he said that unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit. So we answered the question, why did Jesus die? To bear much fruit.

Then we asked, who is the fruit? You are the fruit, a people for himself. He made a people out of those who were not a people. He produced you, his church, his people.

You are the fruit. And the third thing was how Jesus describes that fruit, what it looks like. This morning we pick up at verse 27, where Jesus says, "Now is my soul troubled."

"And what shall I say? 'Father, save me from this hour'?" I want you to track with me the whole time this morning: everything Jesus says in this passage and in last week's passage concerns what he called the hour, the hour that has come for the Son of Man to be glorified. Everything he says points to this hour. "Now is my soul troubled."

The word "soul" here is the same word translated "life" in verse 25. It's often used for the seat of the emotions, as theologians like to put it. A better word for you might be heart, or mind. Wherever you'd say the fullness of your emotions lives is what Jesus means when he says his soul is troubled.

The very seat of my emotions is agitated. It's troubled. Verses 27 and 28: "And what shall I say? 'Father, save me from this hour'? But for this purpose I have come to this hour."

"Father, glorify your name." We see something on display in these words that's hard for many of us to get our minds around: the humanity of Jesus. When God became flesh, when the triune God sent one person of the Godhead, the Son, Jesus, to earth to take on human flesh and become like us, he lived on this earth with two natures: his deity, his Godhood, and his humanity, taking the likeness of men. Okay.

One God in three persons was hard enough. Now one of the three persons has two natures. It gets hard. And honestly, I'm not going to stand up here and try to make it easy, because it isn't.

But Jesus, God the Son, took on human flesh. And here's why it matters for us this morning: this is something you can relate to. This is real agitation of soul as he fixed his eyes on the hour to come. To say it more directly, however uncomfortable it may make you: the hour of his death on the cross for your sin scared Jesus.

He trembled. In the garden of Gethsemane his sweat became like great drops of blood, so great was the trouble in his soul. He felt it. He understood what he was doing, and he responded to it rationally. This is terrifying.

That was his human nature on display. Why does it matter that we see it? Because we can look at the divine nature of Jesus and use it to discount everything else he shows us, until we're unimpressed with all he did in the flesh. If he's God, well, of course.

If he's God, well, of course. Think about it like this; I spent a lot of time thinking about it this week. We tend to put righteousness in two buckets.

There's the righteousness that's simply expected, that gets no applause. To give an easy example, if you work at a store, nobody claps for you at the end of every day you don't steal from the register.

But when you find a wallet on the ground and don't lift the twenty out of it before you return it, or you return it at all, or you find the ID, look up the address and take it to the owner, you're praised for going out of your way to do the right thing. Two buckets. One doesn't impress us. One does.

Here's why. When we see the chance you had to do wrong, and we see that you chose to do right, we find it praiseworthy. Now, I'm going into the depths of my thoughts here, but in our brokenness I think it comes down to this: we want glory. We praise righteousness that shows you're more righteous than someone else.

Everyone is expected not to murder. You don't get applause for every day you go on not murdering. But some acts of righteousness make you better than someone who merely doesn't murder, and those get applause. We're glory seekers. I think that's most often why we sort righteousness into two buckets.

If that sounds like heresy, come and argue with me about it; I'm convinced it's true. As for God, what makes him good, what makes him righteous? There's nobody policing God. Nobody polices God.

He is the God of all wisdom. He can see every possible outcome, every possible chain of actions. He has the power and the ability to do anything, and he is restrained only by the bounds of his own nature. Why doesn't God snap his fingers and make this whole thing disappear?

Because he's bound by the goodness of his own nature. Why do I say this? Because Jesus Christ was tempted for forty days in the wilderness by his enemy, Satan, wasn't he? Scripture says he was tempted as we are, "yet without sin." But how can Jesus be tempted if he was incapable of giving in to the temptation?

We're unimpressed with his temptation if we treat him as a machine, a robot, some alien being, like Spock, as if he were always just going to do the Father's will automatically. Jesus says, I and the Father are one. I do only the will of him who sent me.

I say only what my Father is saying. Our wills are perfectly aligned. And yet in the garden of Gethsemane Jesus can pray a real prayer: "not my will, but yours, be done." And in a moment like this one he says, now is my soul troubled; what shall I say? Father, save me from this hour? But for this purpose I have come to this hour.

Father, glorify your name. When Jesus asks, what shall I say? Father, save me from this hour? In the garden, he says it. And the answer is, yes, you may say it.

Say it. Go ahead. This is a small application, but take it anyway, because as I get to know you, I see you fighting the good fight. Listen: it is not sin for you to be tempted. After forty days of fasting, bread sounded great to Jesus.

But insofar as you are tempted and then choose to do the will of God, that is true righteousness. So what we see is Jesus bringing together in one person the perfect nature of God and a true human nature, weak flesh like ours, yet without sin. He walks among us as the God-man, Jesus, fully God and fully human at the same time. And the weakness of the flesh could not overcome him: he could be tempted in the flesh, and yet he was without sin. This is biblical.

That's all to say that Jesus wasn't smiling when he drank the cup of God's wrath. He wasn't glad to do it in the way we might imagine, as if it came naturally to him, as if he'd been looking forward to this moment since eternity past.

He wasn't saying, watch this, as he jumped off the roof into the pool. It wasn't nothing to him. It was as horrifying as it sounds. And even thinking about it, days before it happened, his soul is troubled.

And yet he's resolved not to be saved from the hour, but to fulfill its purpose. That's verse 27. Father, save me from this hour? No. For this purpose I have come to this hour.

What purpose? Verse 28: "Father, glorify your name." Father, glorify your name. Luke 2:14 is where the angels announce the birth of Jesus.

"Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace among those with whom he is pleased!" At his birth it was declared that his coming was for the glory of God in the highest, and for peace among the people of the earth with whom he is pleased. And here, as he comes to the end of his life, Jesus affirms it again: Father, glorify your name, as I bring peace to those with whom you are pleased. Why is he dying? To bear much fruit.

To bring peace to the nations. To make children out of rebels, sons out of enemies. As he comes to do what was announced over him at his birth, peace for the people with whom God is pleased, he says, it was for this reason that I came to this hour. So Father, do what we set out to do.

Glorify your name. Glorify your name. When we talk about the glory of God, it can feel like a vague term, can't it?

The glory of God. Who could define such a thing? The glory of God is the riches and majesty of who he is, shown and magnified. And Jesus is saying, Father, magnify your glory through me.

We are his image-bearers. We were made to reflect his image. We were made to glorify him, and in glorifying him to find our deepest satisfaction, our joy, our contentment. Like any tool, we work best doing what we were made to do.

To the extent that you reflect his glory, you enjoy peace, because you're doing what you were made for. So Jesus is after your joy when he is after the glory of the Father. Let's do it, he says. "Then a voice came from heaven: 'I have glorified it, and I will glorify it again.'"

This is the Father speaking. He has glorified his name in all things at all times; that's true. But he has also just raised Lazarus from the dead, and I'm fairly sure he means at least that.

We spent several weeks on the raising of Lazarus. Jesus had said that illness was for the glory of God, and then he raised Lazarus from the dead, a prelude to what was coming. And the Father says, I've glorified it, and I'll do it again.

Think about what that sounded like to Jesus' ears: to be reminded by the Father of what he had done with Lazarus, and what he would do again, as Jesus looked with terror at the hour of his death. The Father's assurance on him.

I'll do it again. I'll do it again. Jesus knew this, and still he was encouraged in his humanity by the reminder. That's a model for us. You think you know things?

You need to hear them again anyway. I know you know things. I know things. Sometimes I have to beg you to tell me things you think I already know. Tell me again.

I know. Obviously I know; I'm up here telling you. Tell me anyway. The Father tells him again anyway.

Do you think that when Jesus said, Father, glorify your name, the Father thought, I wasn't going to, but now that you've asked, I will? No. But he loves the prayer, and he loves to answer it. This is a peek into the relationship between the Father and the Son, and into how the Father sustained the Son in the flesh. So he speaks: I have glorified it, and I will glorify it again.

Verses 29 to 32: "The crowd that stood there and heard it said that it had thundered. Others said, 'An angel has spoken to him.' Jesus answered, 'This voice has come for your sake, not mine. Now is the judgment of this world; now will the ruler of this world be cast out. And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself.'" Notice the word "now." We tend to look at judgment either as something in the future or as something in the past, and we miss what he means by "now." When Jesus went to Calvary, to the cross, God the Father had already searched the whole world. He knew and had identified everyone who would be covered by the blood of Christ, everyone who would be declared spotless and righteous by the blood of Christ.

He gathered all of those people, you, the fruit, and heaped you on the shoulders of the Son. And then he poured out his wrath on the Son. And Jesus died.

Not only your sin. Hear this clearly. Often I think we say, what God did is take my sin, put it on Jesus, and when Jesus died, give me a clean slate. All my past sins are forgiven. I know we believe this.

In church history, some people put off baptism as late in life as they dared, because they believed water baptism was what washed away your past sins. They'd wait as long as possible without risking dying first. After baptism washed away your former sins, you'd keep up your righteousness and pay off any later sins through contrition, confessing to a priest or whatever it was. Obviously that's garbage theology. I hope it strikes you as garbage. But you don't get there overnight.

You get there from a small seed of unbelief: sure, the cross of Christ can forgive the sins I've already committed, but now that I'm forgiven, I've sinned again. Now what? That's a crisis. No. Your very self, your sinful nature, was nailed to Jesus.

So when he died, you died. And when he rose, the old you didn't rise. What rose was a new creation. You weren't just wiped clean and given another try. You've been made new.

You were made new, so that your very nature has changed. I know it doesn't always feel like your nature has changed. I know. But when you sin now, in your new nature, you're doing what is contrary to your nature. When you sinned before, you were doing what came naturally.

It made sense, because you were a sinner. Now when you sin, it makes no sense, because you've been given a new nature. The old self has died with Christ, and you've been raised to newness of life in the Spirit, which means that at any given moment, by the power of the Spirit, you can bear fruit. That's the gift right now. There's a book I love by Paul Miller called J-Curve.

You should read it if you get a minute. But it talks about this idea from last week, life through death: you die, and then you experience life. That's where the J comes from: down, then up.

He says we experience four J-curves. The first is Jesus' own: he literally died and literally rose. The second is the one we experience by faith: we are joined by faith to Jesus in his literal death and resurrection, so that our old self dies and we are raised to newness of life.

Then there's today, the now: we die daily and daily experience new life as we love people who are hard to love, as we obey Jesus, as we repent of our sin, as we put down our lesser righteousness and cling to him. That's the daily dying and rising of the Christian life. And ultimately we look ahead to the final J-curve, when we will literally die a bodily death and literally be given a glorified new body and raised to new life in perfect eternity with the Son. Four J-curves. I think sometimes we get confused because we think there's only one.

How do we cram them all into this passage? But we've been given more than this passage. We understand that it's an already-and-not-yet situation. So in verse 31 he says, "Now is the judgment of this world." That's what I mean when I tell you that your judgment, your death, is a historical event.

I know it's mind-boggling, but your judgment, your death, is a historical event. It already happened. When Jesus died on the cross, you died with him.
