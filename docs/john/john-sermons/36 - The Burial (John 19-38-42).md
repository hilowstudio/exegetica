# The Burial (John 19:38-42)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 19:38-42

---

We're in John 19, beginning in verse 38, and this morning I'm going to preach this passage twice. I'll tell you what's happening on the earth, and then what's happening below the surface of the earth. We'll start with the easy part: what's happening on the earth? We're going to be introduced to a couple of men. If you've been with us, Nicodemus will be reintroduced to you, and we'll meet a man named Joseph of Arimathea. We'll see their part in the burial of Jesus. We'll see Pilate again, and a centurion, and soldiers. We'll see a whole cast of characters, and we'll learn a good deal about the events of that day and why they were done.

I'm going to overlap a little with the crucifixion account to give us some continuity, because I want you to see it. In the crucifixion we saw Jesus voluntarily laying down his life. He had been beaten, mocked, scorned and scourged, clothed in a purple robe in mockery, with a crown of thorns pressed into his head. He bore his own cross and carried it to the Place of a Skull, a place called Golgotha.

There he was pierced through with nails, through his wrists and through his ankles, and the cross was hoisted up between two common criminals. One of them would place his faith in Christ in his final hour. The other would die apart from God.

And Jesus, looking out at the onlookers, prays for them: "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do." Then, in his last moments, he declares, "It is finished," and "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!"

And he breathed his last. Then we read that there's a clamor to hurry up and get these bodies off the crosses and buried. If you're not familiar with the Jewish law or Jewish burial customs, you may wonder why, but John gives us the details. It was the day of Preparation, and the Sabbath that followed was a high day, falling in the week of the Passover. The Jewish day began at sundown, around six in the evening, and Jesus breathed his last around three in the afternoon.

Here's why that mattered. God gave a command to the Jews in Deuteronomy 21, and it reads like this: "And if a man has committed a crime punishable by death and he is put to death, and you hang him on a tree, his body shall not remain all night on the tree, but you shall bury him the same day, for a hanged man is cursed by God. You shall not defile your land that the LORD your God is giving you for an inheritance." So the Jews were operating under this law: when a criminal is hanged for his crimes, God commands that the body not be left hanging overnight, lest it defile the land he gave them as an inheritance. Concerned about the Passover, and about the Sabbath, the day of rest when no work is done, they had to get these bodies off the crosses.

And they had to do it before sundown. So there's an urgency among some of the people to get on with it, so that they can get back to obeying God. You can see the irony: let's hurry up and finish crucifying God, so we can get back to obeying God. But I won't harp on that, because I've been preaching it for weeks.

Instead I want to hold out to you Galatians 3:13, Paul's interpretation of this law in Deuteronomy: "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us—for it is written, 'Cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree'—so that in Christ Jesus the blessing of Abraham might come to the Gentiles, so that we might receive the promised Spirit through faith." God, cursed by God, taking the curse of sin upon himself, and carrying it with him into today's events, into the tomb, into the grave.

So this morning we're not just talking about a criminal's death. We're talking about the death of death in the death of Christ. We're talking about the death of the penalty of sin. We're talking about the death of the wrath of God, which was kindled against sinners, because it was poured out and satisfied in Christ Jesus on that cursed tree. Amen?

So they take him down, and it goes like this: "After these things Joseph of Arimathea, who was a disciple of Jesus, but secretly for fear of the Jews, asked Pilate that he might take away the body of Jesus, and Pilate gave him permission. So he came and took away his body. Nicodemus also, who earlier had come to Jesus by night, came bringing a mixture of myrrh and aloes, about seventy-five pounds in weight.

"So they took the body of Jesus and bound it in linen cloths with the spices, as is the burial custom of the Jews." Before I tell you who Joseph of Arimathea was, who Nicodemus was, and who Pilate is in this exchange, I want you to know what the other Gospel accounts tell us. Mark tells us that Pilate was surprised to hear that Jesus was already dead. Jesus died a quick death on the cross. In some cases crucifixion could go on for days.

So to confirm that Jesus was really dead before he permitted them to take him down, Pilate summoned the centurion and asked him whether Jesus was already dead. Mark tells us that when he heard from the centurion that he was, he granted the body to Joseph. In the crucifixion account, you'll remember, the Jews asked Pilate to have the legs broken so that the bodies could be taken down. The idea is that when you're hoisted up on a cross, you're struggling by your own strength to stay alive, using your legs to hold your body up. What's killing you is asphyxiation. You're suffocating, and your legs are delaying it.

So if we break your legs, we speed up your death. But when the soldiers came to Jesus, they saw that he was already dead. So rather than break his legs, one of the soldiers pierced his side with a spear. You need to see the angle. Jesus is lifted up, and the soldier is down here.

So to pierce his side, you have to go up and under the rib cage. That's where we read that at once there came out blood and water. Biologically, we have to imagine that the spear pierced the pericardium, the sac of fluid around the heart, so that blood and water poured out. I point it out to say that the Gospels give us everything we need to answer the question, did he really die? I don't know if any of you have wrestled with some of the ancient heresies about the death of Jesus, but I need you to hear it.

The Gospel accounts make it very clear: Jesus was dead dead. He was all the way dead. He didn't swoon. He didn't faint. He wasn't carried off passed out to the tomb to wake up later.

Jesus was dead. And on the testimony of a Roman centurion that Jesus was in fact dead, Pilate granted that the body be removed and Jesus taken away to a tomb. The others were probably thrown into a common criminals' grave. Then we learn about Joseph of Arimathea.

If we take all the Gospel accounts together, Matthew tells us that the tomb they lay Jesus in is Joseph's own new tomb, which he had cut in the rock, and Luke and John tell us no one had ever been laid in it. After the burial it was Joseph who rolled the great stone across the entrance to seal the tomb. We'll talk a little more about their burial customs.

Mark tells us that Joseph of Arimathea was a respected member of the council, the Sanhedrin, who was looking for the kingdom of God. Luke tells us he was a good and righteous man who had not consented to the council's decision and action. Knowing these things about Joseph, he starts to sound a good bit like our friend Nicodemus. If you haven't been with us, let me catch you up. If you have, you've met Nicodemus twice now in this Gospel.

You met him in John 3, and you met him again in John 7. I want to reread for you John 3:1-16, the first time we meet him, because it's relevant for our passage today. Nicodemus seeks out Jesus to have a conversation with him: "Now there was a man of the Pharisees named Nicodemus, a ruler of the Jews. This man came to Jesus by night and said to him, 'Rabbi, we know that you are a teacher come from God, for no one can do these signs that you do unless God is with him.'

"Jesus answered him, 'Truly, truly, I say to you, unless one is born again he cannot see the kingdom of God.' Nicodemus said to him, 'How can a man be born when he is old? Can he enter a second time into his mother's womb and be born?' Jesus answered, 'Truly, truly, I say to you, unless one is born of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God. That which is born of the flesh is flesh, and that which is born of the Spirit is spirit.

"'Do not marvel that I said to you, "You must be born again." The wind blows where it wishes, and you hear its sound, but you do not know where it comes from or where it goes. So it is with everyone who is born of the Spirit.' Nicodemus said to him, 'How can these things be?' Jesus answered him, 'Are you the teacher of Israel and yet you do not understand these things?

"'Truly, truly, I say to you, we speak of what we know, and bear witness to what we have seen, but you do not receive our testimony. If I have told you earthly things and you do not believe, how can you believe if I tell you heavenly things? No one has ascended into heaven except he who descended from heaven, the Son of Man. And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up, that whoever believes in him may have eternal life.

"'For God so loved the world, that he gave his only Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life.'" Nicodemus gets one of the longest and best conversations with Jesus in his earthly ministry, I think. He seeks Jesus out, he asks him a question, and he gets the fullness of an answer, doesn't he? Nicodemus, the law alone is not enough. You must be born again.

How can I be born again? You must be born of water and the Spirit. I don't understand. Well, when the Son of Man is lifted up, people will be invited into this eternal life, this new birth I've described to you. And Jesus is the one who gave him this image: as Moses lifted up the serpent, so must the Son of Man be lifted up.

I say this to say that when this man saw that man lifted up on a cross, he had more context for what was happening than maybe anyone. Jesus had told him this must happen. And you have to imagine that when Nicodemus sees it, he's thinking, he said this must happen, and if this happens, then I can have eternal life. I can be born of the Spirit. And we see something happen in Nicodemus, don't we?

There's a change. The change didn't start in this moment. It started after his conversation with Jesus. We see it a second time in John 7. This is a shorter account.

The chief priests and the Pharisees had sent officers to arrest Jesus during the Feast of Booths, and the account goes like this: "The officers then came to the chief priests and Pharisees, who said to them, 'Why did you not bring him?' The officers answered, 'No one ever spoke like this man!' The Pharisees answered them, 'Have you also been deceived?

"'Have any of the authorities or the Pharisees believed in him? But this crowd that does not know the law is accursed.'" And we've got Nicodemus over here, one of the Pharisees, secretly thinking, I have. And he tries to give a defense, doesn't he?

"Nicodemus, who had gone to him before, and who was one of them, said to them, 'Does our law judge a man without first giving him a hearing and learning what he does?'" And they shut him down: "They replied, 'Are you from Galilee too? Search and see that no prophet arises from Galilee.'" So we've got to see, through these three passages, in chapter 3, in chapter 7, and now here in chapter 19, the growth of Nicodemus as a disciple.

He was a member of the Sanhedrin, the high court of Jerusalem. So two members of the Sanhedrin, the ruling court that condemned Jesus to death, are the ones who attend to his burial. I want you to see how remarkable that is: members of the court that condemned him were some of the first to come and worship him at his death. It means nobody is beyond reach, and we'll get there in a second.

But first I want to give you the simple details. Joseph of Arimathea and Nicodemus attend to his burial. Nicodemus came bringing a mixture of myrrh and aloes, about seventy-five pounds in weight, and they bound the body in linen cloths with the spices. I was reading up on this to get some sense of whether that was a lot, and it turns out that's a lot. Some years later, when Gamaliel the elder died, one of the most honored rabbis of that century, about eighty pounds of spices were burned at his funeral. Josephus, the Jewish historian, says five hundred servants carried spices in the funeral procession of Herod the Great.

Seventy-five pounds of spices is a fortune. Jesus received a king's burial. And that should tell us a good deal about the posture of Nicodemus's and Joseph's hearts at this burial. And this fulfilled Isaiah 53:9.

"And they made his grave with the wicked and with a rich man in his death, although he had done no violence, and there was no deceit in his mouth." So he's buried. He's in the tomb, with the stone rolled across the entrance. But the chief priests and the Pharisees were not happy.

They were concerned, so they go to Pilate and say, listen, while he was alive he taught that he would rise after three days. So can you guard the tomb, just in case his disciples get any ideas about coming and stealing the body? Just for three days. And Pilate obliges: "You have a guard of soldiers. Go, make it as secure as you can." So a guard of soldiers goes and guards the tomb, and Matthew says they made it secure by sealing the stone. That doesn't mean they sealed it with tar or something like that. They sealed it with an official seal, probably a cord across the stone, fixed and stamped with the authority of Rome. It would have been easy enough to cut, but the whole idea of a Roman seal is that you don't break it.

To break that seal was to defy Rome itself. So with the full weight of the Roman Empire behind them, they guard this tomb. This will not be opened. I've never seen that kind of effort put into guarding a dead man, let alone from a band of fishermen disciples from Galilee. There had to have been some sense, especially given what they saw at the cross, that something was going to happen. And something was happening, church.

We'll get to Easter next week. But right now it goes quiet, doesn't it? It seems like he lost. He's dead dead, carried to a tomb, with a stone rolled across to keep the stench inside. As far as anyone could see, decay was all that was left.

The way Jewish tombs worked, the body would lie in the tomb cut from the rock for about a year, not embalmed for preservation, but covered in spices and herbs to control the smell. After about a year it would have decomposed to bones, and the bones would be removed and given a more permanent burial. So a body would only remain in the stone tomb for about a year, and there was a long period of mourning for the dead. Joseph and Nicodemus had done everything a burial required. The reason I point this out is that some have said there was a whole plot and ploy to fake a resurrection.

You've probably heard them: everyone trying to come up with ways to explain how this dead man came back to life. But what I want to hold out to you is that every last person recorded as going to the tomb went there to visit a dead man. They didn't go expecting a resurrection. They went bearing the things you bring to a funeral.

They went to say goodbye. They went to do what you do for the dead. And that's what they've done. They've given him a king's burial. And then it goes quiet.

Now we shift our attention to what was happening in the silence. What happened on the earth is remarkable, but to the eye, all we've seen so far is the death of a so-called Messiah. What now? My hope is that by looking at what Jesus was doing in these days in the tomb, we'll come to rejoice that our God does some of his best work in the dark. When God is most silent, sometimes he is doing his grandest work.

And this account is evidence of that. So where did Jesus go? What was Jesus doing? Many of us have not thought deeply about death. Many of us have not thought deeply about where we go when we die.

Let alone stopped to consider: has it always been the way it is now? Has it changed? Did the death of Jesus, did the atoning blood of Jesus, change what happens when we die? I want to answer that for us today.

In Matthew 12:40, Jesus said of himself, "For just as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the great fish, so will the Son of Man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth." The heart of the earth. And of this place, the heart of the earth, he turned to the thief on the cross and called it paradise. He said to him, "Truly, I say to you, today you will be with me in paradise." Because where Jesus is going that day is not yet to the right hand of the Father.

That happens after the ascension. And we know this because he confirms it when he returns. When he appears to Mary Magdalene in John 20, about a chapter from now, Jesus says to her, "Do not cling to me, for I have not yet ascended to the Father; but go to my brothers and say to them, 'I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God.'" So after the resurrection he tells Mary that he has not yet ascended to the Father. But he told the thief, "Today you will be with me in paradise."

So where was he? That's what I want to answer for you this morning. To do it, I want to bring us to a passage in Luke where Jesus gives us some insight into this place with a story. Luke 16:19.

Let me just read it to you. It's a story that Jesus tells: "There was a rich man who was clothed in purple and fine linen and who feasted sumptuously every day. And at his gate was laid a poor man named Lazarus, covered with sores, who desired to be fed with what fell from the rich man's table. Moreover, even the dogs came and licked his sores.

"The poor man died and was carried by the angels to Abraham's side. The rich man also died and was buried, and in Hades, being in torment, he lifted up his eyes and saw Abraham far off and Lazarus at his side. And he called out, 'Father Abraham, have mercy on me, and send Lazarus to dip the end of his finger in water and cool my tongue, for I am in anguish in this flame.' But Abraham said, 'Child, remember that you in your lifetime received your good things, and Lazarus in like manner bad things; but now he is comforted here, and you are in anguish. And besides all this, between us and you a great chasm has been fixed, in order that those who would pass from here to you may not be able, and none may cross from there to us.'

"And he said, 'Then I beg you, father, to send him to my father's house—for I have five brothers—so that he may warn them, lest they also come into this place of torment.' But Abraham said, 'They have Moses and the Prophets; let them hear them.' And he said, 'No, father Abraham, but if someone goes to them from the dead, they will repent.' He said to him, 'If they do not hear Moses and the Prophets, neither will they be convinced if someone should rise from the dead.'"

So Jesus gives us this picture of the place where Abraham is, Abraham being long dead. And this man who has just died goes to be where Abraham is. In this place Jesus describes a great chasm, within eyesight of those in the place he called Hades, a place of torment.

A place where the rich man thirsts. Abraham says none may cross from one side to the other. But of this place into which Jesus descended, he says to the thief, you will be with me in paradise. Paradise is a garden word, the word for the parks of Persian kings, and it came to name the home of the blessed dead. Older translations call Abraham's side "Abraham's bosom," and it's the same word used for the disciple reclining at Jesus' side at the table. It's a seat of honor, where you sit in peace beside the father of the faith, Abraham, in the place the Jews called Sheol and the Greeks called Hades.

And Jesus says this place is really two: there is the place of torment, and there is the place of peace. And then Jesus descends to this place. We read about his descent several times in the Scriptures. Ephesians 4:8 says, "When he ascended on high he led a host of captives."

We'll get to that. "And he gave gifts to men," meaning the Holy Spirit and the gifts that come with him. "In saying, 'He ascended,' what does it mean but that he had also descended into the lower regions, the earth? He who descended is the one who also ascended far above all the heavens, that he might fill all things." Peter says likewise in 1 Peter 3: "For Christ also suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring us to God, being put to death in the flesh but made alive in the spirit, in which he went and proclaimed to the spirits in prison, because they formerly did not obey, when God's patience waited in the days of Noah."

All of this put together tells us how the Lord ordained things before the atoning blood of Christ. Those who died in the faith, looking forward to the promised Messiah but not yet having received the payment of their sin by the blood of Christ, were held in a place of peace in Sheol. Abraham's side was Jesus' word picture for it. This place was not heaven, but it was peaceful. But they were captive there. Why?

Because he had to go and proclaim to the spirits in prison, who formerly did not obey when God's patience waited in the days of Noah. For me this doctrine rests finally on Romans 3, and then I'll try to be done cross-referencing all over the place today. In Romans 3:21, Paul wrote, "But now the righteousness of God has been manifested apart from the law, although the Law and the Prophets bear witness to it." Okay? What this means is that those who were under the law before Christ came received the testimony of an alien righteousness to be had in Christ Jesus.

The Law and the Prophets told of it in the days of old. They bore witness to it. "The righteousness of God through faith in Jesus Christ for all who believe. For there is no distinction: for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God, and are justified by his grace as a gift, through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus, whom God put forward as a propitiation by his blood, to be received by faith. This was to show God's righteousness, because in his divine forbearance," listen close, "he had passed over former sins. It was to show his righteousness at the present time, so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus."

So here's what Paul is teaching us. Put all these doctrines together, and you see that it has always been by faith that we are saved. Even when we were under the law as our keeper, it was by faith. It was by faith that Abraham was saved. It was by faith that Moses was saved. And that faith kept them as they awaited the atoning blood of Christ, to be applied to their disobedience so that they would be justified before him the same way we are, by faith. What do I mean by this?

Scripture isn't speaking loosely when it says that Jesus descended and led a host of captives with him when he ascended, or that he went and proclaimed to the spirits in prison. Jesus descended into the lower regions of the earth. He gathered the church, those who died in faith before his atoning blood was applied, and he took them with him to heaven when he ascended, so that today that place is empty.

And now, when you die, church, you go directly into the presence of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit in heaven. Not a place of waiting, not a place of peace: heaven. And that's not all. Then comes the resurrection from the dead, when Jesus Christ returns for the final judgment. And for those who die apart from him, I want to make this clear.

If you die today apart from Christ, church, you will be cast into the lower regions of the earth, into that place of torment, and that place is not empty. Those people await their final judgment. At the final judgment, all will receive resurrected bodies. The unjust will be raised from the dead. Their bodies will be resurrected and joined with their spirits, and they will receive the final judgment of God.

And then they will be cast into the lake of fire to suffer eternal punishment for their sins. They will pay and pay and pay, but they are not Christ, and so they can never make a complete payment for their own sin. Likewise you, who have been in the presence of God in spirit but not in body, will receive the resurrection from the dead. Your spirit will be joined with a glorified body, and you will receive the final judgment. You will hear declared over you, "Well done, good and faithful servant," as the righteousness of Christ is spoken over you and you are justified before God. And then you will be invited into what is called the new earth, where heaven and earth come together, and you will dwell bodily with your God for eternity.

If you have questions, come talk it out with me. The reason we have to wrestle with what happens when we die is that a wrong idea about what happens when we die will give us a wrong idea about this life, and about what determines what happens when we die. Some of you call yourselves Christians, and I'm not saying you're not, and yet you have no theology whatsoever about what happens when we die, except that in some general sense you'll be with God. I'll tell you, a major obstacle in my own faith, as I was becoming a disciple of Christ and trying to study his word, is that I just wasn't all that excited about heaven.

I didn't want to go to hell, but I also didn't want to be a baby flying around shooting arrows at people. I had no concept of what heaven was, so I just made stuff up. I bet I can fly in heaven. I just pictured a bunch of cupids flying around.

Right? Whatever. Church, what you long for, even more than heaven in the general sense we mean it, is the new earth. That's the promise Christ made to you. There is a place God is preparing for you, where heaven and earth are brought together, and you get to reign bodily in the kingdom of God, in the presence of God.

You'll stand before his glory and not melt, but receive it as the very light of day, the very warmth on your skin. You were made to dwell bodily. God did not make a mistake when he made humanity. What he has promised is to restore you to what you were made for: to enjoy him and worship him and glorify him forever. But that also means that to die separated from him is to one day hear a judgment spoken over you, that you will pay bodily, eternally, for your sin.

None of us knows if we have tomorrow. None of us knows if we'll make it home. It is by faith alone in Christ alone, beholding him lifted up and declaring that the Son of God made payment for my sin, that we can be justified before God and one day hear, well done. I could preach a whole sermon series on this, and I know I've given you a lot. What I want to see is this church become a church that longs desperately for heaven, that has so right a view of what happens after we die that we could say with Paul, "For to me to live is Christ, and to die is gain."

I want us to believe and understand what is coming for us, what is promised to us, the inheritance that is coming for us, so that we march on through pain and suffering in this life with our eyes fixed on the prize, because we know our God is good and faithful. Likewise, if you are in this church and you do not belong to Christ, I want you to be very uncomfortable here. I want you to hear what the Scripture says is coming for you. If you go before your Creator God in the merits of your own righteousness, it will not stand up against his holy judgment. Repent today.

Receive Christ for your salvation today. If you don't know what that means, come and talk to me. I'll stay up all night. Let's get it right today. And if you're in him, let's live like it.

This life is not all there is. We've got good things coming. So maybe a good way to spend this life is the thing I've been preaching every week: when Jesus died, the righteous for the unrighteous, so that the hope of salvation might come to the Gentiles, he opened the door. But it won't always be open. You know where you're going.

Why not tell a friend? You won't bring anything with you into eternity except maybe someone else, by sharing the gospel, which is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes. Let's pray.
