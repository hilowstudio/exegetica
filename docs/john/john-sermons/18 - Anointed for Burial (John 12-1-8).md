# Anointed for Burial (John 12:1-8)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 12:1-8

---

I don't know if I've ever said this explicitly, and if I have, forgive me. At the beginning of this series, the pastors broke the book of John into four parts as we preach it in order. We started with "In the Beginning," a long, slow walk through the introduction to John's Gospel. Then came "Signs of the Savior," the public ministry of Jesus, when we worked through everything he did in public, his signs, his miracles and his healings, showing the world who he was and certifying who he claimed to be.

Now we're moving into the third part, which we've called "The Teachings of the Good Shepherd." At the end of his earthly ministry, Jesus begins to withdraw from the public eye and spend time intimately with his twelve. That really begins in chapter 13, with the farewell discourse in the upper room, and it runs to the final part, which we've called "New Life," the passion account, where Jesus is crucified and raised. So we're in an odd transition between chapters 12 and 13, where he's still somewhat public.

He hasn't gone into Jerusalem yet in his triumphal entry. But John says he no longer walked openly among the Jews, who were raging against him and looking for every chance to put him down. So this is a transition chapter, and in chapter 13 he'll be alone with his disciples. That's the direction we're heading. If I hadn't shared that with you yet, maybe it will help you follow along the rest of the way.

You'll remember that we spent the last two weeks on the raising of Lazarus. It was an incredible, loud, public miracle: a man dead four days, raised to new life by the voice of Jesus calling him out of the tomb. Afterward, John tells us, Jesus no longer walked openly among the Jews, because they wanted to kill him.

The Passover is at hand. We're six days before the Passover, the text says. Verses 1 and 2: "Six days before the Passover, Jesus therefore came to Bethany, where Lazarus was, whom Jesus had raised from the dead. So they gave a dinner for him there. Martha served, and Lazarus was one of those reclining with him at table."

I'm going to focus where John focuses, and this is mostly Mary's story, so we'll talk a lot about how Mary responds at this dinner. But almost as a throwaway, verse 2 says, "Martha served, and Lazarus was one of those reclining with him at table." Dead Lazarus.

Four-days-in-the-tomb Lazarus, whom Jesus called out with "Lazarus, come out," is reclining with him at this dinner. As I prepared, I was talking with the Lord about times you may have brushed up against death, bodily death first. Have you ever had a near-death experience, and then afterward, doing normal things felt really strange?

I almost died, and now I'm playing Nintendo. That feels strange. I just almost died, and now I'm eating a sandwich. I remember almost drowning at the beach one day when I was about ten, and then not drowning.

And then we were eating sandy sandwiches on a picnic blanket. It's strange to go from brushing up against death to ordinary everyday life. And when you go through a near death, or worse, the actual death of someone you love, there's something strange afterward. How do I just resume normal life once I've brushed up against death, once I've smelled its stench, tasted it, come near it? Well, here's Lazarus, who actually died.

He died and stayed dead for four days, until one day his eyes opened, wakened by the voice of his Savior, and he was hit with the stench of his own death. The stone was rolled away, he walked out, and now, some time later, he's reclining at table with Jesus. 

It's disorienting, this mixture of the stench of death and the light of life. And yet this messy breaking-in of new life into the awful sin and death of this world is the whole testimony of John's account. This particular scene did a number on me. It reminded me where my life has always come from, and where I need to remind you to find yours.

Six days before the Passover, Jesus comes to Bethany, where Lazarus was, whom Jesus had raised from the dead. So they gave a dinner for him there.

Martha served, and Lazarus was one of those reclining with him at table. So you have this picture of Jesus and Lazarus at the table, reclining together, and Martha serving. We don't know exactly where this is.

We know it's in Bethany. It may be in the home of Simon the leper, or it may be Martha's house, depending on how you line this account up with Mark's. Then, verse 3: "Mary therefore took a pound of expensive ointment made from pure nard, and anointed the feet of Jesus and wiped his feet with her hair. The house was filled with the fragrance of the perfume." So first we see Lazarus, the one who tasted death and was brought back to life, sitting at this table with Jesus.

How does he respond in this after-death meeting with Jesus? He's reclining. He's reclining. Then we see Martha. How does she respond?

Mary and Martha were the ones who sent word to Jesus: our brother is sick; we need you. He showed up, and Lazarus was already dead. They went out to him.

They wept. They took him to the tomb, and they saw with their own eyes the miracle of their brother being raised from the dead. Now Martha gets to have this dinner with him. How does she respond? She serves.

Then we see Mary, and her response is different from Lazarus's and Martha's. Mary takes a pound of expensive ointment made from pure nard, anoints the feet of Jesus and wipes his feet with her hair, and the house is filled with the fragrance of the perfume. Mary anoints him. So we see three different responses, and all of them are beautiful. I want to use them this morning to show you what to do when you come into the presence of Jesus, in the light, after you've brushed up against death.

After you've come into contact with death. After you've smelled its stench. After you've reached the point where you feel this thing can't be raised; it's past saving; it's outside the reach of Jesus. And now I'm in his presence, and I've seen his goodness.

How do I respond? Lazarus reclines. He just wants to rest and be with Jesus. He wants to eat with him. He wants to simply be with him.

He wants to be in his presence and enjoy him. Some of you need to respond like Lazarus. You want to jump straight to serving or pouring something out. But for you, maybe it means stopping, sitting and being with him. Just being with him.

Lazarus does nothing in this account worth recording in the chapter, and he's the one who was raised from the dead. He's just with him. For a lot of us, and I'll admit it's true for me, that's the hardest way to worship Jesus: just being with him. I want to do things for him long before I want to simply be with him.

I'll pour out something of worth for him, sacrifice for him in some way, long before I'm comfortable just being with him. I know the type, and if you've lived long enough, you can think of people like me in that way. I want to busy myself in response to Jesus, but bringing myself to sit down and just be with him is harder. As a pastor it can be especially hard. Every time you open the Bible, it feels like you're thinking about the next sermon, or a good illustration, or some way to use this to serve God.

But sitting with the Bible open just to hear from him and be with him, to dwell with him, is harder for some people. Lazarus just reclined. Martha served. It may or may not have been her house.

But she was bringing the food to the table and keeping the cups filled. Martha wanted to serve those at the table, not just Jesus, but those he loved. And this too is how we're to respond to Jesus.

That's why we call everyone to serve. Once you've received this death-to-life kind of service from Jesus, the natural response is for every last one of us to serve in return, to offer him our hands. How can we love you and love the ones you love? That's why I'm up here at all.

And that's why people teach the children, serve at the welcome table, and serve one another in all the ways we hear about. Ideally none of it is to earn anything from God. It's a response to what he's done for you, so that you love him and love whom he loves. But all of that is a footnote compared to what we see from Mary. Mary takes a pound of expensive ointment made from pure nard.

It's called spikenard. It came from the Himalayas, far to the east. It was very expensive, very rare, a very fragrant oil. She breaks it open, anoints the feet of Jesus and wipes his feet with her hair, and the whole house is filled with the fragrance of the perfume. As I read these details to you, or as you read your Bible, it's important to remember that every sentence is there because it stood out to the inspired writer.

John noted that the whole house was filled with the fragrance of the perfume. That's not a passing detail. If you compare this account with Mark's, and I don't want to go far there, because I didn't leave myself enough time after spending twenty minutes baring my soul up here, you'll find that some people believe Mark and John describe two different events, two dinners and two anointings.

Others believe both accounts tell of the same event. I'm in the camp that believes it was the same event, and I think I could convince you over lunch. It's not very important for this sermon, except that I'm going to borrow one detail from Mark and apply it to this passage. If that bothers you, just know it happened, if maybe not on that day. In Mark, the ointment is poured out on his head, so at least someone, probably Mary, poured this oil on his head and his feet.

And in Mark, Jesus says, "She has done a beautiful thing to me." John doesn't record that. Jesus defends her in John too, but those aren't the words John remembers. He remembers something else Jesus said. Either way, in a roundabout way, here's the takeaway I was trying to get to: Jesus loved this offering of worship.

The way Mary worshiped Jesus here delighted him. And not only Jesus; it delighted John, who even remembered how this perfume filled the whole house. She takes a pound of this ointment, breaks it open, pours it out on his feet and worships him. What she's doing here is loving him.

When I first wrote this sermon, the main point was going to be: are you loving Jesus because you love Jesus, or are you loving and worshiping him for what you want to get from him? We'll treat that as a sub-point instead. Because what we see from Mary is simply that she loves Jesus. This is a gesture of love.

I'm going to get the most valuable thing I have and pour it out on you, because you brought Lazarus back from the dead. No one has ever done anything like that for me. I don't know anyone else who could. You have loved me, Jesus, as no one else has ever loved me, and I want to love you with the highest gesture of love I can offer.

I'm going to take the most valuable thing I have, break it open and pour it out over your feet. And with my own hair I'm going to wipe your feet. This is the ultimate act of humble submission and lowly service. It says, you are so high, so much higher than me. Verses 4 to 6: "But Judas Iscariot, one of his disciples (he who was about to betray him), said, 'Why was this ointment not sold for three hundred denarii and given to the poor?' He said this, not because he cared about the poor, but because he was a thief, and having charge of the moneybag he used to help himself to what was put into it."

The same comment is made in Mark's account, which I think is further evidence that this is the same event. From it we learn that this ointment was worth three hundred denarii. A denarius was a day's wage in the ancient world, so we're talking about three hundred days' pay, roughly a year's salary, in this flask. And she has just broken it open and poured it over Jesus. As it's happening, Judas sees it and starts to panic.

He tries to stop it. Hold on. Is this the best use of the nard? This is worth three hundred denarii, after all. If we sold it, we could give the money to the poor. And of course this passage exposes him as a thief.

Of the twelve disciples, he was the one responsible for the group's moneybag. When they received offerings or came by money of any kind, he was responsible for it, and he would help himself to money from that bag regularly. So what he sees is a missed opportunity. If this money had been used in some other way, some service that glorifies God, it would have benefited him far more than being wasted on the feet of Jesus. In Mark, people essentially say, what a waste.

Why was it wasted like that? If you read the other Gospel accounts, you'll know that by this point Jesus has said plainly, at least three times, that he is on his way to a cross, that he intends to die. I think that creates a crisis of faith for Judas. He was counting on Jesus to be his earthly king, the one who would drive out the Romans and bring Israel into prosperity, and who might remember him on the day he took his earthly throne and give him a place of honor in the royal court. Those were the hopes of people who looked to Jesus to be an earthly messiah.

I'm drawing near to power for my own benefit. But at this point in his ministry, with Jesus leaving no doubt that he had come to die, Judas is torn. I need to get off this boat before it's too late. I've staked everything on a man who means to die here. I've got to get off.

That means every penny spent on this man hurts me and my plans. And here I need to circle back to my question for you. Why are you worshiping Jesus? Why are you following him? On what basis are you following him?

What are you looking for? What are you looking to? Because Judas was one of the twelve. He was there with Peter and the rest in those high moments. He was in the room at this intimate dinner. But he was there for a different reason than Mary, wasn't he?

A very different reason. And likewise, if you look at any church, and I don't mean a building, you'll talk to people who say, yes, I'm a Christian. Yes, I follow Jesus.

Ask them why, and you'll get an array of answers. Ask them about the way they worship, and you'll get a lot of different answers. Sometimes you'll see someone object to the way Jesus is being worshiped, not because it withholds some glory from him or is unworthy of him, but because it seems like a waste.

There's a more practical way we could have done that, a way that would have benefited me and that person. That's far too costly to you. It's a cheap worship that almost never costs you anything and almost always promises you a gain. Somehow my ministries are arranged around loving people who can serve me back.

And once there's no return on this form of worship, I'm out of there. You're not above these little calculations in the decisions you make as you seek to follow Jesus. But I tell you, Jesus spits this kind of worship out. It isn't true worship. "Why was this ointment not sold for three hundred denarii and given to the poor?" He said this, not because he cared about the poor, but because he was a thief, and having charge of the moneybag he used to help himself to what was put into it.

And Jesus says, verse 7, "Leave her alone, so that she may keep it for the day of my burial." The Greek can be read another way, and the ESV gives it in its footnote: "Leave her alone; she intended to keep it" for the day of my burial. I think that reading lines up better with what Jesus says in Mark: "She has anointed my body beforehand for burial."

He certainly isn't telling her to stop and save the oil for later. He's delighting in the way she's anointing him right now as the fragrance fills the house. Leave her alone; she intended to keep it for the day of my burial. Verse 8: "For the poor you always have with you, but you do not always have me." Jesus is saying, I'm right here. I'm here now.

I'm right in front of her. This is the best use of this oil right now: that she pour it out on me while she still can. In Mark, she has anointed my body beforehand for burial. And I wonder how seen she felt in that moment, when Jesus got inside her head and told everyone what she meant this oil for: leave her alone; she intended to keep it for my burial.

Or in Mark: she has anointed my body beforehand for burial. He's acknowledging that she knows his death is near. She feels it, and she loves him, and she's saying, I don't know when I'll be with you next. I don't know the hour of your death, but you keep saying it will be soon. I don't know if I'll have another meal with you. But you're here now, and I have this, so I offer it to you now while I can.

It's a true offering of worship, moved by love. She loves him, and she didn't want to miss the chance to offer him the most expensive thing she had. If I were sitting at a table with the man who raised my brother from the dead, and he said he was going to die, and he had also said, in veiled terms, that he would rise again from the dead, I might just believe it. I might believe that though he died, yet he would live.

I might start to sense that this man means it, that he's the real thing. If anyone believed before the resurrection that he would really come alive again after three days, it may have been the sisters who saw it happen to Lazarus. There was faith in her offering. And this is my final main point this morning. I want to make sure you're clear on it. When you stake your hopes on Jesus, either you're staking them on a dying man, as Judas thought he was: I've pinned all my hopes on someone who's just going to die.

And if he dies, I'm out of luck, so I need to get off the ship and go elsewhere. Or you're staking your hopes on one who has victory over death, who can give eternal life to anything you entrust to him. How you see Jesus will determine how you worship Jesus. How you see Jesus will determine how you worship him. Do you believe that pinning your hopes on him is pinning them on death, or on life?

I know we know the right answer to these questions, which is why keeping them hypothetical isn't always very helpful. So what are you withholding from Jesus? Let this speak to you.

But don't answer too quickly. What are you withholding from him? What are you trying to help him with? What aren't you sure he'll show up for, so that you're afraid it might stay dead, or die, if you count on him for it?

You are withholding something. There are things you won't bring into the light with Jesus, because, like Martha last week, who said, please don't roll that stone away, Jesus, by now there will be an odor, we don't want to bring them into the light with Jesus. We're certain that if we shine light on this thing, it will only mean more death.

So we keep it over here. Keep it from Jesus. Don't take it to Jesus. It's outside his reach. I'll handle it myself. And so it stays dead.

This woman poured out everything on him, knowing he was going to his burial, out of pure love, and in hope that he can make dead things alive again, because she had seen it with her own eyes. That's my invitation to you this morning. Come out of hiding. Take the things you would withhold from Jesus, and the fear that dead things stay dead, and remember that Jesus says, not so; in me, dead things can live again. Bring them to him. Bring them into the light. Make them known. Take a real risk and make them known to one another, so that those who have seen dead things come to life can speak that truth over you and encourage you to walk in the light with him. Let's pray to that end.
