# Lazarus, Come Out! (John 11:38-57)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 11:38-57

---

We're continuing our series through the book of John, in chapter 11, from verse 38 to the end of the chapter. If you've been with us, you'll know how we got here; if not, I hope this summary will do. We're midway through an account in which Jesus raises a dead man to new life. His name is Lazarus. Last week, word came to Jesus from this man's sisters, Mary and Martha. They sent to Jesus where he was to tell him that the one he loves is ill.

"Lord, he whom you love is ill," they say. When the word reaches him, he tells his disciples, this illness does not lead to death; it is for the glory of God, so that the Son of God may be glorified through it. Then he waits two more days where he is before making his way back to Bethany, the village Lazarus his friend was from. When he arrives, Lazarus's sister Martha runs out and meets him on the way.

She says, if you had been here, my brother would not have died. And Jesus says, your brother will rise again. Martha has no category for this. She says, yes, I know he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day. She thinks he's referring to the end of all things, but she has no real expectation in that moment that Jesus is about to raise her brother from the dead that very day. And he says, no.

I am the resurrection and the life. Whoever believes in me, though he die, yet shall he live, and everyone who lives and believes in me shall never die. Do you believe this?

Martha gives an amazing confession of faith: I believe, Lord, that you are the Christ, the Son of God, who is coming into the world. Then she goes and tells her sister Mary that Jesus has arrived and is asking for her. Mary comes, with a great crowd of Jews around her, and says the same thing: if you had been here, Jesus, my brother would not have died. Jesus sees a mix of tears: the sincere tears of Mary and Martha, who are calling on him and depending on him, and the tears of their fellow mourners, the Jews who had come to mourn with them for a week, as was customary then.

He sees the tears of the Jews mixed with the tears of those who truly loved Lazarus and loved the Lord, and an agitation wells up within him. And at the end of the passage, some in the crowd say, could not he who opened the eyes of the blind man also have kept this man from dying? You can see that not everyone in the crowd is coming to Jesus sincerely. That catches us up to where we are this morning.

Last week we did a lot of work on bad theology about sickness, about death, about who Jesus is in these hard moments. We talked about the ideas we have that Jesus somehow surrenders his lordship or his goodness, that he stops knowing or becomes unaware, that he grows distant or cold in our moment of suffering. Either something must be lacking on my part for there to be suffering in my life, maybe I've brought this on myself, it's the Lord's displeasure with me, the Lord's absence from me, and he's waiting for me to clean myself up and pull myself together to bring his presence and favor back into my life; or else something is lacking on his part. Maybe he sees, but he doesn't care.

Maybe he sees and cares, but he isn't able. Maybe he's able and cares, but he doesn't see. Something is lacking on his part, and there's nothing I can do about it. And a great despair comes over us in our hour of suffering because of all kinds of bad doctrine about pain, suffering and death. So we did a lot of work last week on those things, and this morning we'll see Jesus resolve all the tension we walked through. In my notes I wrote down four points, and I'll give them to you up front so you can listen for them. First, the Father, Son and Spirit are always within hearing of each other's voice. The three persons of the triune Godhead are always within hearing of one another.

The Father always hears the Son. The Son always hears the Father. And the Spirit always hears the Son and the Father. There's constant, open communication among the three persons of the triune God. Point two: the dead obey the voice of the Lord.

The dead obey the voice of the Lord. Point three: the unregenerate try to silence the voice of the Lord, but they cannot. And as part of that, we'll see this morning that even his accusers and deniers prophesy about him. And my last point says simply: Jesus wins.

Jesus wins. You can write that down. What I really mean is that Jesus makes really dead things really alive. Jesus makes really dead things really alive. Let's make our way through it together.

John 11, verses 38 and 39: "Then Jesus, deeply moved again, came to the tomb. It was a cave, and a stone lay against it. Jesus said, 'Take away the stone.' Martha, the sister of the dead man, said to him, 'Lord, by this time there will be an odor, for he has been dead four days.'"

I want to stop here. This is Martha. If you weren't with us last week, try to put yourself in Martha's shoes. If you were here, this will come faster for you, but listen. Martha has just given that beautiful confession of faith.

Jesus has just told her that her brother will rise again, and she has confessed: yes, I believe that all who put their faith in you will never perish. I believe it.

And all those who have died, as you've just said, will yet live. I believe it, Lord. I believe that you are the Son of God, who has come into the world. I believe it.

And now Jesus shows up at the tomb and says, roll that stone away. Look at Martha's response. "Take away the stone," he says, and Martha, the sister of the dead man, says, "Lord, by this time there will be an odor, for he has been dead four days." In my notes, all I wrote was: Lazarus was dead dead.

Four-days dead. Starting-to-stink dead. Body-decomposing dead. Lazarus was dead.

Really dead. So dead that when Mary got up and ran out of her home weeping on hearing that Jesus had arrived, everyone around her made the natural assumption. When someone died in Jewish culture, there was a seven-day mourning period. The mourners would surround the chief mourner and follow her wherever she went in her grief, back and forth between the home and the grave. So when they saw her get up, people followed her, naturally assuming she was going to the tomb. Not to open it, not after four days, but to grieve there.

That was their assumption. So they come to the tomb, and everything says: keep that thing shut, because that body is four days dead. Grieve here, because that body is four days dead. Lazarus is gone.

He's gone. And when Jesus says, roll that thing away, what Martha fully expects to meet is her brother's rotting body. She says, Lord, we can't roll that stone away. He's been gone four days. I need you to hear that.

Because there are things in your life, and I'm not only talking about your sin here; I'm talking about hopes and dreams that have been crushed, about people, about broken relationships. There are dead things all around you. We'll come to the chief dead thing in this message.

But there are things about which we wouldn't dare to hope in Jesus when he says, open that up. Let it breathe. Let's get some air in there, because I want to do something. I want to take something dead and bring it to life. And we'd rather keep the stone shut.

Because if you don't show up, Jesus, what I'll be confronted with is the stench of death. I'd rather keep it hidden behind that stone than risk opening it up to you, Jesus, because if you don't show up, I don't know that I could face it. Martha was well within her right mind to worry about rolling that stone away. This was the beloved sister of the dead man, at the height of her mourning. She desperately needed Jesus.

She sent word to him before she thought it was too late. But now we've moved into an entirely different phase of grief, where surely it's too far gone. Surely it's too late. So when Jesus says, roll the stone away, Martha objects. It's our custom at home, when I'm putting my kids to bed, to spend time in devotion to the Lord, and one thing we do is tell each other our favorite stories about Jesus.

Sometimes that's with the Bible open, and sometimes it's more like oral tradition. I'll say to my littlest, tell me one of your favorite stories about Jesus, and I'll let him speak true things about Jesus over me in his own words, the way he remembers the story. It's a lovely time. One night, when my youngest was four, I was telling him the story of Lazarus, the time Jesus brought his friend back from the dead. He listened intently and ate it up. It was the first time he'd really heard the story with any understanding. And when I finished, he said to me, very astutely, Dad, after Jesus raised Lazarus from the dead, did he ever die again?

Good question. Yes, buddy, he did. He sure did die again. And that's why it's so incredible that our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ laid down his life for us and then took it up again. He said that if by faith we are united with him in a death like his, we will also be united with him in a resurrection like his and live forever. We won't die two deaths. We'll live one life, always before the face of our God.

I thought I'd just had an incredible theological moment with my four-year-old, but he was still fixed on his question about Lazarus. He said, okay, so Jesus gives us all one extra life, and then it's game over. Three years later, at seven, he went into the waters of baptism, joining Christ in the likeness of his death and rising in the likeness of his resurrection, seeing with open eyes that Jesus gives new life to us who are spiritually dead. That's ultimately where I want us to go this morning as we look at the physical resurrection of Lazarus.

Let's stay with our story. Martha objects: there will be an odor, for he has been dead four days. This is a sub-point, but I want you to think about what it was like for Lazarus to be raised in this situation. We'll get some of the details as we work through it, but come on.

At his first resurrection, to stay with my four-year-old's question, he was raised to a linen cloth wrapped around his face. He was raised to bound hands. He was raised to wrappings around his feet. He was raised to the stench of his own decomposing body.

That's what Lazarus was raised to at his first resurrection, and for some of us that's the resurrection we prefer. Some of us would count it all joy if the Lord would forget eternity and just enter our temporary, momentary affliction and fix what's broken here on this side of eternity, so that we could walk a little more comfortably in a sick and dying world before we die again, with no regard at all for eternal matters or for total, permanent restoration. I wonder how welcome the sight was when Lazarus opened his eyes again to bodily life. Let's see. Verses 40 and 41: "Jesus said to her, 'Did I not tell you that if you believed you would see the glory of God?' So they took away the stone."

You may remember the moment in Exodus when Moses begs the Lord, "Please show me your glory." And the Lord, in his kindness, puts Moses in a cleft of the rock. He makes all his goodness pass before him and proclaims his name over him as he passes by, while Moses is hidden in the cleft.

But he won't let Moses see his face, because "man shall not see me and live." This is the nature of sin. We were created for the glory of our King. We were made to magnify his glory, to reflect his goodness and majesty and wonder back to him.

We were made for a purpose, and the purpose of humanity is to reflect the glory of God as his image-bearers in this life. We're meant to bask in the warmth of his glory, to find our life and our purpose in the presence of his glory. And the great loss of sin, among all its losses, is that in our sinful state we can no longer look on the glory of our Creator, the very thing we were made for. So even Moses, the chosen man of God in those ancient days, says, Lord, show me your glory, and God says, hide over here and I'll show you my back.

But here Jesus says something entirely different, doesn't he? Not to his prophet Moses, but to his beloved friend Martha: Did I not tell you that if you believed you would see the glory of God? If you believed, you would see the glory of God. The thing your soul cries out for, deep down, louder than anything in this life, is simply to see the glory of your God.

That the veil would be lifted, that you would behold him in all his fullness, all his goodness, all his glory. That's what you were made for. And Jesus says the path to it is to believe in me. Martha, you've said you believe in me, so watch this. Watch this. We're about to see an incredible miracle.

But in making this declaration, Jesus has already looked past the miracle. The miracle certainly served to signify and certify who he was and whom he was pointing to, but Jesus has his eyes fixed on the glory of his Father, the glory of the Son, the glory of the Spirit. Did I not tell you that if you believed you would see the glory of God? So they took away the stone.

Verses 41 and 42: "And Jesus lifted up his eyes and said, 'Father, I thank you that you have heard me. I knew that you always hear me, but I said this on account of the people standing around, that they may believe that you sent me.'" So Jesus prays in front of the crowd. He lifts his eyes to heaven and prays a prayer of thanksgiving to his Father: thank you that you have heard me.

In saying that, he names who is initiating what's about to happen: the Son. Jesus desires to raise Lazarus. When he got the news, he knew God the Father was up to something in this sickness of Lazarus. He said, this illness does not lead to death; it is for the glory of God, so that the Son of God may be glorified through it.

And in effect Jesus has said to the Father, let me raise him, and the Father, who always hears the Son, has said, go ahead. So he thanks him for hearing him, and then he makes a public declaration within his prayer. Have you ever heard a pastor do that, start teaching while he prays?

He starts saying true things about God to God while he prays, or saying things to the people listening to his public prayer. There's room for it. Jesus is doing it here. Thank you that you have heard me.

Not because you ever fail to hear me, but for these people standing around, that they may believe that you sent me. Jesus knew that if he said in front of the crowd, thank you, Father, for hearing me, someone might conclude that sometimes the Father hears you and sometimes he doesn't, that you can do miracles only when your Father is listening. So Jesus says, Father, let me clarify.

I knew that you always hear me, but I say this on account of them, that they may believe that you sent me. This is the high claim of Jesus that keeps bringing him within an inch of his life, though he won't let it happen before his hour, because he is Lord, and he and the Father are one. They live in total unity. At Christ's baptism, at the beginning of his public ministry, the Father let those around him see an amazing moment, when the Holy Spirit came down on him in bodily form, and from there the Spirit led him into the wilderness and carried him every day of his public ministry as he walked this life in human form.

So in this moment, when Jesus lifts his eyes to heaven and speaks as if he were standing right at the Father's ear, he is doing it in the power of the Spirit. All three in one are in perfect, total, open relationship with one another. And I bring this up because every time Jesus prays, not only when he teaches us the Lord's Prayer, he's teaching us something. It was Jesus who told us that it is better for us that he go away, so that the Spirit, the Helper, would come. And if you belong to Christ, what makes you a Christian, above all else, is that you have been brought from death to life by the Holy Spirit dwelling in you. That means when you pray, you have oneness with the Father, the ear of the Father, as the Holy Spirit himself intercedes for you, always praying the perfect prayer to the Father on your behalf.

You who have been made one with the Holy Spirit share in the oneness of the Father and the Son, and you can commune with him directly in prayer. That's what Jesus does here as he thanks the Father, so that the crowd might believe that the Father sent him. Then, verses 43 and 44: "When he had said these things, he cried out with a loud voice, 'Lazarus, come out.' The man who had died came out, his hands and feet bound with linen strips, and his face wrapped with a cloth. Jesus said to them, 'Unbind him, and let him go.'"

We come back around to the strange way Lazarus came out. His ankles are bound, perhaps so his body could be carried more easily to the tomb. His hands are bound, and a cloth is over his face. So he came out with some trouble, apparently, but he did come out. I don't know if you want to picture it with me, but it gets pretty strange in my imagination. As this crowd watched Lazarus come out, I don't think the first reaction was praise and wonder. I think it was probably terrifying. I don't know whether Jesus took care of the odor.

But the tomb opened, the smell probably came out, and then out came this mummy of a man, hopping his way out of the tomb, because he was still bound. Jesus hadn't yet given the command to unbind him. And yet, for all that held him back, the dead obeyed the voice of the Lord.

The dead obeyed the voice of the Lord. This is a major point for me this morning and in our text, and you can take it to the bank: the dead obey the voice of your Lord. Nothing escapes the lordship of Christ. "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. He was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him, and without him was not any thing made that was made. In him was life, and the life was the light of men. The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it."

If all things were made through him and for him, and as Abraham Kuyper put it, there is not one square inch of all creation over which Christ does not cry, Mine!, then not even death can wriggle you free from his lordship. Even the dead respond to his voice as if it were nothing, because he looked at the void and said, let there be, and it was. If nothing listens to him and becomes something, then something listens to him too, even when that something is dead. Some of you need to hear this, and not only about the life and death of people, but about the life and death of all things. There are things you're counting as dead dead, things you've decided are now outside the reach of your Lord's lordship. So we don't talk to him about them by the Spirit, to discover that he may have every intention of glorifying the Son by bringing that dead thing to life, as he has been doing from beginning to end.

If you belong to him, you are the proof. You and I were spiritually dead in the trespasses in which we once walked. To take a sinner from the pit of hell and set him in the high court of the King in heaven is a greater thing than to say to a tomb, Lazarus, come out. Raising Lazarus pales in comparison to what he is doing day by day, by the power of the Spirit, in dead sinners all across the world, right here included. You are proof that he brings dead things to life, because you were dead in the trespasses in which you once walked. But he has made you alive together with him, if you are found in Christ.

So take heart. And think this morning about the person you believe is too far gone. If the church, filled with new life, people who had hearts of stone made into hearts of flesh, once spiritually dead and now filled with the Holy Spirit of God, can't look at dead things and say, I wonder what God is going to do, then we are to be pitied. The proof that no one around you is too far gone is that you weren't too far gone.

And if you walk in here thinking of yourself as anything other than the worst among us, you've lost sight of all that Christ has ransomed you from, and all he still intercedes for you about as you keep stumbling against the flesh on your way to your eternal home at the King's table. Here Jesus has shown his lordship over the dead. Lazarus has come out. And Jesus says to those around him, unbind him and let him go.

I find that interesting, and I won't press it too far. But I love it when Jesus lets his people take part in his ministry. He could have brought Lazarus out unbound. Instead he brought him out and let everyone get a good look at how he went in. Then he invited them to go and take off those bindings. Here was a living man dressed like a dead man, and Jesus said to those around him, let's make the clothes match his state.

Take off those bindings. They don't make sense anymore. And that's the invitation he makes to us day by day in our new life: not to go back to our grave clothes, not to return to the stench and filth of our sin and put them back on, wearing the flesh as if it still fits. It doesn't anymore. Put off the flesh, and stay with Christ in the Spirit. Let's move on. Now a whole plot unfolds.

This is my third point. Jesus claims lordship even over the dead; even the dead respond to his voice. But the unregenerate, those who are not born again, who are dead in their sin, can't receive it. They won't receive it. They will do everything they can to silence him, as we all did until we were given new life. And that's what we see here.

None of this is enough for them. Verses 45 to 48: "Many of the Jews therefore, who had come with Mary and had seen what he did, believed in him." Amen. "But some of them went to the Pharisees and told them what Jesus had done. So the chief priests and the Pharisees gathered the council and said, 'What are we to do? For this man performs many signs. If we let him go on like this, everyone will believe in him, and the Romans will come and take away both our place and our nation.'"

So they convene this council, and in response to Jesus bringing the dead to life, they say, let's shut this man down. If that seems bizarre to you, remember the place where you once walked. It isn't that bizarre. Your eyes have always seen the Lord testifying to his goodness, his mercy, his majesty, his glory. Everything around you shouts his glory, and everything in you, until the moment you were given eyes to see, did all it could to blind you to it and to invite others to follow you into death and blindness. That's the nature of sin. It's utterly corrupt.

So they gather this council and start to plot: how do we shut this man down? And Caiaphas speaks. Verses 49 and 50: "But one of them, Caiaphas, who was high priest that year, said to them, 'You know nothing at all. Nor do you understand that it is better for you that one man should die for the people, not that the whole nation should perish.'" He says it out loud: we have to kill him.

He says it under a pretense. Everything Jesus is doing is disrupting the status quo. If the status quo is disrupted, it will attract the attention of the Romans. If it attracts the Romans, they'll come in. They might tear down the temple. They might take away our standing as a nation. They might crush us. They remember the Babylonian exile well. For them it's very important to keep access to the temple, so that worship and sacrifice can go on.

They want to stay in the land, to be a people who can say, the favor of our God is on us, because we still occupy the land he gave us. So under this pretense: what Jesus is doing will cost us our land and our access to the temple, and it will bring the Romans' disfavor on us, so for the sake of our relationship with God, let's kill him. It's hypocrisy, and our sin always runs that way.

But even Caiaphas, calling for the death of the Messiah, is fulfilling the word of God. Verses 51 to 53: "He did not say this of his own accord, but being high priest that year he prophesied that Jesus would die for the nation, and not for the nation only, but also to gather into one the children of God who are scattered abroad. So from that day on they made plans to put him to death." Caiaphas the priest spoke to shut down and kill the Messiah, on the false claim that it would keep God's favor and keep off the Romans' disfavor.

But the Lord took his words and turned them into prophecy, so that he spoke the truth: one would die for all, and not for the nation of Israel only, but for people scattered across the earth. If this doesn't show us the high sovereignty of our glorious Lord, nothing will. He speaks to the physically dead and they obey. He speaks to the spiritually dead, and even when everything in them is driven by sin, their very words are used as prophecy to bring about the salvation of the world. Our Lord is sovereign over all. He's sovereign over all. And from that day on, as they made their plans to put him to death, verses 54 to 57: "Jesus therefore no longer walked openly among the Jews, but went from there to the region near the wilderness, to a town called Ephraim, and there he stayed with the disciples. "They were looking for Jesus and saying to one another as they stood in the temple, 'What do you think? That he will not come to the feast at all?' We've divided this series through John into four parts.

After the introduction, "In the Beginning," came "Signs of the Savior." That's what we've been preaching for what feels like forever. Now Jesus ends his period of public miracles and withdraws into a private ministry with his inner circle, his disciples. We're calling this next part "The Teachings of the Good Shepherd." It's a whole new focus in the life of Jesus. And even though we'll celebrate it, and we're grateful the disciples recorded everything that happened in this inner-circle ministry Jesus now moves into,

we also do well to weep that the supposed people of God are the ones who drove Jesus into this period of his ministry. As the day drew near, by the hand of the sovereign Lord, he could no longer walk freely among the Jews, the nation of Israel, the people of God, because if they'd had their way, they would have put him down before his hour had come. We'd push him off to a place where he doesn't dwell among us, off gathering with his inner circle, because we don't really want Jesus. We spit out the Jesus who is Lord of the dead. We spit out the Jesus who is Lord of the spiritually dead, who requires things of us, who looks at you and sees your sin and holds you accountable for it before a holy God, who lays down his life for it, praise God, and who requires you to join him in that death in order to have your new life.

Let me circle back to Martha for a moment, as a word of warning to the church. We'd like the stone to stay closed. We'd like the stench of our filth to stay hidden from the eyes of Jesus. We don't want him to see it or speak to it. We don't want him to take dead things and bring them to life, because that means I have to admit it's dead. It means I have to smell it. It means I have to deal with it.

That's dangerous, because even the most religious among them, leaving Martha and Mary aside for a minute, wanted the favor of God, but they didn't want the lordship of Christ in order to get it. And God's favor rests on the Christ; his favor toward you is found in Christ dwelling in you. There are no half measures. No one here has met God halfway. He isn't more pleased with you than with someone else on the basis of your own works.

You're dead or you're alive. So my invitation to you in closing is to answer this for yourself, and answer it quickly. I don't want to sow any seeds of doubt in you if you belong to him. But maybe you don't. Maybe you think Jesus loves you because you're great.

Maybe you think Jesus loves you because you obey. Be sure that your right standing with God rests on the work of another: that Christ took a dead thing and made it alive. And once you're sure, take your eyes off yourself and put them on another: the one you believe is too far gone, the one you've stopped sharing the gospel with. Ask the Lord to give you courage to join him in his life-giving work of spreading the gospel to the ends of the earth, so that people might be reconciled to him and do what we were made to do: enjoy and delight in his glory, and reflect his glory back to him.

Let's pray.
