# Who Will Eat the Bread of Life? (John 6:60-71)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 6:60-71

---

I preached this on Father's Day. Someone once told me that the voice of a father becomes the inner voice of a child. I don't know how true that is, though I believe it's partly true. If it is, it's a heavy burden, and who can bear it? I certainly don't want my voice to be the dominant inner voice of my children.

So to the fathers, first: the Lord sees you. Every time you represent the good and perfect voice of your Father in heaven to your children, he sees it, and he rejoices over the kingdom work you're doing in them. But far more important, hear this: every time you fail to do that, every time your voice doesn't reflect the perfect voice of your Father in heaven, because of Jesus you are perfectly covered, perfectly spotless, perfectly forgiven. And in all the ways you will fail your children, their Father in heaven never will. You have that assurance from him.

And to those grieving today, the father you didn't have, the father you've lost, or the father you wish you were: your Father in heaven looks on you with delight and offers himself to you perfectly as your great comforter. I pray that as we look at him together this morning, we would each enter into that rest.

A good friend once told me that when he was nine or ten, his dad took him to a sporting event. In the bathroom at the venue he did or said something that embarrassed his father, and his father said to his buddies, "Whose kid is this? Surely not mine." Harmless enough. But somehow those words went straight into my friend's heart, and they stayed with him for decades. So many other things in his life told him he was one misstep away from being disowned, unchosen, by the people who loved him. Some forty years later, around fifty years old, wanting to resolve that pain, he went to his father to make peace about the sentence spoken all those years ago. And understandably, his dad had no memory of ever saying it.

That's the nature of our relationships with our fathers and with one another: we were created to long for belonging. We were created as an overflow of the love that has existed eternally between God the Father, God the Son and God the Holy Spirit. Forever they have loved one another perfectly. Forever they have known perfect belonging. And when you and I were placed on this earth, we were created in the image of God, as Genesis says, male and female. That means not only that we resemble him, but that we share in the life he shares within himself: that we, as created beings reflecting his image, would dwell with him and enjoy him forever. True belonging. We were made for it, and that's why we long for it. It's why we feel it so deeply when it's broken in our human relationships, and why we need our Father in heaven so much to be that for us. He designed us to need it.

If you're feeling that need this morning, you're joining me in it, and joining the whole world. I've titled this sermon "Did I Not Choose You?" Those are Jesus' final words in this interaction, and I pray that as we work through it, we'll find peace and comfort in the truth that our God, from eternity past, looked on you and chose to set his care and affection and saving grace on you, chose to adopt you specifically.

This is the third of three sermons walking through the bread miracle. Part one looked at the miracle itself, part two at the crowd's response, and today, part three, at the disciples' response. In part one, when Jesus was brought the bread and the fish and multiplied them to feed the five thousand, plus the women and children, who ate? Everybody. The whole crowd ate. In the same way, just as the sun shines on the skin of every person on earth today, and a cool breeze passes over that same skin to ease the heat, our God stands ready to pour out his common grace on all people. He extends his hand of care and invitation and love to the whole earth.

But last week we saw a smaller group from that crowd get into boats and chase him to Capernaum to ask for more, and he said some really hard things to them. In fact, this morning's passage begins, "When many of his disciples heard it, they said, 'This is a hard saying; who can listen to it?'" So let's remember what he said. He said four things to the crowd. First, all that the Father gives me will come to me. Second, whoever comes to me I will never cast out; in fact, it is the Father's will that I should lose nothing of all that he has given me. Third, no one can come to me unless the Father draws him. And fourth, the new life you're invited into comes by eating my flesh and drinking my blood: you must receive me, my broken body and my shed blood, to enter eternal life.

Those were the four teachings, and this morning opens with the response. Verses 60 to 63: "When many of his disciples heard it, they said, 'This is a hard saying; who can listen to it?' But Jesus, knowing in himself that his disciples were grumbling about this, said to them, 'Do you take offense at this? Then what if you were to see the Son of Man ascending to where he was before? It is the Spirit who gives life; the flesh is no help at all. The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life.'"

So we open with a visceral response from the disciples. Jesus has said, my Father gives you to me, and when he does, you will come. When you come, I won't cast you out. But you can't come unless he carries you to me. And when he does, the new life comes from eating my body and drinking my blood. And they say, "This is a hard saying; who can listen to it?"

That's the right question. It is a hard saying. I preached it last week, and in many ways it's a miracle of the Lord that when I quote it to you, you don't get up and walk out of the room just like the crowds did. And Jesus had just answered the question: who can listen to it? Those the Father has given me. They will come to me. They will hear. My sheep hear my voice, he says elsewhere. They're asking the right question, but he has already answered it: the ones who can listen are the ones who are mine, and the ones who are not mine cannot bear to listen.

Knowing in himself that they are grumbling, he asks, "Do you take offense at this? Then what if you were to see the Son of Man ascending to where he was before?" To the crowd that probably sounded rhetorical, or hypothetical, or theoretical. But we know the end of the story. The Son of Man will ascend to where he was before. Jesus is pointing their offense back to that. Where was he before? He has just told them: I am the bread of life that the Father sent from heaven. What if you saw me ascend to heaven? Would you still be offended? What if I returned to where I belong, where I came from? Would you still be offended?

That question cuts two ways, and we'll get there. But our author opened his book by teaching us who Jesus is and where he came from: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. He was in the beginning with God. All things were made through him, and without him was not any thing made that was made. In him was life, and the life was the light of men. The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it." This Jesus says, what if you saw me ascend back to that? Would you still be offended when I tell you that it is by my sovereign hand, by my sovereign will, by the decision and active pursuit of the Father, the Son and the Spirit, that you become mine? To take offense at this is to lose sight of who he is and where he came from. And when he speaks of seeing the Son of Man ascend, it wasn't hypothetical. He would in fact ascend before many witnesses to the place he came from, and he would have it written down in several places so that we could behold it, and the Spirit could give us faith to believe it and see it for ourselves.

All of this sets up the next sentence: "It is the Spirit who gives life; the flesh is no help at all." Seeing the crowd's offense at his claim that all the Father gives him will come, and that unless the Father gives you to him you won't come, he doubles down: it is the Spirit who gives life, and the flesh is no help at all.

So we've watched him build a case, and I'm repeating it on purpose so we see the flow from Jesus' own mouth. He taught it in this order deliberately. All that the Father gives to the Son will come to him. Whoever comes to him, he will never cast out. He won't lose a single one. Unless the Father draws you, you can't come. If you come, you will receive me, my body and blood, and that will be your salvation. And it is the Spirit, capital S, the Holy Spirit, who gives this life. The flesh is no help at all.

So the offense of the word picture he used, that you must eat my flesh and drink my blood, he now clarifies. He is speaking of a life given in the Spirit. You receive into yourself the body and blood of Jesus Christ by his Holy Spirit. He said he is the bread of life and you must eat it. Now he says it is the Spirit who gives that life. You take in the body and blood of Jesus Christ by the Holy Spirit. That's what Jesus is saying.

Verses 64 and 65: "'But there are some of you who do not believe.' (For Jesus knew from the beginning who those were who did not believe, and who it was who would betray him.) And he said, 'This is why I told you that no one can come to me unless it is granted him by the Father.'" Again he doubles down. This is why I told you. That's twice in the same teaching that Jesus says no one can come to him. And the word "can" covers both ability and permission. The first time, he speaks of ability: no one can come to me unless the Father drags him, carries him on his own back to me. Like a man with no legs, you don't have the ability to walk to me or pursue me. I pursue you. The second time, he speaks of permission: no one can come to me unless it is granted him by the Father. So in our dead, sinful state, nothing in us, neither our ability nor God's permission, allows us to approach Jesus. Our coming to Jesus is entirely in the hands of God. It is a gift of God given to us. He said so clearly last week: eternal life is a gift that the Son of Man will give you.

And verse 64 says, "Jesus knew from the beginning who those were who did not believe, and who it was who would betray him." When John writes "from the beginning," we know what he means, because he has already told us what he means by the beginning. The very beginning. From the beginning Jesus knew who would believe and who would not, and he knew who would betray him. So he gives us this narrowing focus for understanding his teaching: it will be God alone who brings people from death to life so that they can live forever, abundantly, with Jesus Christ. And that is a deep offense.

Verse 66 is the response: "After this many of his disciples turned back and no longer walked with him." We have to see this. The crowd was large until Jesus taught this, and once he taught it, the crowd got small. It shrank not when he said, you have to do this and you have to do that. When it was a list of rules, a whole lot of people thought, all right, I've got this. But the moment he said, there's nothing you can do; the Lord must do it, the crowd thinned. Suddenly it's deeply offensive to be told that my faith is not a gift I give to God but a gift he gives to me.

This overlaps with last week, but Jesus is dismantling the idea that people can come to him with demands: do this for us, make it rain bread, and we'll believe, as if our faith were a gift we give him. When he turns that on its head and says, no, your faith is a gift I give you, the crowd leaves. They want nothing to do with it. And a great many people today, in our communities, perhaps in our churches, certainly all across America, are very willing to build huge crowds around a Jesus they've invented. They tell him what they want and what their will is and what he must do to be a good Jesus, and they say, in return, if you do everything I expect and fall in line with my will, I'll give you the gift of my allegiance, my faith. I'll follow you as long as you listen to me. So there are fifty million Jesuses being worshiped in this country, but only one of them is the real Jesus, and he won't let his followers define him. He says, no. I tell you who I am. I am the bread of life.

This is sobering, but it matters, because it shows how specific Jesus' calling on your life and mine is. I don't want to sow seeds of doubt in believers. If you belong to Christ, you belong to him. He has made it so clear. He has given you the comfort that all whom the Father gives him are secure in his hand. He will not lose a single one. That is your comfort this morning. But as we said last week, that comfort is hard to receive if we don't understand how we became his. If we earned it, we can lose it. If it depends on something about us, that thing can stop being true, and then he'll cast us out. So he's teaching us how it is that we come to be his.

And it is exclusive. Highly exclusive. Jesus said, I am the way, the truth and the life; no one comes to the Father except through me. He said no one comes to me unless the Father draws him. He says no one comes to the Father or to him except by the power of the Spirit. There are not many paths to eternal life. There is one. That's something we share with the people in this passage: we live in an age when the most offensive thing you can say is that there is one exclusive way, one truth, one life, one Jesus, and that we don't get to define him.

We share it with every generation before us, because it was the original sin to say to God, I reject you as God and claim the throne for myself. I decide what's best for me. We reduce him to useful, worshiping him only as far as he serves our will. But Jesus said, I have come to do the will of my Father who sent me. And when he says he'll send the Spirit to us, he says the Spirit will do his will perfectly. We were designed to enter that unity, union with Christ, in which we would experience true belonging, true adoption, an identity-changing, identity-shaping belonging that everyone is searching for.

It's exclusive, and it's exclusive in the best way. You're not going to walk into a bar and fight for every woman in the room. But if someone makes a pass at your girl, you're ready to scrap. There is a particular love set on those who belong to you. We believe love is a verb, a choice, because it's shown and embodied for us in Jesus Christ, who said himself, "Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends." God's love is an active verb. He made us his own and gave us a new identity in Christ with a specific love, pursuing you specifically and bringing you into new life. Yes, he fed the multitudes. Everyone ate. But not everyone received eternal life. Not everyone was given permission and ability by the Father to come to Jesus. In fact, most of them would abandon him right here, at the mere suggestion that it works that way.

Verses 66 to 69: "After this many of his disciples turned back and no longer walked with him. So Jesus said to the twelve, 'Do you want to go away as well?' Simon Peter answered him, 'Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life, and we have believed, and have come to know, that you are the Holy One of God.'"

That's a response of joyful surrender. Jesus has just said, in no uncertain terms, that everyone the Father gives him will come to him and he will not let them go. And Peter answers, Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life. If we believe this, then we're among those you said will come to you and whom you'll never let go. Where else can we go? We can't escape this. We can't run from it. We're yours. It's joyful surrender, like the adoption I talked about last week. You can run, but you can't hide. You belong to him now, and even your running is different. When you take your kids to the park, you're not watching everyone else's kids like a hawk. They can fall. But your kid can run, and can't hide. You'll make sure they're all right. Peter is recognizing whom he belongs to.

Verses 70 and 71: "Jesus answered them, 'Did I not choose you, the twelve? And yet one of you is a devil.' He spoke of Judas the son of Simon Iscariot, for he, one of the twelve, was going to betray him." I'll preach this in two parts.

First: "Did I not choose you, the twelve?" The exchange starts with Jesus asking, are you going to go away too? Peter answers, where would we go? And Jesus answers, did I not choose you? I wonder whether we really let ourselves believe that to the point where it comforts us. I think all of us believe it to the point where it makes us uncomfortable. Having a God who chooses is very uncomfortable, because if he's in control, I'm not. And that's hard about everything. Think about half of your arguments with your spouse: we want to be in control. When we start yielding to the Lord's sovereign control over matters like salvation and our standing with him, it's easy to feel uncomfortable. But will we believe it deeply enough to be comforted by it? If it still makes you uncomfortable, that's the struggle between a child and his father over control. I want to be able to say I had a say in this.

My wife has given me permission to share this. A year or two ago, a pastor told the story of a father who ran marathons and triathlons with his disabled son, pushing and pulling him the whole way, so that the son was decorated with every medal the father won. And my wife, in a moment of vulnerability and honesty, said to me and to our small group, "I love that for that kid, and I hate it for me. I don't want to be carried across the finish line. I want to be the one who ran the race. I want to be the one who earned it." She put into words for all of us what we naturally do with God: thank you, but no thank you. We won't let ourselves be comforted by it, because it would mean a surrender that says, I don't earn what you give freely. Worse, I can't earn what you give freely. It would require honesty about our weakness, our frailty, our sin and our undeservedness, honesty that God doesn't owe us anything and that everything he has given us is grace alone. That's hard, until it becomes comfort.

Here the disciples are beginning to enter that comfort as they resign themselves: to whom else shall we go? You alone have the words of eternal life. It is through death to self that we find new life in him. As long as we cling to our works, our good deeds, our worthiness, something about us that makes us lovable and adoptable, we'll stay locked in this resistance with God and find it offensive that he's a God who chooses. But if the salvation of God depended on you, that would be a terrifying prospect. As long as it rests in the hands of God, there's hope. If it rests on you, good night.

So he says it directly: "Did I not choose you, the twelve? And yet one of you is a devil." Notice what Jesus did not say. He didn't say, did I not choose you, the eleven, and one of you is a devil? He says, did I not choose you, the twelve? And yet one of you is a devil. And John, struck by it, repeats it: he spoke of Judas, for he, one of the twelve, was going to betray him. We're being begged to ask: why would Jesus choose his betrayer to be one of the twelve? He certainly wasn't caught off guard. We were just told that Jesus knew from the beginning who did not believe and who would betray him. So why choose his betrayer to be one of the twelve?

I have two answers this morning. Jesus gives the first himself, twice in John. In John 13, on the night he washes his disciples' feet before he is betrayed, he says, "I am not speaking of all of you; I know whom I have chosen. But the Scripture will be fulfilled, 'He who ate my bread has lifted his heel against me.' I am telling you this now, before it takes place, that when it does take place you may believe that I am he. Truly, truly, I say to you, whoever receives the one I send receives me, and whoever receives me receives the one who sent me." There he quotes Psalm 41:9, if you want to write it down. Quoting David's prophecy that the one who ate his bread would lift his heel against him, Jesus says the reason he chose his betrayer to be one of the twelve was so that Scripture would be fulfilled.

He says it again in his high priestly prayer, John 17:11 and 12: "Holy Father, keep them in your name, which you have given me, that they may be one, even as we are one. While I was with them, I kept them in your name, which you have given me. I have guarded them, and not one of them has been lost except the son of destruction, that the Scripture might be fulfilled." Again, that the Scripture might be fulfilled.

So, reason one: Jesus chose his betrayer to be one of the twelve so that Scripture would be fulfilled. Stay with the thread, because this is why we preached this in three parts. Those Scriptures were centuries old, and Jesus has been showing himself to be the God of the Exodus, the God of the Old Testament, the God of eternity, the God who was there from the beginning. When he walks the earth fulfilling Scripture written ages before, he is showing us something. He is also showing a sovereign power we have to come to terms with: the power to choose, when he came into the world, who his crowd would be, which twelve he would lay his hand on and bring into new life, and who would be his betrayer. If he sovereignly ordains all of it, then he certainly has the sovereign power to choose.

That is reason one, and it's comforting. We worship a Jesus so sovereign and so in control that he carries out Old Testament prophecy as he walks the earth. He fulfills the promises of the Old Testament. He looks across the whole arc of redemptive history, from creation through the law, the prophets, the priests and the judges, and brings it all to fruition in his own life, death and resurrection. If he can do that, we can be encouraged that Jesus Christ is the promise keeper, the Anointed One, the one sent from God, the one on whom everything in the Old Testament rested. He is bringing it all to pass. Amazing.

Reason two is that he would show us that nothing evil or bad in this life ever catches him off guard, and that all of it is being used to carry out his good purposes. We know this, because if a Roman cross can be an instrument of life, then certainly every evil thing can be, and is being, used by God to accomplish his good purposes in redemptive history. Every evil thing, including the worst thing you can imagine, betrayal with a kiss by a friend from the inner circle, is being used to accomplish God's plan of redemption.

We have to see it. Jesus foreknew and foreordained that his betrayer would be among the twelve, so that he would be betrayed at the right hour, sentenced to die on a cross, laid in a grave, raised from that grave and ascended to the right hand of the Father, so that he would redeem his church. He did it for you. All of those choices were him choosing you. He was carrying out God's plan to ransom a remnant for himself and build a church as a beautiful tapestry of worship to the Son. This is his work, his choosing, his design, his method, by his power. And we are the recipients.

That means that all the evil that has happened in your life, and all the evil you have committed, has never at any point fallen outside the eternal plan of God. Your sin was written in. He already knew it, factored it in, and carries out his good purposes through it and despite it. In fact, we come to experience him and his glory and the riches of his mercy in unique ways precisely because he weaves in the sin and evil of humankind and of this world.

This is meant to comfort you, because sometimes it's you. Some of you are carrying guilt for things you yourselves have done, the secret and dark sins of your own life, things you can't forgive yourself for, things you think you've ruined so badly that you must have knocked God's good plans off course. I'm telling you, the Lord saw it, knew it and factored it in. He is working out his redemptive plan through you, in you and despite you, and if you belong to him, you are forgiven.

For some of you it's not so much your guilt. You've been the victim of other people's evil, people who harmed you in real ways, made you bleed and made you cry, who left you calling out for God to be your Father because he's the only one you've ever been shown you can count on. Even their evil has been factored in, folded into God's plan to bring about his redemption.

We can know this from Acts 4. Turn there with me, starting in verse 23. Peter and John have been brought before the council, where Annas and Caiaphas were present, some of the same men who oversaw the sham trials of Jesus. After they stand before the council, they go to the other believers to report what happened: "When they were released, they went to their friends and reported what the chief priests and the elders had said to them. And when they heard it, they lifted their voices together to God and said, 'Sovereign Lord, who made the heaven and the earth and the sea and everything in them, who through the mouth of our father David, your servant, said by the Holy Spirit, "Why did the Gentiles rage, and the peoples plot in vain? The kings of the earth set themselves, and the rulers were gathered together, against the Lord and against his Anointed"—for truly in this city there were gathered together against your holy servant Jesus, whom you anointed, both Herod and Pontius Pilate, along with the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel, to do whatever your hand and your plan had predestined to take place. And now, Lord, look upon their threats and grant to your servants to continue to speak your word with all boldness, while you stretch out your hand to heal, and signs and wonders are performed through the name of your holy servant Jesus.' And when they had prayed, the place in which they were gathered together was shaken, and they were all filled with the Holy Spirit and continued to speak the word of God with boldness."

So when I tell you that your evil, and the evil done against you, never fell outside the plans of God to bring about his redemption of creation, I mean it. Listen to how they said it: in this city there were gathered together against your holy servant Jesus, Herod, Pontius Pilate, the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel, to do what? Whatever your hand and your plan had predestined to take place. All of it, every detail, falls squarely within his sovereign control and his sovereign plan.

So what do we do with that? The truth is, we'd like to believe God is a beggar, holding out his hat, who would really like us to do what he wants, who asks us to come but has no power to make it happen, looking at you and me and saying, please come, please obey. We strip him of his power and say, you can ask, but you can't make. That's a horrifying prospect: a well-meaning God who can't carry out what he wills, who depends on you and me to carry out his plan in creation and is easily thwarted by a world power, by a Herod or a Pontius Pilate, or by your dad. That's not our God. He has factored the fallen nature of humankind into his redemptive plan, and every single detail is accounted for as he brings about new life.

Let me let Paul say it more directly, in Romans 9. Listen closely: "I am speaking the truth in Christ—I am not lying; my conscience bears me witness in the Holy Spirit—that I have great sorrow and unceasing anguish in my heart. For I could wish that I myself were accursed and cut off from Christ for the sake of my brothers, my kinsmen according to the flesh. They are Israelites, and to them belong the adoption, the glory, the covenants, the giving of the law, the worship, and the promises. To them belong the patriarchs, and from their race, according to the flesh, is the Christ, who is God over all, blessed forever. Amen."

In this first part Paul is saying, if I could become accursed so that my kinsmen would be saved, I would, but I can't. The Israelites, the Jews, my own people, won't come to Christ. They've rejected him, and it breaks my heart, because the promises and covenants were made to them, and the Christ was born from their people. He is stricken with sorrow. And in answer to his own heart, he says, "But it is not as though the word of God has failed. For not all who are descended from Israel belong to Israel, and not all are children of Abraham because they are his offspring, but 'Through Isaac shall your offspring be named.' This means that it is not the children of the flesh who are the children of God, but the children of the promise are counted as offspring. For this is what the promise said: 'About this time next year I will return, and Sarah shall have a son.' And not only so, but also when Rebekah had conceived children by one man, our forefather Isaac, though they were not yet born and had done nothing either good or bad—in order that God's purpose of election might continue, not because of works but because of him who calls—she was told, 'The older will serve the younger.' As it is written, 'Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated.'"

Now Paul connects this to Isaac, Jacob and Esau. God promised Abraham that the blessing would come through his offspring, and it's the children of the promise who inherit eternal life. Yet the Jews have rejected Christ. So not everyone born of Abraham is Israel. It must be those born of what? Of the Spirit. That's Paul's point: we misunderstood it. Before Jacob and Esau were born, God chose that Jacob would be his appointed one and Esau would be passed over.

I'm sure you're responding to that in your heart, so Paul answers you. Verse 14: "What shall we say then? Is there injustice on God's part? By no means! For he says to Moses, 'I will have mercy on whom I have mercy, and I will have compassion on whom I have compassion.' So then it depends not on human will or exertion, but on God, who has mercy. For the Scripture says to Pharaoh, 'For this very purpose I have raised you up, that I might show my power in you, and that my name might be proclaimed in all the earth.' So then he has mercy on whomever he wills, and he hardens whomever he wills. You will say to me then, 'Why does he still find fault? For who can resist his will?' But who are you, O man, to answer back to God? Will what is molded say to its molder, 'Why have you made me like this?' Has the potter no right over the clay, to make out of the same lump one vessel for honorable use and another for dishonorable use? What if God, desiring to show his wrath and to make known his power, has endured with much patience vessels of wrath prepared for destruction, in order to make known the riches of his glory for vessels of mercy, which he has prepared beforehand for glory—even us whom he has called, not from the Jews only but also from the Gentiles?"

In Romans 9, Paul takes what Jesus is saying here and applies it across the whole arc of redemptive history. If we really think it through: when God flooded the earth and rescued Noah, he showed himself to be a God who chooses. When he said, Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated, he showed himself to be a God who chooses. When he hardened Pharaoh's heart but ransomed his people Israel, not because of their works but by the blood of a lamb, he showed himself to be a God who chooses. And when he tells us that those who have new life in the Spirit received it as a free gift of God, he shows himself to be a God who chooses. And he chose you.

What I've seen happen when we really start believing this is apathy toward evangelism and mission: if God chooses and he's in control, just let him do it. And yet he commanded us. In Matthew, Jesus said, "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me. Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you. And behold, I am with you always, to the end of the age." Why go? In Acts, when Paul is in Corinth, a major city where Christ is barely known, the Lord says to him in a vision, "Do not be afraid, but go on speaking and do not be silent, for I am with you, and no one will attack you to harm you, for I have many in this city who are my people." I have many in this city who are my people, present tense, in a city that had barely heard the gospel. That emboldened Paul all the more to preach the gospel, because, as he said, he was not ashamed of it: it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, and to those who are perishing it is a fragrance of death. So we go all the more, declaring the gospel where we live, because we believe Jesus has many people in our city. He already knows them, and all whom the Father has given him will come to him. We need only obey and proclaim the gospel, and we will see him work.

But before we ever do that, we first need to come to know and enjoy our own election, our own being chosen. In every other relationship you want to be chosen. You want someone to look on you and set their affection on you, to love you with a love that is a verb, to decide to set their care on you, to love you by name with a particular affection. And we don't want to let God love us that way. But this is the way the Lord loves us. When you've really tasted it and seen it, and come to understand that it had nothing to do with you, that's when you'll go. That's when you'll share with others a gospel that has nothing to do with them either.

And when we understand that our God looked across time and space and set his affection on us, we respond by setting our affection on others. Some of you already do this, for example by sponsoring a child in need, choosing that specific child and setting your care on them. Let's be a people who proactively set our affection on others in response to the affection that has been set on us. I celebrate that the Lord moves in us and actually changes us, so that we can follow him and walk in his likeness as we were designed to do. Let's take time to respond to him in prayer.
