# Love One Another (John 15:12-17)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 15:12-17

---

Our task this morning in John 15:12 to 17 is to work out what Jesus means when he calls his disciples his friends, and what it means when he calls us, even commands us, to love one another the way he has loved us. As I wrote this sermon this week, one of my main thoughts was that he's saying this to men he walked with for three years, night and day, hour by hour, minute by minute. To them he says, love one another. I give you this commandment: love one another the way I have loved you. As we'll see, he didn't feel much need in this passage to spell out for them all the ways he had loved them.

He just said, love one another the way I've loved you. Everything we've been doing these last three years: like that. Different parts of his love may have come to mind as they thought about all the ways Jesus had loved them, because he had. He loved them personally and individually, and he loved them as a group of disciples. No one needed to ask, what do you mean when you say you love us?

They had met his love again and again, in so many ways, over years. So my first order of business, as I wrote this and as I talk with you now, is to show that when you've encountered the love of Jesus, you don't have to think very long to know what he means when he says he loves you. My first move was to ask a bunch of people I love a simple question. I told them, on Sunday I have to preach the command of Jesus to love one another the way he has loved us.

It seems to me it would be great to hear from some people: what's your favorite way Jesus has loved you? So I asked. Last night I was having dinner with my father-in-law, and I asked him. He gave me half an answer and stored up the rest in his heart, then pulled me aside later that night to talk privately. He said, Adam, I've been thinking about your question. I've heard that in certain professions, in certain circumstances, people can identify the smell of death. You come to know it, and when you're in the presence of death, something in you intuitively knows it.

And I've found that something similar is true of hopelessness. I was three years old when my mother drove me to the orphanage for the first time. When I walked in, I smelled hopelessness. What I love most about the way Christ has loved me is that every day I've belonged to Jesus, I've never been without hope, because I have an advocate.

I asked my youngest son, and he said instinctively, Dad, he literally died for me. He's seven. My middle son knows the story of the crisis during my wife's pregnancy with him and the miracle surrounding his birth, and he said, Jesus not only saved me literally, he saved my soul. My mother-in-law said she loves the way he has protected her from herself.

My wife said, I love the way he sees me, the way he knows me, the way he has been intimate with me. I could keep going, of course. What I'm showing you is that when you ask someone who has been loved about the love they've received, they don't have to think long before they start telling you how they've been loved. And that's especially true when you've received the love of Christ. You can't miss the love of Christ.

The love of Christ is full of so many wonderful stories that when he says to you, love one another the way I have loved you, it ought to come quite naturally to start thinking about the ways he has loved you. So here's the warning this morning, as far as the command goes: if you haven't tasted and seen the love of Christ for yourself, if you haven't received it, you can't obey this. Jesus doesn't bother to give all the details of how he loved them, but he does bother with one specific way, the highest, greatest way he would love his disciples, and it hadn't happened yet. So it makes sense that he'd talk with them about it, because he hadn't yet laid down his life. Beyond that, he leaves it to them, with his help in that moment and with the Holy Spirit's help in the future, to understand what it means to receive his love and be loved by him.

But here's what I want you to hear up front: you must first receive and know the love of Christ before any of this applies to you. And if you have received and known the love of Christ, then all of it applies to you, and it will be so. Do you understand? So Jesus says, verses 12 to 15, "This is my commandment, that you love one another as I have loved you.

"Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends. You are my friends if you do what I command you. No longer do I call you servants, for the servant does not know what his master is doing; but I have called you friends, for all that I have heard from my Father I have made known to you." Jesus introduces a couple of ideas here.

The first is that he wants us to love one another. His charge to the disciples in the room, and through them his charge to us, is that we love one another. This morning we're talking about love for our brothers and sisters in the faith. We could talk about many other commands to love: to love the outsider, the outcast, the lost, all kinds of people.

But this passage is specifically about Christians loving Christians, the church loving the church, disciples loving disciples. We're talking about the love without which, Jesus said, the world will never know we are his disciples: "By this all people will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another." I want to stay laser-focused on the church loving the church this morning, because that's what Jesus is talking about. If you've received the love of Christ, and you're in the company of someone who shares that story, your love for one another is unique among all the loves on earth.

The love two people have for one another when both have received this supernatural love from Christ Jesus will be foreign to any love the world can offer itself apart from Christ. It's even different from the love between a believer and an unbeliever, because that love can't be returned in the highest sense. We're talking about the highest earthly love between human beings: the love of a Christian for a Christian, people who understand how much they've been forgiven, how much they've been loved, how much they've been touched by the grace of God, so that they can extend it to one another.

So Jesus gives this commandment, that we love one another as he has loved us. I'll hold the next verse, verse 13, and preach it as the central verse after I work through the others. All I want to draw from it at this point is that it reintroduces the idea of friends, because Jesus is about to talk about what it means to be his friend. Verse 14: "You are my friends if you do what I command you." Connect "if you do what I command you" with what he said in verse 12: "This is my commandment."

What is the command you follow that marks you as his friend? This one: that you love one another as I have loved you. I don't want to open this text up to mean a thousand different things. Jesus is laser-focused and very clear in this passage. This is my commandment.

Love one another. You are my friends if you do what I command you. He's talking about the commandment he just gave. And when we talk about the commandments of God, as I was saying to someone out front this morning, I felt a certain agitation as I thought about the commands of my Lord Jesus Christ. When he commanded the wind and the waves, what did they do? They obeyed.

When he commanded a legion of demons, what did they do? They obeyed. When he commanded the dead to rise, what did they do? When he commanded sickness to leave, what did it do? So how arrogant of us, his children, to suppose that when he commands us, he's just hoping for the best?

The children of Christ, the friends of Christ, aren't in some category separate from everything else. We yield when he commands. All things yield when he commands. When Jesus Christ commands something, you can take it to the bank. It is so.

If you want proof, let Jesus give it. At the end of this passage, in verse 17, he says, "These things I command you, so that you will love one another." Not so that you might. Look back at verse 16: "You did not choose me, but I chose you and appointed you that you should go and bear fruit and that your fruit should abide." It will be so. We have the confidence of assurance, those of us who are in Christ.

If we are his friends, we will obey. Jesus says we are his friends if we keep his commands, and I think we need to explore that word "if" for a minute. We're conditioned to read "if" statements as connecting reward to behavior, as if Jesus were saying, if you jump over the hurdle, you get the dog treat.

As if that's what he means when he says, you are my friends if you keep my commandments: you keep the commandments and thereby become his friend, and friendship with him is the reward for keeping the commandments. But this is a very different kind of "if." You're very familiar with this other kind of "if"; you just never think about it. It's more like this: if you're wearing the collar I put on you, you're my dog. Not sure you're my dog? Well, are you wearing the collar?

There you go. You're my dog. Look at the flow of his statement. Verse 12: "This is my commandment, that you love one another as I have loved you." Have loved: past tense. He already loves you.

"Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends." He calls them friends. "You are my friends if you do what I command you. No longer do I call you servants, for the servant does not know what his master is doing; but I have called you friends, for all that I have heard from my Father I have made known to you." I chose you, and I appointed you.

He couldn't make it any clearer that he's giving a command to his friends, not giving a command so that we might become his friends. Those are categorically different. So he says in verse 14, you are my friends if you do what I command you. No longer do I call you servants.

The Greek word is doulos, often translated "slave." It's complicated. "For the servant does not know what his master is doing; but I have called you friends, for all that I have heard from my Father I have made known to you." So he starts talking about how we are no longer servants or slaves, but friends.

He builds it all around the fact that he has revealed the words of the Father to us. He shows us layers of relationship: the Father communicates to the Son, and the Son has friends, and he communicates to his friends what the Father communicates to him. Everything I've received from the Father, I've made known to you. That's why I no longer call you servants or slaves, because a servant doesn't know what his master is doing.

Some parts of the picture stay the same after the move from servant to friend. There's still a master, and he's still doing things. What changes is your relationship to the master: you actually know what he's doing. I hope I'm not making it too simple, but I picture a master preparing a feast, with a great reason to celebrate.

We could apply all sorts of doctrines to that; maybe we imagine the feast of heaven. He's making all the plans for this feast, and he tells his delighted son all about it. It's for the son and his friends. He tells him everything, and the son is very excited.

There's this building excitement about what the father is doing, planning a great feast. But the servant, the slave, hears only, go slaughter the choice calf. Go start a fire. Go.

Go gather the best wine. He isn't invited into the details of why he's doing any of it or what's happening. He just carries out the master's commands. I try to do what I think I'm supposed to do, but I have no idea why I'm doing it. I even start assigning reasons to my activity that are flatly false, even heretical. Like misapplying verse 14.

You are my friends if you do what I command you: if I make that mean I obey the commands of God and become his friend because of it, I'm not a Christian. I'm a slave. But to say, as a friend of God, I obey his commandments: that's Christianity.

So Jesus declares, I don't call you servants. A servant doesn't know what his master is doing. I've called you friends, because all that I've heard from the Father I've made known to you. That means all our obedience, including obedience to this commandment, serves not to qualify us for Jesus but to confirm that we belong to him. So when we sin, when we don't obey the commands of Jesus, what we lose is the assurance that comes from obeying them.

We lack that confirmation that we are who Jesus says we are. But rest assured: if he commands it, it will be so. He has appointed you to go and bear fruit. So if you belong to him, you will bear fruit. And Jesus makes this claim: "Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends."

Let me read you Romans 5:6 to 8. Paul writes, "For while we were still weak, at the right time Christ died for the ungodly. For one will scarcely die for a righteous person—though perhaps for a good person one would dare even to die—but God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us." Romans 5 paints a picture in which the love of God is magnified the more we understand how unworthy the object of his love is: no one has greater love than this, that he would love his enemy.

Not that he would love his friend. In that passage we're told that human beings will, rarely, "scarcely," die for a righteous person. Perhaps, more likely, someone would even dare to die for a good person. But what makes God's love divine, what makes it higher, the greater love, is that he would die for us while we were still sinners, die for the ungodly, for the unrighteous. Here, though, Jesus says something different.

In John 15 he says, "Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends." Maybe I'm just inviting you into confusion you never would have thought of. Sorry. In Romans 5, the quality of God's love is magnified by the unworthiness of the objects of his love.

What Jesus talks about here is the quality of God's love magnified by its price. He emphasizes the laying down of one's life: not the recipient, but the act itself. Put them together, and you have a God who lays down his life to make his enemies his friends. Together, it's too wonderful for words.

But if Jesus Christ lays down his life for his friends, then of course we want to be found among those friends. If it's for his friends that he laid down his life, how do we come to be his friends? If I've said it once, I'll say it a thousand times: by grace alone. We don't earn the favor of Christ.

We don't earn the salvation of the Lord. We don't take the position of the deserving poor. I emphasize "deserving poor," because in some ways I find this is rising to rival self-righteousness as the main departure from orthodox Christianity. I think we all know what I mean by self-righteous: I do good, and God rewards me.

That's garbage. It's rubbish. It binds you for hell. But a second idea is growing in popularity: that I'm the deserving poor, that God takes pity on my moral poverty, not because of what Christ has done, but because he feels bad for me. He looks at the things that happened to me, and that's enough for him to turn a blind eye to my sin. I find an excuse before the Father for my lack of perfect holiness, not in what Christ has done, but in the fact that he understands.

That too is a damnable lie. Your right standing before God the Father rests on the merits of Christ alone, not on your merits, and not on the excuses we have for what we haven't done. All of us stand without excuse for falling short of the glory of God, which is why it's such great news that he came for us. So he claims that no love is greater, magnified by its price, his very life laid down for us, and, as Romans 5 makes clear, by the unworthiness of those he loves. He says we're no longer slaves because his words have been made known to us, and here we're stumbling into the doctrine of adoption.

In this contrast between friends and slaves, the slave doesn't know what the master is doing, but the friend does. Think about being over at a friend's house when you were a kid. The dad gives the plan for the day: later we're going to do this, and then that. And your friend runs to you: my dad says we're going to do this, and then this, and then this. And then the father does it, the son is included, and the son's friends get to be part of it too, not because of anything the friends have done, but because the father loves the son and invites him to bring his friends along.

It's an imperfect picture, but I hope it helps. It's because we've been adopted, and this comes from Romans 8, starting at verse 12: "So then, brothers, we are debtors, not to the flesh, to live according to the flesh. For if you live according to the flesh you will die, but if by the Spirit you put to death the deeds of the body, you will live. For all who are led by the Spirit of God are sons of God."

"For you did not receive the spirit of slavery to fall back into fear, but you have received the Spirit of adoption as sons, by whom we cry, 'Abba! Father!' The Spirit himself bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God, and if children, then heirs—heirs of God and fellow heirs with Christ, provided we suffer with him in order that we may also be glorified with him." Here we're introduced to the idea of becoming a debtor to grace, because of our adoption. Tim Keller tells a story,

and he's told it many times. I don't remember the details exactly, but it goes something like this. He was sharing the gospel with someone, teaching her that we are adopted into the family of God by grace alone, and he saw the scales fall from her eyes as she said, if it's by grace alone, then I owe him everything.

Her first, natural assumption had been: if I earn it, then he owes it to me. I have him on a leash just as he has me on a leash; we work together on this. But if I didn't earn it, if he did it by grace alone, then I owe him everything. And in that way, while we are freed from our bondage and slavery to sin, we become slaves of God.

Somehow, to be a friend of God is to be a servant of God, because what he has done by grace has made him Lord. So as we come under the lordship of Christ, we carry on with this idea from Romans 6: "For sin will have no dominion over you, since you are not under law but under grace." And from verse 16: "Do you not know that if you present yourselves to anyone as obedient slaves, you are slaves of the one whom you obey, either of sin, which leads to death, or of obedience, which leads to righteousness?

"But thanks be to God," listen closely, "that you who were once slaves of sin have become obedient from the heart to the standard of teaching to which you were committed, and, having been set free from sin, have become slaves of righteousness. I am speaking in human terms, because of your natural limitations. For just as you once presented your members as slaves to impurity and to lawlessness leading to more lawlessness, so now present your members as slaves to righteousness leading to sanctification." Just a little more. Listen closely.

"For when you were slaves of sin, you were free in regard to righteousness. But what fruit were you getting at that time from the things of which you are now ashamed? For the end of those things is death. But now that you have been set free from sin and have become slaves of God, the fruit you get leads to sanctification and its end, eternal life. For the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord."

Amen? So Jesus speaks of friendship with him as a transfer from slavery to sin to slavery to God: from serving the flesh and serving sin to serving our Father. We're not saved into autonomy. We're not saved into our best self. We're saved into the ranks of the Almighty.

His voice now drives what we do. And what activity is he chiefly concerned with? Circling all the way back around: that we love one another the way he has loved us. You must bear the fruit of love for your brothers and sisters in Christ. This is the great assurance of your salvation, the assurance of your standing.

The assurance that you are a son is this: do you love the other sons? Do you love the other daughters? Do you share a Father? And under the umbrella of his love, does it drive you to love one another, so that the world may know you are truly his disciples? And just as Jesus didn't feel the need to stop and give them thirty-seven ways they must love one another, I don't want to do that for you either.

But when I ask you, what's your favorite way Jesus has loved you, you'll be able to answer. I'd start there when it comes to loving other people. Does that make sense? And you'll find that as you submit to the lordship of Christ, his commands reach into every corner of your life, so that your whole life is lived in worship of the King, which means lived in love for his children. You must love the church.

Receive the love of Christ, then love one another in the same way. And as you love one another that way, let it confirm that you are in the love of Christ. It's circular, and it's beautiful. In some future passage we'll talk about how that overflows into love for the world and love for the lost. But you will never love your neighbor if you don't love your brother.

And you will never love your brother if you don't know the love of God. Jesus is very clear here. And though this is brief, it's brief on purpose. I don't want to add to it or take away from it. So hear this invitation, and then we'll pray.

Love one another as I have loved you. Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends. Fully understood, fully applied, fully rooted in your life, this command requires you to lay down your life. It requires you to die for your friends. These men would literally go and do it; all but one, as far as we know, would go on to die for the sake of the church.

At the very least it means dying to self, to preference, to convenience, to comfort. You must love one another. By this the world will know that you are his disciples. Let's pray for it.
