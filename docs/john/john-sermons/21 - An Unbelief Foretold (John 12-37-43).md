# An Unbelief Foretold (John 12:37-43)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 12:37-43

---

I confess to you this morning that we're walking through more hard things. More hard things. I'll put it on the table up front: this is so hard that over the course of church history the church has split over it, new denominations have formed over it, and people have debated and argued over it. All that to say, I'm not going to stand up here and settle all of it in a single sermon.

What I'll try to do this morning is what I'm called to do every week: present what the text says, and trust the Holy Spirit to help us understand it. You may find I'm heavy on Scripture this morning, and I ought to be heavy on Scripture every morning. But I want to let the Scriptures speak here, speak for me, speak through me, and speak to you. Our Gospel writer, John, opened his account, if you remember, all the way back in chapter 1, by declaring that the light came into the world, and that though the world was made through him, the world did not know him.

"He came to his own, and his own people did not receive him. But to all who did receive him, who believed in his name, he gave the right to become children of God." So this Gospel opens with the declaration that the light was rejected, rejected by his very own.

He opens with that. And here, as I've been saying for the last couple of weeks, we're coming to the end. We're at the end of the public ministry of Jesus Christ. This is it.

After this he will withdraw, and the rest of his time will be spent with the twelve, and then the eleven, and then his passion week will come and he will rise again, and we'll walk through all of that. But this is it for his public ministry. John wrote in chapter 1 that this was coming; he led with the rejection of Christ.

And here John gives us the reason, an explanation for the rejection of the Christ. Why did his own people reject him? Why did his own people not receive him? That tension needs answering. Let's read it.

Verses 36 and 37: "When Jesus had said these things, he departed and hid himself from them. Though he had done so many signs before them, they still did not believe in him." We've gone straight through from chapter 1 to here, so we've read the signs.

We know the signs. We saw him give sight to the blind. We saw him raise Lazarus from the dead. We saw him multiply the loaves and the fish. We saw the signs, so many signs.

And John writes, still they did not believe in him. And he sets out to answer why. Before we jump in: if you're anything like me, as you've read these signs of the Savior through this Gospel, part of you has thought, if I'd been there, if I'd seen it, I would have believed. Or you look at the Israelites wandering in the desert: show me a pillar of cloud and fire.

I'd believe. But there's an arrogance in that. It says there's something about them that isn't true of you, that somehow, given enough evidence, you'd have found your way, climbed your way, reasoned your way into saving faith in God, in the Christ. I want to tear that down this morning. In ourselves, we are no different from the Israelites in any of these accounts. We're not. There is something that makes you different.

There is something that makes you different. I'll close with it rather than steal from my wrap-up. They saw all the signs. That's the tension. We want to ask, why?

Was something lacking in Jesus? Did he not do enough? Did he not go far enough? Did he not show himself the right way, at the right time, to the right people?

Was he not who we say he is? These were his people. If anyone should have believed, it should have been them. Was it failure?

Or was it a plan? What was it? Here's how John answers. Verses 37 and 38: "Though he had done so many signs before them, they still did not believe in him, so that the word spoken by the prophet Isaiah might be fulfilled: 'Lord, who has believed what he heard from us, and to whom has the arm of the Lord been revealed?'"

We'll turn to Isaiah in a minute. But first I want to put you in the room in Luke 2. You know the story if your family reads about the birth of Christ every year. In Luke 2 we meet a man named Simeon.

Anybody remember Simeon? The Spirit had shown Simeon that he would not see death until his eyes had seen the Messiah, the Christ. Luke 2, around verse 28. Mary and Joseph bring Jesus to the temple in Jerusalem, and Simeon, led by the Spirit, comes to the Christ as a baby.

He "took him up in his arms and blessed God and said, 'Lord, now you are letting your servant depart in peace, according to your word; for my eyes have seen your salvation that you have prepared in the presence of all peoples, a light for revelation to the Gentiles, and for glory to your people Israel.' And his father and his mother marveled at what was said about him." What an amazing prayer.

Verse 34: "And Simeon blessed them and said to Mary his mother, 'Behold, this child is appointed for the fall and rising of many in Israel, and for a sign that is opposed (and a sword will pierce through your own soul also), so that thoughts from many hearts may be revealed.'" So at Jesus' birth, Simeon, carried by the Spirit, who was told he would see the Christ with his own eyes, speaks a blessing over Mary: through this child many will fall and many will rise, and a sword will pierce even your own soul.

We know that Mary, his own mother, would come to faith in him as her Savior, as the Messiah. And we know he would be rejected. He would be rejected. From the beginning this was understood. We see it at his birth.

We see it at the beginning of John's Gospel. And here, at the end of his public ministry, seeking to give an answer, John has the whole counsel of Scripture to choose from. He's led by the Spirit, so this is God choosing Isaiah 53 to bring answer and closure. But let me ask you: what would you choose? If I asked you to use Scripture alone to help me understand why Jesus' public ministry among his own people met with such rejection and failure,

where would you point me? What chapter and verse? Would you end up in Isaiah? God ends up in Isaiah through John. He gives us Isaiah 53.

Let me turn there and read it to you. Isaiah writes, "Who has believed what he has heard from us? And to whom has the arm of the LORD been revealed?"

"For he grew up before him like a young plant, and like a root out of dry ground; he had no form or majesty that we should look at him, and no beauty that we should desire him. He was despised and rejected by men, a man of sorrows and acquainted with grief; and as one from whom men hide their faces he was despised, and we esteemed him not." I've been described as many things in my life, and I would never want to be described as a root sprung up out of dry ground.

We're talking about frailty: small in stature, weak, meek. "No beauty that we should desire him." Plain at best. Jesus was probably ugly. No beauty that we should desire him.

Frail, like a root sprung up out of dry ground. So, number one, why was he rejected? At the end of our passage this morning we'll see that some people prefer the glory that comes from man to the glory that comes from God. And when you look at a man like the one described in Isaiah 53, you're looking at a man stripped of all the human glory you might be looking for. They wanted a man.

Big, strong, a conqueror, a king, prospering, something to look at. This is my guy. This is my champion. This is my king. A Goliath type. I want my Messiah to have all the glory of man, to be the best of us, the best we have to offer. But they got Jesus.

Small, plain. It even feels strange to say it up here. I don't like it. You can't call Jesus ugly. But Isaiah says it: no beauty that we should desire him.

He didn't come wielding any of the things we ask men to wield before we'll bow to them. He offered us none of that. And Isaiah prophesied that they would not believe when they saw him with their own eyes, because he would be none of the things they were looking for. He was despised and rejected.

Verse 4 of the same chapter: "Surely he has borne our griefs and carried our sorrows; yet we esteemed him stricken, smitten by God, and afflicted." That's what we thought of him. "But he was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities."

"Upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace, and with his wounds we are healed. All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned—every one—to his own way; and the LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all. He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth; like a lamb that is led to the slaughter, and like a sheep that before its shearers is silent, so he opened not his mouth."

"By oppression and judgment he was taken away; and as for his generation, who considered that he was cut off out of the land of the living, stricken for the transgression of my people? And they made his grave with the wicked and with a rich man in his death, although he had done no violence, and there was no deceit in his mouth. Yet it was the will of the LORD to crush him; he has put him to grief; when his soul makes an offering for guilt, he shall see his offspring; he shall prolong his days; the will of the LORD shall prosper in his hand."

"Out of the anguish of his soul he shall see and be satisfied; by his knowledge shall the righteous one, my servant, make many to be accounted righteous, and he shall bear their iniquities. Therefore I will divide him a portion with the many, and he shall divide the spoil with the strong, because he poured out his soul to death and was numbered with the transgressors; yet he bore the sin of many, and makes intercession for the transgressors." Why did the Christ meet such rejection and failure in his public ministry among his own people?

Our author John says it was so that Isaiah 53 would be fulfilled. This was the will of the Lord. Back to our passage, verses 39 to 41: "Therefore they could not believe. For again Isaiah said, 'He has blinded their eyes and hardened their heart, lest they see with their eyes, and understand with their heart, and turn, and I would heal them.'"

"Isaiah said these things because he saw his glory and spoke of him." Now John has gone from Isaiah 53 back several chapters to Isaiah 6:10. You may remember the scene. Isaiah is given a vision of the throne of God. He sees the seraphim around him and the train of God's robe filling the temple, and he's overcome with awe. He says, "Woe is me! For I am lost."

"For I am a man of unclean lips, and I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips; for my eyes have seen the King, the LORD of hosts!" And the Lord says, "Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?" And Isaiah says, "Here I am! Send me."

Send me. And God says to him, "Go, and say to this people: 'Keep on hearing, but do not understand; keep on seeing, but do not perceive.' Make the heart of this people dull, and their ears heavy, and blind their eyes; lest they see with their eyes, and hear with their ears, and understand with their hearts, and turn and be healed." Then Isaiah answers, "How long, O Lord?"

And he says, "Until cities lie waste without inhabitant, and houses without people, and the land is a desolate waste, and the LORD removes people far away, and the forsaken places are many in the midst of the land." Is that enough? No, not yet. Verse 13: "And though a tenth remain in it, it will be burned again, like a terebinth or an oak, whose stump remains when it is felled." The holy seed is its stump.

Hold on to that last sentence: the holy seed is its stump. If you take notes while I preach, and for time's sake, write this down.

Isaiah 53 and Isaiah 6. Study them this week. Wrestle with what I'm saying to you. The holy seed is its stump. The Lord commissioned Isaiah in Isaiah 6.

Isaiah beheld Jesus; John tells us it was Jesus he saw. Go as my prophet and say to my people: don't see, don't hear. Stay blind. Harden. Harden. Harden.

How long, O Lord? Until nothing is left but the holy seed. Until nothing is left but the holy seed. We'll spend the rest of our time this morning on the holy seed. Thank God. Verse 41: "Isaiah said these things because he saw his glory," Jesus' glory, "and spoke of him," Jesus.

The text shows me four words that I think answer everything I might want to say here. But first: what about you, who weren't there? You're not of his chosen people Israel. You're Gentiles. You weren't there. All you have is the word of the Lord.

Yet on hearing alone, reading alone, you bow your knee and worship the King, while his own people, who were there and saw it with their own eyes, rejected him. Why did they reject him? "So that the word spoken by the prophet Isaiah might be fulfilled." "Therefore they could not believe. For again Isaiah said, 'He has blinded their eyes.'"

"and hardened their heart, lest they see with their eyes, and understand with their heart, and turn." When you see "so that," "therefore," "for" and "lest" in these verses, you're looking at a God who is sovereign over hard hearts. And when the Lord God meets a heart of stone, like the one you had before you were given eyes to see and ears to hear, he does, according to the Scriptures, one of two things. He takes the stone and hardens it, or he takes the stone, removes it and replaces it with a heart of flesh. To wrestle with this, I'm going to ask Paul for help. Turn to Romans 9.

I warned you up front that we'd spend a lot of time in the Scriptures this morning. Romans 9, starting at verse 14. I'll ask you to take time to read chapters 9, 10 and 11 at home as your time allows. Speaking of God's sovereignty over the hardness of a person's heart, Paul writes, "What shall we say then? Is there injustice on God's part?

"By no means! For he says to Moses, 'I will have mercy on whom I have mercy, and I will have compassion on whom I have compassion.' So then it depends not on human will or exertion, but on God, who has mercy. For the Scripture says to Pharaoh, 'For this very purpose I have raised you up, that I might show my power in you, and that my name might be proclaimed in all the earth.' So then he has mercy on whomever he wills, and he hardens whomever he wills."

"You will say to me then, 'Why does he still find fault? For who can resist his will?' But who are you, O man, to answer back to God? Will what is molded say to its molder, 'Why have you made me like this?' Has the potter no right over the clay, to make out of the same lump one vessel for honorable use and another for dishonorable use?"

"What if God, desiring to show his wrath and to make known his power, has endured with much patience vessels of wrath prepared for destruction, in order to make known the riches of his glory for vessels of mercy, which he has prepared beforehand for glory—even us whom he has called, not from the Jews only but also from the Gentiles? As indeed he says in Hosea, 'Those who were not my people I will call "my people,"'" (he's talking about you) "'and her who was not beloved I will call "beloved."' 'And in the very place where it was said to them, "You are not my people," there they will be called "sons of the living God."'"

"And Isaiah cries out concerning Israel: 'Though the number of the sons of Israel be as the sand of the sea, only a remnant of them will be saved.'" That's the stump. "'For the Lord will carry out his sentence upon the earth fully and without delay.' And as Isaiah predicted, 'If the Lord of hosts had not left us offspring, we would have been like Sodom and become like Gomorrah.'" So we read here that leaving the stump, the holy seed, sparing anyone at all when none are worthy, is the very mercy of God.

We don't put him on trial over those who remain in their hardness. We worship him that anyone is spared, that he would not leave his remnant like Sodom and Gomorrah or wipe them off the face of the earth as in the great flood, but that with patient, enduring, steadfast love he would search the earth and apply the blood of Christ to a remnant he would give to the Son as a gift to magnify his glory. Continuing in Romans 9: "What shall we say, then? That Gentiles who did not pursue righteousness have attained it, that is, a righteousness that is by faith; but that Israel who pursued a law that would lead to righteousness did not succeed in reaching that law."

"Why? Because they did not pursue it by faith, but as if it were based on works. They have stumbled over the stumbling stone, as it is written, 'Behold, I am laying in Zion a stone of stumbling, and a rock of offense; and whoever believes in him will not be put to shame.'" Jesus is the stumbling stone. In the hardness of their hearts they staggered away from him in a stupor. But whoever believes in him will not be put to shame.

Skip ahead to chapter 10, verse 18; you'll read the whole chapter at home. "But I ask, have they not heard? Indeed they have, for 'Their voice has gone out to all the earth, and their words to the ends of the world.' But I ask, did Israel not understand?"

"First Moses says, 'I will make you jealous of those who are not a nation; with a foolish nation I will make you angry.' Then Isaiah is so bold as to say, 'I have been found by those who did not seek me; I have shown myself to those who did not ask for me.' But of Israel he says, 'All day long I have held out my hands to a disobedient and contrary people.'"

So you are the ones who are not a nation. You are the foolish nation. You are the ones who found him without seeking him, the ones he showed himself to when you didn't ask for him. You are the Gentiles.

You're the ones he came for after his chosen people rejected him. Let's bring this full circle. Jesus was rejected by his own people, and in that rejection he cast his care and his grace out to the peoples of the earth, gathering a remnant from every corner, every tribe, tongue and nation, including you. For what purpose? Let Paul finish his thought.

Chapter 11, verse 1: "I ask, then, has God rejected his people?" Meaning Israel. "By no means! For I myself am an Israelite, a descendant of Abraham, a member of the tribe of Benjamin."

"God has not rejected his people whom he foreknew. Do you not know what the Scripture says of Elijah, how he appeals to God against Israel? 'Lord, they have killed your prophets, they have demolished your altars, and I alone am left, and they seek my life.'"

"But what is God's reply to him? 'I have kept for myself seven thousand men who have not bowed the knee to Baal.' So too at the present time there is a remnant, chosen by grace. But if it is by grace, it is no longer on the basis of works; otherwise grace would no longer be grace."

"What then? Israel failed to obtain what it was seeking. The elect obtained it, but the rest were hardened, as it is written, 'God gave them a spirit of stupor, eyes that would not see and ears that would not hear, down to this very day.' And David says, 'Let their table become a snare and a trap, a stumbling block and a retribution for them;'"

"'let their eyes be darkened so that they cannot see, and bend their backs forever.' So I ask, did they stumble in order that they might fall?" Again, Christ is the stumbling stone for his people, and they stumbled. But did they stumble in order to fall?

"By no means! Rather, through their trespass salvation has come to the Gentiles, so as to make Israel jealous. Now if their trespass means riches for the world, and if their failure means riches for the Gentiles," for you, "how much more will their full inclusion mean! Now I am speaking to you Gentiles."

"Inasmuch then as I am an apostle to the Gentiles, I magnify my ministry in order somehow to make my fellow Jews jealous, and thus save some of them. For if their rejection means the reconciliation of the world, what will their acceptance mean but life from the dead? If the dough offered as firstfruits is holy, so is the whole lump, and if the root is holy," remember, the holy seed, "so are the branches. But if some of the branches were broken off," and they were, "and you, although a wild olive shoot," you were nothing. You were nobody. You were the foolish nation. You weren't seeking God.

"were grafted in among the others and now share in the nourishing root of the olive tree, do not be arrogant toward the branches. If you are, remember it is not you who support the root, but the root that supports you." You'll say, as Paul goes on, branches were broken off so that I might be grafted in. That's true. They were broken off because of their unbelief, but you stand fast through faith. I promise that's all I have to read today.

When we read these hard words from John, we're seeing a God who is utterly in control. Why did Jesus fail? Fail? If they had received him, he would have failed to carry out the prophecies of God. He didn't come to be praised by men.

He came to save the world. He came for you. He came for me. He came for the nations. He came for the remnant.

He came to gather a people to himself from every tribe, tongue and nation. Does that exclude Israel? By no means. Paul says it is through the salvation of the Gentiles that Israel will be made jealous and that some of them will be saved. To conclude our text in John 12, verses 42 and 43:

"Nevertheless, many even of the authorities believed in him, but for fear of the Pharisees they did not confess it, so that they would not be put out of the synagogue; for they loved the glory that comes from man more than the glory that comes from God." To tell you the truth, I'm not sure exactly how I want to preach this. Everyone I respect is divided on it. Are these people who had sincere saving faith and were cowards in expressing it? Or people who believed, but not with saving faith?

Satan believes that Jesus is who he says he is. So "believe" can mean a number of things. But let me give you what I do know. I said I was done reading, but I guess I'm not. Turn back to John 5 for a moment.

John 5, from verse 41. These are from Jesus' own mouth: "I do not receive glory from people. But I know that you do not have the love of God within you. I have come in my Father's name, and you do not receive me. If another comes in his own name, you will receive him."

"How can you believe, when you receive glory from one another and do not seek the glory that comes from the only God?" So when I hear about people who quietly believed what was going on but wouldn't confess it, and John tells us the reason is that they loved the glory that comes from man more than the glory that comes from God, I think we're talking about people who did not have saving faith. They're people of whom Jesus said, how can you believe, when you love the glory of man more than the glory that comes from God? That's exactly how John describes these men. But I don't know for sure.

Either they were believers stumbling between obedience and disobedience, or they were unregenerate. Neither matters much for the purpose of this sermon. It's the "nevertheless" in verse 42 that leads me to my big concluding point, because otherwise I think I'd be sending you home with a bag of bricks on your back. Here it is: when it seems like evil has won, when it seems like the hardness of human hearts has overpowered the grace and pursuit of God, when it seems like evil has won, it has only just begun. The Lord has only just begun.

He has turned the evil of man to good. He used the rejection of his own people. He used human hands to crucify the Son, to see him dead on a cross with your sin on his shoulders, so that he might ransom a remnant from every tribe, tongue and nation. As the early believers prayed in Acts 4, Herod and Pontius Pilate, along with the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel, were gathered together against him. Your salvation came through the evil, wicked hands of those who persecuted Jesus, his own people among them. So look at the people around you.

Who is too far gone? Jesus' own people handed him over to be crucified, and look what God did with them. I'm not only talking about a reckoning on the final day, when we'll see Israel redeemed. I'm talking about what happened within weeks. At Pentecost, Peter stood up in Jerusalem and preached Christ, crucified and risen, to the very crowds who had called for his death. And when they heard it, "they were cut to the heart, and said to Peter and the rest of the apostles, 'Brothers, what shall we do?'" And Peter said, "Repent and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins." That day about three thousand were added to the church, from among the people of Israel.

And think of Paul the apostle, a Jew of the tribe of Benjamin, who studied the law all his life and sincerely believed he was honoring God as he dragged men and women out of their homes and into prison and approved the killing of Christians. He met the risen Jesus on the road to Damascus, and the persecutor became the apostle to the Gentiles.

God kept their hearts hard so that they would carry out the plan: crucify him. It was the will of the Lord to crush him for our transgressions. But once he was crushed, we saw hearts of stone replaced with hearts of flesh. A great number of those very people were saved, and are still being saved, and will still be saved.

And likewise, it opened up the gospel to you. So besides go and read a lot of the Bible, I have one takeaway for you. Look at those who seem fixed in the hardness of their hearts today, and look at a God who hardens hearts and who in a moment can replace a heart of stone with a heart of flesh. That's Ezekiel 36. It's what he did for you. And it's the gospel, which is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes.

If you weren't too far gone, if Paul wasn't too far gone, if those who crucified Jesus weren't too far gone, then any heart of stone can be replaced with a heart of flesh if the Father wills it. And you've been given the charge to declare the gospel to the ends of the earth, so that we might see the remnant emerge, the holy seed sprout. Two weeks ago I preached on Jesus' words: unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit. This is the holy seed.

This is the stump of Israel. This is the remnant from among the Gentiles. You are the fruit; we've come full circle. All the hardness, all the evil, all of it, the Lord factored in. He worked it in.

He wrote it in. He's using it to accomplish his good purpose: to ransom a people for the Christ. So take heart. Let's pray.
