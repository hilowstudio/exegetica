# I Am the Good Shepherd (John 10:1-18)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 10:1-18

---

We've been working straight through John, and this morning we find ourselves in chapter 10, which continues the conversation we looked at last week. Last week Jesus healed a man born blind. At the end of that miracle he ended up in a back-and-forth with the Jews and the Pharisees. He presented himself to the man he had healed as the Son of Man and called him to faith, and the man believed. Then, in John 9:39, Jesus said, "For judgment I came into this world, that those who do not see may see, and those who see may become blind." And the Pharisees near him asked a question at the end of last week's passage that we'll pick up again this week.

"Some of the Pharisees near him heard these things, and said to him, 'Are we also blind?'" They picked up that he was talking about them. "Jesus said to them, 'If you were blind, you would have no guilt; but now that you say, "We see," your guilt remains.'" Our passage picks up as Jesus keeps teaching the same audience.

So I want us to understand that Jesus isn't preaching to the open air here. He's still talking to the people he has just told that they are not blind, but claim to see, and that in making that claim their guilt remains. I also want us to remember that these are the people who cast the blind man out of the synagogue because of his faith in Christ. Some of what Jesus says this morning is aimed squarely at these religious leaders who are rejecting him, and he has some things to say to them.

But beyond what he says to them, I want us listening for what he says about himself. The most important part of this message will be what Jesus tells us about himself. So let's jump in. We'll find that Jesus claims to be the good shepherd, and in making that claim he contrasts himself with five others.

He contrasts himself with the thief, the robber, the hired hand, the stranger and the gatekeeper. If you take notes, you might write that down. My hope is that as we look at Jesus as the good shepherd over against these five, we'll come to understand more intimately what it means to be Jesus' sheep, and that we'll be able to navigate some of the real challenges of living under the leadership of, and in relationship with, people who fit some of these other descriptions. Let's go verse by verse.

Chapter 10, verses 1 to 6: "Truly, truly, I say to you, he who does not enter the sheepfold by the door but climbs in by another way, that man is a thief and a robber. But he who enters by the door is the shepherd of the sheep. To him the gatekeeper opens. The sheep hear his voice, and he calls his own sheep by name and leads them out. When he has brought out all his own, he goes before them, and the sheep follow him, for they know his voice. A stranger they will not follow, but they will flee from him, for they do not know the voice of strangers." "This figure of speech Jesus used with them, but they did not understand what he was saying to them."

In verses 1 to 5, Jesus introduces the idea of a sheepfold. His hearers needed no explanation, but I don't spend any time with sheep, and most likely neither do you. So I want to take a little time to set up the picture he's using.

In the ancient Near East, many different shepherds each had responsibility for their own flocks. They would lead them to shared pastures where the flocks mingled, all grazing in the same fields.

In the evening, when it was time to lead them away from pasture to a place of rest, the shepherds would use what's called a sheepfold. They built big enclosures out of stones and thorny bushes and led all the flocks into them. There was just one narrow gate in and out. And there was a gatekeeper, an under-shepherd, who kept the gate the sheep moved in and out through.

So picture a large enclosure called a sheepfold, holding many different flocks under the care of many different shepherds, and a gatekeeper who stands at the door. He lets into the sheepfold only the shepherds who own and have authority over those flocks. With that set up, hear it again: "He who does not enter the sheepfold by the door but climbs in by another way, that man is a thief and a robber."

This really happened. If you're digging through the stone wall, or pulling away the thorns, or climbing over the wall to steal sheep, to take what isn't yours and profit from what doesn't belong to you, you are a thief and a robber.

If you aren't coming in through the door, you don't belong in the sheepfold, and the sheep don't belong to you. It's a very direct statement. Remember whom he's talking to. He's talking to a religious elite who believed they had authority over the people of God.

Last week they put themselves on the judgment seat and tried to decide for themselves whether this Jesus could be trusted. And Jesus is telling them, you've put yourselves where you don't belong. You're among the sheep, but you got there by climbing over the wall. You haven't come in through the door, because I am the door. We'll get there.

But in contrast to these thieves and robbers who climb over the wall, verse 2, "he who enters by the door is the shepherd of the sheep." Verse 3: "To him the gatekeeper opens." The gatekeeper looked for the shepherds of the sheep.

You had to own sheep to come into the sheepfold, and the gatekeeper was there to see to that. He would let you in, and the sheep would hear the shepherd's voice, and he would call his own sheep by name and lead them out. Remember, inside the sheepfold many different flocks were mingled together. Then the shepherd would come in. In the ancient world shepherds had their own calls, each with a unique sound.

Whatever sound or tool they used, their sheep's ears were trained to their call. Suddenly what looked like one big flock would separate into individual flocks, and only the sheep that belonged to that shepherd, that knew his voice, would come to him. He would call them by name and lead them out of the sheepfold one by one, leaving the other sheep behind, because they weren't his. It says that when he has brought out all his own, he goes before them, walking ahead of the sheep, and the sheep follow him, for they know his voice.

"A stranger they will not follow, but they will flee from him, for they do not know the voice of strangers." And they did not understand this figure of speech Jesus used with them. How could they? He's telling them that only his sheep hear his voice, and as he speaks to them, they're saying, I don't know you.

I don't know what you're talking about. I don't know what you mean. Within the very parable he's telling them, Jesus is proving what the parable says: my sheep hear my voice, and those who are not my sheep do not. "A stranger they will not follow, but they will flee from him."

So far in this passage Jesus has introduced the thief, the gatekeeper and the stranger, set against the shepherd. As I prepared this message, I was learning to go to the Lord as I read and ask him: what here is food?

What here is food? Because these are the words of the shepherd, and Jesus told Peter, if you love me, "Feed my sheep."

So I asked him, what is food here? And as I talked with him about it, I worked through what it actually feels like, as a sheep, to be under the authority or care of, or in relationship with, people who wear these other titles but aren't shepherds. In some form or fashion, whether in your own family, at work or at the level of government, all of you have at some point been under the leadership of people who are not shepherds in the way Jesus describes here. Instead they are thieves, hired hands, strangers or gatekeepers. Here's the thing.

Here's the difference between the shepherd and the stranger. Suppose a stranger got past the gatekeeper, wore a shepherd's hat, looked like a shepherd and got in. Even inside the sheepfold, what he lacks is the ability to call the sheep. He doesn't know the call. They don't know his voice. He's a stranger to them, and they're strangers to him. This convicted me.

We're going to talk this morning about the role of an under-shepherd, and as one of your pastors, I'm an under-shepherd. Jesus speaks to that role this morning. But I know you can't be a shepherd, or even an under-shepherd, if the sheep are strangers to you and you are a stranger to them. What do I have to say to the sheep if I don't even know their names?

And how can they hear from me if I'm a stranger, if I have no relationship with the people I'm talking to? Jesus has been impressing on me, even as a preacher, that there's a world of difference between preaching to the flock entrusted to your care as an under-shepherd and preaching to the open air, street preaching.

We're talking about speaking to people we're meant to know intimately. If I don't know you, if we're not on a first-name basis yet, I'm truly hindered in what I'm called to do: to know the sheep, not to be a stranger to them, and for the sheep to know me as one of their under-shepherds. It's the same for all of us. When you extend God's care to one another, you'll only be able to do it insofar as you really know one another.

The sheep can't respond to the stranger, and they don't. The stranger can't call the sheep, because they flee from his voice. And the thief seeks only to harm them.

I've been spending a lot of time with a good friend who has walked with the Lord for forty years. He has been very kind to me as a young shepherd, walking with me as a shepherd of shepherds and helping me understand what it means to walk with Jesus while shepherding a church. Over lunch a couple of months ago he told me there's a key difference between a shepherd and a rancher. That stuck with me, and now he teaches it to everybody, but I heard it first.

He said a shepherd and a rancher look exactly the same up until the rancher leads the calf to slaughter. And ranchers don't name their calves, he says. You spend time with them. You lead them to pasture. You get them good and fat.

You protect them from predators. You do all of that. But the whole point for the rancher is to get a good price for them at market, that they'll be good and fat on the day he leads them to slaughter. That's both the thief and the hired hand. There are people in your life who claim authority over you, who claim a relationship with you, who want to lead you, but their inner drive isn't to see you delighting in the chief Shepherd. It's that they and their enterprise can get a good price for you in the end.

Let's bring this home. Even in marriages, both spouses can be ranchers for each other. You make good deposits in each other's lives so that you can eventually lay claim to the other person and have them owe you.

It's a scorekeeping arrangement where in the end I can say to my wife, look how I've loved you; I think you owe me this. Look how I've served you; don't you owe me service in return? All my work to make the sheep beautiful is ultimately so that the beauty comes back to me, so that I get something out of it.

That's entirely different from a shepherd. That's ranching. We see it in relationships all the time. We see it at work too.

Your boss would love to lead you, get the best out of you and train you so that your skills are useful to him and his department. Even the good things, the things you're grateful for, look so similar to shepherding, right up until the slaughter.

And sometimes the slaughter looks like a promotion. It doesn't always look like getting cut because the economy turned: thanks, but no thanks, you're out of here. Sometimes it looks good.

We're going to promote you so you can take more responsibility off my plate. A friend told me that when he was first planting a church, he showed up early every Sunday to set up chairs, and stayed late to break them down. But he wasn't doing it because he had a humble heart to serve the church he got to help plant. There were people he wanted serving this way who weren't, and he hoped that if they saw the pastor doing it, they'd think, if he can do it, I can do it.

Leading by example, they'd start setting up and breaking down the chairs. Everywhere else people will tell you that's good leadership: lead by example; do what you want others to see and copy. But Jesus cares tremendously about a man's motives.

And my friend told me, what I really wanted was to look humble in serving the flock, so they would do what I wanted them to do and I wouldn't have to. I was serving me by looking like I was serving them. It takes a lot of introspection to weigh the motives behind what you do. But here's the thing: Jesus sees it at every point.

He knows us and the reasons we do what we do. Am I trying to get a good price for you at market? Am I trying to get a good return on my investment in our relationship? Or do I simply love you and know you by name? Would I lay down my life for you? That's what Jesus says the good shepherd does.

Let's watch him keep talking about these figures. Because they don't understand him, Jesus speaks again in verse 7. This is a different figure; don't read it as a continuation of what he just said. He goes a different direction with the same imagery. Verses 7 to 10: "So Jesus again said to them, 'Truly, truly, I say to you, I am the door of the sheep.'" A minute ago he was the shepherd. "'All who came before me are thieves and robbers, but the sheep did not listen to them. I am the door. If anyone enters by me, he will be saved and will go in and out and find pasture. The thief comes only to steal and kill and destroy. I came that they may have life and have it abundantly.'"

So in verses 7 to 10 he claims to be the door itself. He's gone from shepherd to door, moving seamlessly back and forth within his picture. We've already talked about the sheepfold and its single way in.

In the big shared sheepfold out in the wild, where everyone brought their flocks in from pasture to safety, there was a gatekeeper, and Jesus has talked about him. But when you had a private sheepfold, on your own land, things were different.

The shepherd often slept with the sheep and was himself the door. He left an opening and made his bed for the night in the entrance of his own sheepfold, so anything that wanted to get in had to go through him. Jesus is likening himself to this kind of shepherd, the one who is himself the door. To enter the sheepfold, you must go through him.

And to get out to pasture, to find your food and water and your delight, you have to go through him. He himself is the door. And in verse 9 he says he isn't only the door; anyone who enters through him will be saved and will go in and out and find pasture. Going back to the first picture, anyone who goes in and out by any other way is a thief and a robber, out only to steal and kill and destroy.

The world, and honestly not only the world, but you, your own mind, and me and my mouth that just says things, will tell us all kinds of things about the way to life, abundant life. We don't want to have to go through the one way. Jesus says the gate is narrow. Jesus says he alone is the way, the truth and the life, and that only through him can we come to the Father. He makes this exclusive claim to be the door himself.

And we want what's on the other side of the door. We want to be saved from the things that want to devour us. We want to be safe. We want shelter. We want care and abundance, food and water and nourishment.

We want to drink to our heart's delight. We want health. We want prosperity. We want all of it.

We just don't want to go through the door to get it. Not really. We'll accept almost any other way, and we'll start promising ourselves, and others will promise us, all kinds of other ways to get these things. And Jesus says no. All of those ways lead only to death.

They're stealing and killing and destroying. But he says he came that we may have life and have it abundantly. Then in verse 11 he moves back to the shepherd picture. Verses 11 to 15: "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep. He who is a hired hand and not a shepherd, who does not own the sheep, sees the wolf coming and leaves the sheep and flees, and the wolf snatches them and scatters them. He flees because he is a hired hand and cares nothing for the sheep. I am the good shepherd. I know my own and my own know me, just as the Father knows me and I know the Father; and I lay down my life for the sheep."

The hired hand, Jesus says, cares nothing for the sheep. The hired hand cares nothing for the sheep.

This is the rancher picture again. I've been spending some time at a horse stable lately, and it has brought this picture to life for me.

There are about twenty horses in those stables. Each day hired hands come in and turn the horses out to pasture to eat. They make sure the hay is stocked. Then they go into the stalls behind them and make sure the gates are sturdy and the barn is in good repair. They clean out the stalls, top off the food and water, and give the horses their medicine. And that's great. The hired hand is doing good work. But I know he won't die for the horses. He's there for a wage, and if they stopped paying him, he wouldn't show up.

That doesn't mean a hired hand has no role to play in the economy of God. What I'm saying is that Jesus claims there is only one who would die for the sheep, and that there's a key difference between the one who would die for the sheep and the one who is there to earn a wage off them, however good his work. As I've gotten to know the owner of this stable, I've learned that she owns a few of the horses, and the rest are entrusted to her care; people pay her to board them. What's interesting about her, as opposed to the hired hands, is that when she looks out at the horses, in the stable or in the field, she notices things about them I will probably never notice. If one is walking just a little differently, she notes it.

And having noted it, though it may be nothing by itself, she'll compare it with how much that horse ate last night. She'll notice if the amount of poop in the stall is different from normal. She doesn't look away from the dirty parts of the job and enjoy only the fun parts. The care of the horses is what drives her, and she notices what the hired hands would not. That's our good shepherd. He knows the sheep by name.

He calls them by name. He knows them. He's not a stranger to them. He says, I know them, and he knows you. If you are his sheep, he knows you, and he notices when something is wrong.

When you depart from what gives you life, abundant life, and you get sick because you're looking for what you need in things that can't give it, he sees it, he notices, and he pursues you. In another passage he says the good shepherd will leave the ninety-nine to go after the one. Not only when danger comes your way, but when you leave the flock and wander off and put yourself in danger, he will go and get you. In Ezekiel 34 the Lord uses this same imagery to rebuke the shepherds of Israel, its leaders.

He tells them: you eat the fat, you clothe yourselves with the wool, you slaughter the fat ones, but you don't feed the sheep. The injured you haven't bound up. The strayed you haven't brought back. So, he says, I will set up over them one shepherd, my servant David, and he will bind up the hurting and seek the ones who wander off. He won't look only at the fattest and most beautiful sheep and how he can profit from them. This is not a new theme at all, and these are not new words for these people to hear.

Jesus says, I am the good shepherd. The hired hand cares nothing for the sheep, but I know my own and my own know me, just as the Father knows me and I know the Father, and I lay down my life for the sheep. Verse 16: "And I have other sheep that are not of this fold. I must bring them also, and they will listen to my voice. So there will be one flock, one shepherd." Staying with the sheepfold picture, Jesus is saying, I have sheep in here, and he means his original people, the Jews, the Israelites.

I have people here, and I have people who are not of this fold. Here he's talking about you. He's talking about the Gentiles. I have others outside this fold. I must go and get them, he says, and they will listen to my voice.

Notice the order, because he does this so often in this book, and we want to let him build his case for the truth he's claiming. He says, in the present tense, I have other sheep that are not of this fold. I must bring them. So he is going to go and get them, which means they haven't yet seen him. And when he brings them, they will listen to his voice.

So somehow they're his before he calls them. He knows who his sheep are, and when he goes to get them, they respond to his voice because they're his. And again, remember his audience: he's speaking to people who cannot hear his voice. And this is where I think there's room for us to rest a little as sheep.

For him to be the good shepherd, we have to admit that makes us sheep. He keeps calling us sheep. But when he speaks, you hear his voice. When he goes, you follow him. And you can tell when other voices are trying to lead you astray. You can tell, this isn't the voice of my shepherd.

This is something different. This feels like chains. This feels like a snare. I've been here before, before my shepherd called me out of it. I know what happens when I go there.

That was all him. And one of our roles as under-shepherds of this church, and you need to hold me and your other shepherds to it, is that we are gatekeepers. We are under-shepherds. We're not being cute when we say Jesus is the lead shepherd of this church. We mean it.

My goal as an under-shepherd, essentially a gatekeeper, is this: when someone comes to the door wanting to spend time with the sheep and come in, the first question is, are you the shepherd? We want to stand between the flock and those who would come in and start selling them things that are the opposite of their shepherd's message. We're under-shepherds, and our role is simply to point to him. Because he says the good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep.

And as shepherds, and even just as Christians, we will die for one another in a sense. Jesus calls us to die to ourselves, to the flesh, to our preferences, and to live to Christ for one another. So in serving one another sacrificially, in that sense, we die for one another. But I'm not going to literally die for you.

And even if I did, even if I were like a soldier who will literally die for you, it wouldn't be for your sin. It wouldn't make eternal payment for you. A soldier might die in a way that gives you one more day of protection from someone who wants to harm you. There are very brave men and women who will love very sacrificially. But what they won't do, what they can't do, is what the good shepherd does: he says that those who enter through him will be saved and will go in and out and find pasture, because he lays down his life for them.

There's a picture here I need us to see. If we don't really see the danger we were in when Jesus laid down his life for us, we'll mistake what he was doing. I think some of us imagine a Jesus who cuts himself open and says, look how much I love you, in some meaningless, sentimental way, as if he threw himself off a cliff shouting, look how much I love you. Some almost manipulative display: man, he would die for me. Thanks, man.

Some theoretical display of his love. But he talks about the shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep on the day the wolf comes. There was a real threat, a real darkness, a real and present danger to the sheep: sin, and our enemy Satan, who prowls around like a roaring lion. And Jesus stepped in for you and let it kill him so that it would spare you. You were in actual, real and present danger because of your actual sin, the sins of the world, and the sin of Adam in the garden that you inherited as a human being who didn't perfectly reflect the image of God, as you were created to. You stood to answer for that, and the answer was going to be death, eternal death, separation from God.

You can't pay for your sin. You can't. If you face that real and present danger, it means destruction. That's why Jesus stood between you and the wolf and laid down his life for the sheep. And as for the sheep not of this fold, he went and got us, amen? From a little village called Nazareth all the way to us.

I mean, come on. How far will he go to get his sheep? It's an unspeakable miracle that his salvation has reached your ears. Verses 17 and 18: "For this reason the Father loves me, because I lay down my life that I may take it up again. No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord. I have authority to lay it down, and I have authority to take it up again. This charge I have received from my Father."

Jesus didn't only die for you. When he died for you, he took the real and present danger that stood against you with him into death. But when he rose, he didn't bring it back up with him.

He died, took hold of your sin and your death and brought them into the grave with him, and then he came up alone, victorious over sin and death for you. Only he has the authority to do that. So the people in your life who claim to love you so sacrificially: look at all I do for you, look at all I sacrifice for you, look at how I die for you. Well, all right.

Can you rise again? Can you come back to life? Because your death isn't all that useful to me. But life, abundant life, that's what we're after, isn't it? And I know that for most of you this morning, I'm not speaking in theory. You can see it right now.

You have real people in your life you look to for care, provision, shepherding, love, admiration, security, meaning and significance. And some of them have been thieves, just trying to get something out of you, something for themselves and their own comfort. Some have been hired hands who look like shepherds right up to the point of slaughter. To get really honest: for the last three Father's Days in a row, my older kids, the ones old enough to think for themselves, have written in their cards to me that they see me as their provider. Dad, we see how hard you work and how you provide for us, and we're so grateful.

Happy Father's Day. I wouldn't mind that in one card, even two. But three cards in a row, and they've learned to connect their father's love with how he provides for them and how hard he works. So this last year I wanted to read in a Father's Day card not just, look how hard my dad works for us, but, Dad, look how you play with us, how you see us, how you care for us, how you listen to us.

No shame on my kids, and no shame on me; thank you, Jesus. But I went to my Father and asked, in what ways am I a hired hand in my own home, doing all the things a shepherd does, but really trying to get results out of my home and my kids, without actually entering in, knowing them by name, loving them and being willing to lay down my life for them? And if I'll do that to them, I'll certainly do it to you.

And so will you. I could stand up here and preach against everyone outside you who takes on these identities and harms you. But I'm preaching to you too: maybe the thief and the hired hand and the stranger is you. Maybe you've done the most harm to yourself and to those entrusted to your care. So where do you go if it's you?

To the good shepherd. Where do you go if it's you? To the good shepherd. Because he took on himself all your sin and all your failure. Everything he called you to, he fulfilled perfectly in his life. And all you failed to do and all you did wrong, he took into the grave with him when he died.

And when he rose to new life, he offered that new life to you freely, so that when you stand before him, you won't hear the record of your deeds. You'll hear the record of his deeds on your behalf. For this reason the Father loves him. And for this reason the Father loves you, if you are found in him: when the Father looks at you, he sees the perfect life, death and resurrection of Jesus Christ.

When Jesus says this, the people are divided. Many say, he has a demon; why listen to him? Others say, can a demon open the eyes of the blind? Maybe we should listen. And they keep debating, because they think they have to decide who he is. But Jesus is who he is without their agreement and without their help.

In the end it comes down to this: are you in the fold or not? So here's my takeaway. Your under-shepherds, your leaders at every level, the leaders in your home, the leader you were meant to be, will look like all kinds of things. That's why Jesus took care to describe them all: the thief and the hired hand, the stranger and the gatekeeper, even the door itself. They all show us a picture of who we are, who he is, and where real life is found.

So I invite you once again to come and go through the door that is Jesus. Don't go through me. Don't go through me. Some of you sit across the table from me and say, if I could just talk to you about this, I'd be okay. And I say, no.

You need to talk to Jesus about this. I'm the under-shepherd. I'm just going to point you to him. Your okayness, your abundant life, doesn't go through me. It doesn't go through your spouse.

It doesn't go through your boss. It doesn't go through the president. It goes through Jesus Christ, the good shepherd, the only one who laid down his life for you. He's the only one. And if others have laid down their lives for you, they haven't taken them up again and brought you with them.

So turn to him, and stop turning to the places that only threaten to steal and kill and destroy. And as we do, hear the charge to be for one another what you need. You can't be the good shepherd. Only he can.

But people who know the voice of the shepherd can tell when they're hearing a voice that isn't his. So if you're not around people who also know your shepherd, then when you're the one sheep that starts to wander from the ninety-nine, who will grab you and point you back to him? That's the role we play for one another.

That's even my role up here: to tune you to the voice of your shepherd. I don't have much to say except to read what he has to say. He's who you need. He's who you need. When you fail to show up for one another, that failure is paid for.

No guilt, no shame, no condemnation. But your shepherd has tuned your ears to know his voice, and there's someone who is deaf to him right now. You could be the one to say to that person, just come; let's go see Jesus together. Let's pray.
