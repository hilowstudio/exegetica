# Your Sorrow Will Turn Into Joy (John 16:16-33)

**Series:** Gospel of John — Adam Vega Sermon Library
**Passage:** John 16:16-33

---

This morning I want to hold out three central points. I'll give them to you up front, and then we'll work through them. First, we'll hear from Jesus how and why he turns suffering, sorrow and pain into joy. How and why Jesus turns your suffering, sorrow and pain into joy. Second, we'll explore the fact that the Father loves you, and so you should talk to him.

And last, we'll talk about how your weakness in the faith and the circumstances of your life can do nothing to change either point one or point two. Remember how this chapter has been building. As we saw last week, the disciples are starting to come to terms with the idea that Jesus is leaving. He has talked to them about sending the Helper, the Holy Spirit.

The Spirit will bear witness about him and bring to mind the things of him. And they'd be thinking, why do you need to send someone to bear witness about you? Because, he says, I'm leaving and going to the Father. So sorrow is beginning to settle in their hearts.

Jesus continues the theme as they start to grasp it: you're leaving. He says it again in verse 16: "A little while, and you will see me no longer." If I told you, you will see me no longer, what you'd hear is "no longer." You're leaving.

I'm not going to see you again. Not for a long time. No longer. Probably not again in my lifetime: that's what you'd understand. But then Jesus comes in hot: "and again a little while, and you will see me."

So the disciples are rightly confused, and they start talking. What is he talking about? What is he saying to us? "Because I am going to the Father"? What does he mean by "a little while"?

We had just started to come to terms with "no longer," with his leaving, his going to the Father, our not seeing him. And now he says, "and again a little while, and you will see me." What does this mean? A little while? In their confusion they're talking among themselves, and Jesus, who loves them, knows what is stirring in their hearts.

So he brings it up. Verses 19 to 24: "Jesus knew that they wanted to ask him, so he said to them, 'Is this what you are asking yourselves, what I meant by saying, "A little while and you will not see me, and again a little while and you will see me"? Truly, truly, I say to you, you will weep and lament, but the world will rejoice. You will be sorrowful, but your sorrow will turn into joy.'"

"'When a woman is giving birth, she has sorrow because her hour has come, but when she has delivered the baby, she no longer remembers the anguish, for joy that a human being has been born into the world. So also you have sorrow now, but I will see you again, and your hearts will rejoice, and no one will take your joy from you. In that day you will ask nothing of me.'" So Jesus is telling them, I'm leaving, but I'm coming back. And he doesn't give them much clarity on what he means by "a little while," even though that's exactly what's stirring in their hearts: what does he mean by a little while?

Before I give you a real sense of what he's saying, I want to name a challenge I think we all feel when we think about God's involvement in our pain and suffering: turning it into joy. That's distinctly different, I think, from replacing suffering. Pay close attention to the picture Jesus uses here.

Forgive the crude picture, but a lot of the time we treat the blood of Jesus Christ like a painkiller, applied to the pain and the sorrow to change the way we feel them. It numbs us to them, or helps us escape them. We imagine Jesus taking our pain and suffering, removing it from us and replacing it with something else, or else numbing us to it. But he's talking about something entirely different.

He's saying that he will take the pain, the suffering and the sorrow and turn them into joy. It isn't about escaping it. It isn't about being numbed to it. It isn't about replacing it. It's actually through it that the joy is produced. That's radically different from how we usually want to approach pain and suffering.

I think we do this with Jesus first because it's how we deal with pain and suffering in every other area of life: we try to flee it, treat it, escape it, replace it. But then he uses this picture of a woman having a baby. When a woman is in labor, screaming in agony, nobody hears the screaming and runs into the room with a baby to hand her. It's through the pain of labor that the joyful baby is brought forth.

Nobody treats labor pain by introducing a baby into the equation. A stork doesn't fly in at the last hour and replace the pain with a baby. The baby is produced through the pain. That's how Jesus talks about his departure. That's how Jesus talks about his work.

But we often see God in one of two ways. I've brought in some illustrations of my own this morning, as if Jesus hadn't given us enough. As you listen, think about which one is more like you. Sometimes we see God as the maker of Chutes and Ladders.

Other times we see him as the maker and master of chess. I'll start with Chutes and Ladders. Picture God as the creator of all things who has made a board game. You've made Chutes and Ladders. You were there at the beginning. You invented the whole thing. You set the bounds and the rules.

You made all of it. You made the end point. Hopefully you know the game; I don't know if it's played much anymore. When you land on a ladder, it propels you forward on the board.

Sometimes you land on a chute, and it sends you backward. But if you play long enough, everybody ends up at the same destination. In some ways a lot of us think of God like this: he created the world like a game of Chutes and Ladders. He was intimately interested in the beginning, setting the parameters, the bounds and the rules.

And he's very interested in the end: play long enough, and everyone gets funneled to the end and ends up where he wants them. But he's distant and removed from the gameplay. He has no say in, or interest in, the roll of the dice or how many ladders or chutes you land on, as long as you end up where he wants. So we make God removed: interested only in the beginning and the end, but uninterested in the process, uninterested in your life, uninterested in the ladder moments and the chute moments. If you think about God this way, he feels most distant and removed in your moments of suffering.

And you treat your loneliness before God in your suffering by speaking slogans to yourself: well, he's sovereign over the end. He knows what he's doing, and eventually this works out for good. He's always a God of "eventually" for you, never a God who is present, until you land on a ladder, and then it's glory to God. Others of us don't see God as this absent, removed God interested only in the beginning and the end. We see him instead as the maker of chess, a master of chess, a highly reactive God.

Rather than being absent, he's frantically scrambling all the time to answer with the perfect move to what he didn't see coming. Chess is all strategy. There are a million possible moves. And God, the maker and perfect player of chess, comes up against an opponent and is always recalculating.

He has to re-strategize all the way through, always responding to the new variables thrown his way. If God were like that, he'd still be worth all our worship, because that would make him an awesome God. But it isn't the biblical God. He isn't frantically reacting as if we were the main authors and he were constantly steering things back on course. When we think that way, to stay with the picture for a minute, I think we imagine the story of the earth something like this: God made the earth, and then Satan said, well, I'm going to make the sun, and it's going to burn the earth.

And then God says, oh yeah? I'm going to make the ozone layer, and it'll block that ultraviolet light, and the light of the sun you meant to burn the earth I'll convert into chemical energy so plants can grow. And then Satan says, well, I'll poke holes in the ozone layer so people get sunburned. And then God says, I'll make one of those plants an aloe plant.

Just back and forth, with God like a master chess player up against Satan, and up against you. He always has a move for your move, but he never really knows what you'll do next, so he's constantly having to change his plan. But here's what the Bible tells us about God.

Let me read you some passages. Genesis 50:20: "As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring it about that many people should be kept alive, as they are today."

Isaiah 45:7, God speaking: "I form light and create darkness; I make well-being and create calamity; I am the LORD, who does all these things." Lamentations 3:38: "Is it not from the mouth of the Most High that good and bad come?"

Philippians 2:13: "for it is God who works in you, both to will and to work for his good pleasure." In Habakkuk 1 and 2, to summarize rather than read both chapters, a prophet brings a word to the people of Judah, God's people: God is going to raise up a wicked people, the Chaldeans, the Babylonians, to bring judgment on them. And a charge is brought against God: how can you use a nation more wicked than Judah to judge Judah? And God's answer in chapter 2 is essentially, because I'm going to judge them too.

Psalm 115:3: "Our God is in the heavens; he does all that he pleases." He's not just a player in the game, or the one who made the game, spun it like a top and stood back to watch. He is the author of the story from start to finish, intimately interested in the details, for you and for me, both in judging the wicked and in bringing his people home. And all of this points to the one from Romans 8:28: "And we know that for those who love God all things work together for good, for those who are called according to his purpose."

So, back to our passage, as we build this theology and get a sense of the might of God. Our Jesus isn't describing himself as an absent game maker or a frantic game player. He likens himself to a mother giving birth. He's intimately interested in the details of your life, so that he can use the suffering of your life to bring about glory and joy. He has to be more powerful than you think he is to do what he claims to do here. He's never playing catch-up. He's never caught off guard.

He's never absent, and he's never removed or uninterested. He's intimately set on using suffering and sadness, sorrow and pain, to bring about glory and joy, like a woman who brings forth a baby through suffering. What a beautiful picture. What if, the next time you suffer, you thought of it this way? What about the suffering in your heart right now, as I start speaking to it? The afflictions you battle: sickness, mental illness, addiction, relational strife, poverty, whatever it is.

Call to mind the suffering and anguish in your life. And here Jesus describes his own departure, the greatest anguish and sorrow in the history of the world, the Son of God handing himself over to be killed. Killed. And he says even this is like a woman giving birth.

And great joy comes on the other side of it. If he can use that... I mean, the greatest picture of this is the cross. I know what I'm saying is hard to believe. I'm not up here saying it with the full strength of faith, as if I believe it completely. And thankfully, as we work through the passage, Jesus will say, I've got that covered too.

The cross was the instrument of death used to crucify the Son. If there's no higher example of our God taking suffering, pain and anguish to accomplish his good and glorious purposes, think of this: the cross is worn on necklaces now, and for us, death means entrance into eternal life. We can count it all joy when we meet trials of various kinds, because we can trust our God, who used the cross to bring about good. That brings us to point number two.

Why would God do this? Because he loves you. In verse 23 he says, "In that day," the day that's coming when you behold the baby on the other side of the labor pains, when you see him again, "you will ask nothing of me." You're asking Jesus for things these days, I hope. And he says a day is coming when you'll behold him again and ask nothing of him.

However good he is as we behold him now by the Holy Spirit, it's going to be that much better. And he goes on, verses 23 and 24: "Truly, truly, I say to you, whatever you ask of the Father in my name, he will give it to you. Until now you have asked nothing in my name. Ask, and you will receive, that your joy may be full." So he's building an invitation into prayer, bold prayer. Verses 25 to 27: "I have said these things to you in figures of speech. The hour is coming when I will no longer speak to you in figures of speech but will tell you plainly about the Father. In that day you will ask in my name, and I do not say to you that I will ask the Father on your behalf; for the Father himself loves you, because you have loved me and have believed that I came from God."

He's talking about a day that will come for these men, who haven't yet asked for anything in his name, because he's right there, so they just ask him. He's leaving, and now they'll start asking for things in his name, the same way you do when you pray in Jesus' name by the power of the Holy Spirit. He's pointing back to what we saw last week, in 16:12, where Jesus said, "I still have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now. When the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth, for he will not speak on his own authority, but whatever he hears he will speak, and he will declare to you the things that are to come. He will glorify me, for he will take what is mine and declare it to you."

So Jesus is saying, I'm going to leave. The Holy Spirit will come. He'll come on you, and you'll start asking for things in my name, and it will be done for you. And why will you do that?

Because the Spirit who is on you is the Spirit who bears witness with your spirit that you've received adoption as sons. Now you can cry, "Abba! Father!" This Spirit bears witness with your spirit that you are in fact a child of God. Why do you come to the throne of God the Father with confidence to ask for things in Jesus' name? Because the Holy Spirit is bearing witness to you of the things of Christ, and the things of Christ are, above all, this: you are now his son.

You belong to him. Can I read this to you again? I never want to suggest that Jesus isn't your Mediator or that Jesus isn't your High Priest. I'll give you all the cross-references if you need them. Jesus is your Mediator, and Jesus is your High Priest.

He stands in for you before the judgment seat of God. But here Jesus wants to limit how far you take that picture. When you talk to the Father, let me use his own words, because this is a bold thing to say: "I do not say to you that I will ask the Father on your behalf; for the Father himself loves you, because you have loved me and have believed that I came from God."

Some of you aren't praying today, or you're praying without confidence, because you picture yourself coming into the throne room of God, standing before him, and Jesus standing between you and God like a heat shield. As if the Father invited you into the house but said, don't look at me. Saved you from hell but said, don't talk to me. As if his general disposition were against you, his face turned away from you. When I was a kid, I was an instigator and a troublemaker.

I say "was," but you know what I mean. I was one of six. We were always trying to read our parents, and I usually wasn't in good standing with mine. So if we wanted something, we'd pick a tribute to go ask.

We'd say, I'm not going to ask. You ask. She'll probably say yes to you. Every parent has favorites; they just don't tell you who. It wasn't me.

And Jesus says, don't do that with me. Don't pray as if you tell me and then I go and tell him: hey, can you go ask Papa? The Father loves you.

Go directly to him. When Jesus says he is your Mediator, he means he's the Mediator who has given you unrestricted access to the Father, not a mediator you go to and say, can you ask the Father for me? He bought you direct access to the Father with his blood. That's life-changing, if we believe it. It's prayer-changing, Jesus says, if you believe it.

Verses 28 to 30: "I came from the Father and have come into the world, and now I am leaving the world and going to the Father." His disciples said, a little too confidently, "Ah, now you are speaking plainly and not using figurative speech! Now we know that you know all things and do not need anyone to question you; this is why we believe that you came from God." Yeah, right.

Verses 31 and 32: "Jesus answered them, 'Do you now believe? Behold, the hour is coming, indeed it has come, when you will be scattered, each to his own home, and will leave me alone.'" In five minutes, he's saying, I'll be alone, and you'll be scattered to your homes. He answers them so truthfully and honestly: you don't believe.

I know you don't believe, because when they seize me, you don't know what's coming, not really, not yet. You're going to flee. You're going to abandon me in my hour of greatest need. You're going to leave me alone. "Yet I am not alone, for the Father is with me."

And the last verse, verse 33: "I have said these things to you, that in me you may have peace. In the world you will have tribulation. But take heart; I have overcome the world." So the flow from point one to point two to point three is this: Jesus is going to take your suffering, your sorrow and your pain and turn them into joy. Why? Because by faith in him, through grace, the Father loves you.

The Father loves you. But Jesus doesn't hold back on the reality of their situation. That matters, because you and I are a lot like them. Let me read back to you what this conversation is like from their side. We don't understand.

What is he talking about? Ah, now we know! Jesus: do you? You'll be scattered, and you'll leave me alone. You'll desert me.

The world will rejoice at your sorrow. He tells them, you'll have sorrow, but the world will rejoice as they hoist me up on a cross. You will have tribulation. He doesn't pull any punches in describing the situation. He doesn't cover it in slogans: it's okay, because you won't have tribulation, you'll have certainty, you won't have any doubt, you'll know exactly what I mean.

You'll be faithful and never scatter. You'll never leave me. He says none of that as the reason for confidence or the reason to take heart. In fact he says, you will do all of those things; you're doing them now, and I want you to take heart, because I have overcome the world. Because of what he has done, not because of what we've done.

That's why we pray with boldness. You can run into the throne room of grace. You can stand before the throne of God the Father today with confidence, because your key to that room had nothing to do with you. It was him, always him. And he is using the present suffering in your life, your actual, real, present suffering, and all the suffering on the face of the earth from beginning to end, including a Roman cross for the Son of God, to bring about your humble repentance and your worship of the King.

Repentance and worship of the King. The fact that you can say, not me, but him: that was born through the pains of childbirth. Rejoice in anything the Father brings into your life that brings you to the foot of the cross to call on him. Take heart. You will have trouble, but he has overcome the world.

This is our banner. This is what we preach week by week. This is what we believe. What Jesus wanted to change for his disciples here was where their confidence came from and what moved them to pray. It isn't the strength of our faith that gets us into that room. It's the object of our faith, and Jesus is the perfect object of our faith. So I'm asking you to join me now in praying with confidence.

Count your sin as rubbish. Count your résumé as rubbish. Nothing you bring to the table gets you access to that room. But we're going to walk in there anyway with a swagger and a strut, because we belong there. Because that's our Dad: Abba, Father.

Because Jesus bought that room for us. So let's go in there together now. Let's pray.
