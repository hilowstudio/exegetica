# Framing: John 9:1-41, "Sign 6" (Unit 13)

Adam's theological framing for this unit, in his Theologian's voice (Reformed, exegesis-first, christotelic, grace-centered). Four lessons, all built on Adam's sermon 14, "Though I Was Blind, Now I See" (John 9). This unit plants no new graded verse. It is review only. Quote the ESV in the finished materials.

## The christotelic center

Jesus meets a man who has never seen a face, makes clay from dust and spit, and sends him to a pool named Sent. The man comes back seeing, and from that moment the chapter runs on two clocks at once. One man's sight grows brighter by the verse while a room full of trained eyes goes dark. The unit lands on Christ the light of the world (9:5, restating 8:12 from Unit 12) who gives sight to a beggar and receives his worship as God (9:38), and who divides the world by his coming into those who know they are blind and are given sight, and those who say "we see" and keep their guilt (9:39-41). Sermon 14 preaches the whole chapter as the shape of a real conversion: the Lord comes to a man with no power to come to him, opens his eyes, and the man ends on his knees. Adam's own testimony is the frame. He was utterly blind the day he met Jesus, and the only account he can give is the man's own, "I was blind, but now I see" (9:25).

- L1 (9:1-12) lands on Christ the light and the Sent One, who forms new eyes from dust and sends a blind man to the pool called Sent.
- L2 (9:13-23) lands on Christ confessed as a prophet by a man who keeps growing, while fear of the synagogue keeps his parents quiet.
- L3 (9:24-34) lands on Christ, whom the healed man confesses is "from God," and for whom he is thrown out.
- L4 (9:35-41) lands on Christ the Son of Man who receives worship as God, and who judges the world into sight and blindness.

John built the whole scene on the feast frame Units 10 and 12 set up. Booths lit great lamps in the temple (the setting of 8:12), and its water was drawn from Siloam (Unit 10). Both come back here. The light of the world gives light to blind eyes, and he does it through the pool the water ceremony used.

## The contested points, ruled

**"Who sinned, this man or his parents?" (9:2), and "Neither... but that the works of God might be displayed in him" (9:3).** Rule out the individual scoreboard, and hold God's purpose without cruelty. This is the payoff of the Unit 8 ruling on 5:14, and it has to be taught with the same care. The disciples voice a real assumption of their world, that a birth defect traces to somebody's sin, either the man's or the parents'. Jesus refuses the whole question as they framed it. Chrysostom reads the grammar carefully and it is worth the disciple tier: Jesus is not declaring the man and his parents sinless in general, he is severing this blindness from any private tally of their guilt. Chrysostom's pastoral point is the key: "blindness is not an evil, sin alone is an evil," and this man "received benefit from his blindness, since he recovered the sight of the eyes within." Theodore of Mopsuestia adds the restraint teenagers need: the causes of such suffering are secret, and what is hidden now will be made plain in the world to come.

Now the guard against cruelty, which is the heart of Adam's sermon on this verse. God did not blind a baby for decades merely to stage a miracle later. Teach it the way Adam does. The works of God "displayed in him" name what Christ does at the pool, the sight given and the worship it produces, and the man's whole life is gathered up and redeemed in that moment. Adam turns 9:3 straight into freedom for the suffering student: you did not owe the Lord your suffering for your sin, and the question to bring to your own pain changes from "what did I do wrong" to "how does my Lord want to display his glory here." Hold both halves the way Unit 8 held them. Suffering came into the world through sin in general, under the Fall, and this man's blindness was not the receipt for his private guilt. Refuse the cruelty of telling a sick teenager their illness is God's punishment, and refuse the sentimentality that empties the verse of God's real purpose. God means to be seen in this man, and being seen by God turns out to be the best thing that ever happened to him.

**"We must do the works of him who sent me... I am the light of the world" (9:4-5), and the tie to 8:12.** Rule 9:5 a deliberate restatement of the anchor students planted last unit, now acted out. At Booths Jesus said he is the light of the world under the temple lamps (8:12, Unit 12). Here he says "as long as I am in the world, I am the light of the world" (9:5) and then gives light to eyes that never had it. The cross-references tie 9:5 to 8:12, to 12:46, to the Prologue light thread (1:4-9), and to Isaiah 42:6-7, the Servant given as a light for the nations, to open the eyes that are blind. The claim of 8:12 stops being a saying and becomes a healing. The "we must work... while it is day" (9:4) carries the same urgency Unit 10 tracked in "my time," the Son living on the Father's clock and moving toward the night of the cross. For the room: last unit Jesus said he is the light, this unit he proves it on a man born in the dark.

**The mud, the spit, and the pool called Sent (9:6-7).** Rule the manner of the healing a deliberate sign, and rule Siloam a double meaning John hands us on purpose. Jesus heals plenty of people with a word. Here he kneels, makes clay, and sends the man to wash, and every early reader saw why. Irenaeus reads the clay as an echo of Genesis, the Word who formed man from the dust now forming the eyes that were missing. This is exactly Adam's instinct, that the God who made a whole man from dirt can make eyes from dirt, and Adam's own caution ("I am not saying he made little eyeballs") is the right level of restraint. Teach the creation echo as a window on his deity and do not press the mechanics past what the text says. Chrysostom explains the errand to Siloam plainly: Jesus sends the man away so nothing is ascribed to the fountain, since the power is Christ's word and not the water, and the long walk with clay on his eyes makes the whole neighborhood a witness. On the name, John stops the story to translate it. Siloam means "Sent," and Jesus is the Sent One (4:34; 5:23; 7:28; 8:26). Augustine says it flatly: had he not been sent, none of us would be set free from our sins. Give the whole room the plain picture (the pool is called Sent, and Jesus is the Sent One) and give the disciple tier Irenaeus on the clay and Augustine on the name.

**The growing confession against the hardening blindness (9:11, 17, 33, 38), the irony of the whole chapter.** Rule this the spine of the unit, and build the four lessons on its four rungs. The man's account of Jesus climbs every time they push him. "The man they call Jesus" (9:11), then "he is a prophet" (9:17), then "if this man were not from God, he could do nothing" (9:33), then "Lord, I believe," and he worshiped him (9:38). Adam's sermon tracks this rung by rung and makes it the pattern of a person coming to faith. The Pharisees move the opposite direction, from "this man is not from God" (9:16) to open contempt and expulsion (9:34), until Jesus names their state: they say "we see," and their guilt remains (9:41). The study notes call it a surprising reversal of roles, physical sight given to a beggar assumed to be in spiritual darkness, spiritual blindness settling on the men with working eyes. This is the payoff of the quiet contrast planted at Unit 8. The Bethesda man, healed and asked nothing, went and reported Jesus to the authorities (5:15). This man, healed and pressed hard, grows louder in confession the more they lean on him and loses his place in the synagogue for it. Teach the contrast gently. Both are pictures of grace to the undeserving, and the difference is what grace grows into when the Spirit is at work. Adam makes the man's boldness the model: you do not need all the answers, you can speak plainly to what is true for you, that you were once blind and now you see.

**"Put out of the synagogue" (9:22), and the cost of confession.** Rule the threat real and the parents' fear understandable, and rule "the Jews" here the hostile leadership, never the people. Being put out of the synagogue meant being shunned by your whole community, cut off from worship and neighbors, and "the Jews" in this verse names the leaders opposed to Jesus, since the parents themselves are Jewish. The parents confirm the two facts they cannot deny (this is our son, he was born blind) and hand the dangerous question back to him: "he is of age, ask him" (9:21, 23). Adam reads their evasion honestly. They had heard the name and were afraid to say it, because saying it would cost them the synagogue. Teach the cost as real and the fear as human, and let it land on the modern student who knows exactly what it is to go quiet when naming Jesus would cost them the room. Carry the anti-Jewish guard the whole course carries. Jesus, the man, his parents, and the divided Pharisees (9:16) are all Jewish, and John shows Jews divided over Jesus. The line measures response to Christ, and it falls on the churched student as readily as anyone.

**"I was blind, but now I see" (9:25), and the man's boldness under interrogation.** Rule it the irreducible testimony of a man who has no training and needs none. Pressed to condemn Jesus as a sinner ("Give glory to God. We know that this man is a sinner," 9:24, their formula for demanding a confession under oath), the man gives back the one thing they cannot argue him out of: "Whether he is a sinner I do not know. One thing I do know, that though I was blind, now I see" (9:25). This is the sermon's title and its center. Adam preaches it as the template for every believer, the plain report of what Christ has done. When they press again, the man turns sharp, "Do you also want to become his disciples?" (9:27), and reasons them into a corner, "if this man were not from God, he could do nothing" (9:33). His confidence rests on what happened to him, and his boldness grows out of sight already given.

**Jesus receiving worship (9:38), a claim to deity.** Rule the man's act worship of God, and rule Jesus' silent acceptance the proof. The verb ranges from bowing before a human superior to worship of God, and the lexicon lists 9:38 among the debated cases. The ruling rests on three things the text gives. John uses this same verb for worshiping God at 4:20-24 (Unit 6). The man's words are "Lord, I believe" (9:38), a confession of faith. And Jesus receives it and says nothing to stop it, where Peter refuses the same act (Acts 10:26) and the angel refuses it twice (Revelation 19:10; 22:9). John frames the scene so that a man given sight falls down before the Son of Man and is not corrected. Teach the whole room the plain sense, that the man ends up worshiping Jesus, and give the disciple tier the weight, that Jesus accepting worship is a claim only God can accept. This is the same forcing claim Unit 8 met at 5:23 and Unit 12 met in the "I am" sayings. Worship him, or account for why he took worship that belongs to God alone.

**"For judgment I came into this world" (9:39-41), the two kinds of blindness.** Rule it the dividing effect of the light, and guard it hard from becoming a verdict on intelligence. Jesus says, "For judgment I came into this world, that those who do not see may see, and those who see may become blind" (9:39). The cross-references anchor it in John's own light theology (3:19; 12:46; 8:12) and in the mission of Acts 26:18, to open their eyes and turn them from darkness to light. Augustine states the sense best: those who confess they cannot see and seek a physician are given sight, and those who think they can see and seek no physician remain blind. Cyril guards the hard edge, that Christ is not the author of anyone's blindness, and the unbelieving drew the affliction on themselves by refusing the light. When the Pharisees ask, "Are we also blind?" (9:40), Jesus answers that blindness owned is covered, and sight claimed keeps its guilt: "now that you say, 'We see,' your guilt remains" (9:41). Teach the two blindnesses as the sermon does. Humility that admits blindness receives sight, and claimed sight that will not come to the physician hardens into guilt. Adam widens it to every person: either you are blind and the light of Christ brings you into seeing, or you can see and the light of Christ blinds you, and no one can say Jesus is Lord except by the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 12:3).

## Christological and covenantal connections

- **The light of the world giving sight (8:12 and 9:5).** The anchor planted last unit is acted out here. Cross-references tie 9:5 to 8:12, 12:46, the Prologue (1:4-9), and Isaiah 42:6-7, the Servant who opens blind eyes. Christ says he is the light, then makes a blind man see.
- **The sign as a window (the semeion read from Unit 3).** John never calls this a wonder. The Pharisees themselves call it a sign (9:16), and the healing is a window onto the greater sight Jesus gives, physical eyes opened as the picture of the eyes of the heart opened. Read the deed through to the person, the Unit 3 rule.
- **Siloam "Sent" and Jesus the Sent One.** The pool's name is the chapter's quiet sermon. Jesus, sent by the Father (4:34; 7:28), sends the man to the pool called Sent. The Sent One is the fountain, and the pool only carries his name.
- **Jesus receiving worship as God (9:38).** The Son of Man takes the worship the man gives and does not refuse it, the deity claim the whole Gospel has been building (1:1; 5:23; 8:58; 20:28).
- **The 9:3 payoff of the Unit 8 sin-and-suffering ruling.** What Unit 8 promised at 5:14, this unit delivers at 9:3. The individual scoreboard is wrong, and God has a purpose in suffering that is kind, not cruel.
- **The growing-confession contrast to the Bethesda man (Unit 8).** The man of chapter 5 reported Jesus to the authorities. This man confesses him louder under pressure and is expelled for it. The contrast planted in Unit 8 pays off here as two shapes of grace received.

## Reaching the three-tier room

The contact points are strong and concrete: suffering you did not cause, being interrogated or pressured about your faith, growing more sure of Jesus over time, paying a real cost for standing with him, and the difference between admitting you are blind and pretending you can see.

- **Searching student.** The floor is the beggar and his one sentence. A man born blind gets his sight from Jesus and cannot explain how, and when the smartest people in town corner him, all he says is "I was blind, but now I see" (9:25). Every searching student should leave the unit able to find John 9 and say that sentence. Adam's testimony is built for this student, the man who was utterly blind the day he met Jesus, and Adam says plainly that you do not have to win an argument to belong to Jesus, you need the Spirit to open your eyes. The floor on 9:3 is the suffering point, aimed at the kid carrying pain they did not cause: your suffering is not God settling a score with you.
- **Churched but coasting.** Their mirror is the Pharisees and the parents. A person can know the rules cold, have working eyes, sit in the synagogue every week, and be the one going blind while the beggar sees. Press 9:41 gently: the danger is saying "we see" and never coming to the physician. And the parents are the coasting student's other mirror, people who heard the name and went quiet because saying it would cost them the room. Ask Adam's question in the plain form: when naming Jesus would cost you something, do you name him or hand the question back like the parents did.
- **Maturing disciple.** Give them the weight: 9:5 restating 8:12 and the Isaiah 42 Servant, Irenaeus on the clay as a Genesis echo, the Siloam-Sent wordplay and Augustine on it, the worship question at 9:38 and why Jesus' silence settles it, the four rungs of the confession against the Pharisees' hardening, and the two-blindnesses saying read with Augustine and Cyril and Romans 1. Let them trace how one healing sorts a whole town into sight and blindness.

Keep faith content invitational and participation assignable. Every student can analyze the confession that grows and the blindness that hardens. No student is required to profess it.

## Memory: review only

This unit plants no new graded verse. Students hold the seven anchors they already carry: 20:31, 1:14, 1:12, 3:16, 4:24, 6:35, and 8:12 (planted last unit in Unit 12). Assign nothing from chapter 9 to the graded line and quiz nothing new. Run a light cumulative pass on the seven, and let 8:12 ("I am the light of the world") sit next to 9:5, since 9:5 is the same claim acted out on a blind man. The next new anchor is 10:11, the good shepherd, in Unit 14.

Offer John 9:25 as an optional stretch off the graded line, on L3, where it lives: "One thing I do know, that though I was blind, now I see." It is on the taught-not-memorized list, it is the sermon's title and the unit's one unforgettable line, and it is short enough that a searching student can carry it. Keep it off the graded set, the way earlier units offered 2:11, 5:24, and 7:37-38. It is a gift for the student who wants one sentence to hold.

## Pitfalls with teenagers

- Making 9:3 mean "God blinded a baby just to show off." The cruel misread, and the biggest one. Teach it as Adam does. God's purpose in the man's suffering is kind, his whole life gathered up and redeemed the moment Christ opens his eyes, and Chrysostom's "blindness is not an evil, sin alone is an evil" keeps it from monstrous. Never let a student leave thinking God hurts people to make a point.
- The prosperity misread, "God always heals if you have enough faith." The opposite error. This man had no faith to heal himself, and Jesus came to him first. Cross before crown, healings are signs and not a formula.
- "Be bold like the blind man" moralism that misses the grace. The man is bold because he was found, not to get found. Keep the order straight: the Lord pursued him, and he had no ability to come on his own. Teach the boldness as fruit of sight given, never as the price of it. Watch the close of L3 for this legalist side door.
- Anti-Jewish reading of the Pharisees and the expulsion. "The Jews" in 9:22 names the hostile leadership. Jesus, the man, his parents, and the divided Pharisees are all Jewish. The line measures response to Christ, and it falls on the churched student in the room as readily as anyone.
- The two-blindness saying weaponized into "smart people can't be saved." The blindness Jesus names is the refusal to admit need, "we see" said by people who will not come to the physician (Augustine). Plenty of learned people have come to Christ as beggars. Aim 9:41 at pride, and keep it off the honor student.
- Using the man's suffering to shame anyone. His blindness is handled the way Unit 6 handled the woman's history, in daylight and gently, to heal. Do not use his years of begging, or anyone's suffering, as a lever.

## Also worth emphasizing

- "The man they call Jesus" (9:11), and the fact that the man has not yet seen Jesus. Through most of the chapter the man is confessing a Jesus he has never laid eyes on, because he was sent away to wash before he could see. His first sight of Jesus' face comes at 9:37. The confession grows in a man who is still, in one sense, waiting to see. A warm beat for the student whose faith is real and whose sight feels partial.
- Jesus finds him after the expulsion (9:35). "Jesus heard that they had cast him out, and having found him he said..." The shepherd goes looking for the one cast out for his sake. Aim it straight at the student who has paid a cost for naming Jesus. He sees you thrown out, and he comes and finds you.
- The spit and the reversal of uncleanness. Spit was thought to defile, and Jesus heals with it, the same reversal as touching a leper and cleansing instead of catching it. A small window on how grace runs the other direction through Christ. One beat for the disciple tier.
- Sermon 14 is the spine of the unit. Adam preached the chapter as the anatomy of a conversion, the growing confession as the pattern, "I was blind, now I see" as the one testimony every believer can give, and the two blindnesses as the line that runs through every person in the room. Borrow that arc lesson by lesson.

## The four-lesson split

The seams cut on the chapter's own scenes, grouped so each lesson takes one rung of the growing confession. This keeps every interrogation whole, gives the short Thursday the tight climactic scene, and lands every lesson on Christ.

- **L1 (i=53, Mon Nov 30, regular 50), John 9:1-12: the sign and the first questions.** The disciples' question and the ruling on suffering (9:2-3), "I am the light of the world" acted out (9:4-5), the clay and the pool called Sent (9:6-7), and the neighbors' first interrogation, where the man's confession begins at "the man they call Jesus" (9:8-12). Carry the 9:3 ruling in full (the Unit 8 payoff), the 8:12 tie, and the Siloam-Sent wordplay. Set the Feast of Booths frame from Units 10 and 12 here. Lands on: Christ the light and the Sent One, who forms new eyes from dust and sends a blind man to the pool called Sent.

- **L2 (i=54, Tue Dec 1, regular 50), John 9:13-23: the prophet, and the fear of the synagogue.** The first Pharisee interrogation and the division among them (9:13-17), the man's confession climbing to "he is a prophet" (9:17), and the parents' evasion under fear of expulsion (9:18-23). Carry the cost-of-confession ruling and the anti-Jewish guard on 9:22. Set the growing-confession contrast to the Bethesda man here. Lands on: Christ confessed as a prophet by a man who keeps growing, while fear of the synagogue keeps his parents silent.

- **L3 (i=55, Wed Dec 2, regular 50), John 9:24-34: "I was blind, now I see," and thrown out.** The second Pharisee interrogation (9:24-29), the man's testimony under pressure (9:25) and his sharp defense reasoning up to "if this man were not from God, he could do nothing" (9:30-33), and the expulsion (9:34). Carry the boldness-as-fruit-of-grace ruling and guard the legalist side door at the close. Offer John 9:25 as the optional stretch here, off the graded line. Lands on: Christ, whom the healed man confesses is "from God," and for whom he is cast out.

- **L4 (i=56, Thu Dec 3, Thursday 36, single-focus), John 9:35-41: found, worship, and two kinds of blindness.** One tight scene for the short day, one key question: "Two kinds of blindness stand in this room. Which one gets to see, and how?" Jesus finds the man he was thrown out for (9:35), the last rung of the confession, "Lord, I believe," and worship (9:36-38), and the saying that divides the world into sight and blindness (9:39-41). This is the climax the whole chapter has been climbing, and the single-focus Thursday gives it undiluted. Carry the worship ruling (received as God) and the two-blindnesses ruling (Augustine and Cyril, humility receives sight, claimed sight keeps its guilt). Lands on: Christ the Son of Man who receives worship as God, and who judges the world into sight and blindness.
