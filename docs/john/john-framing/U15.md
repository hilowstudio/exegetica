# Framing: John 11:1-57, "Sign 7 (Lazarus)" (Unit 15)

Adam's theological framing for this unit, in his Theologian's voice (Reformed, exegesis-first, christotelic, grace-centered). Five lessons. L1, L2, and L3 build on Adam's sermon 16, "The Death of a Friend" (11:1-35). L4 and L5 build on sermon 17, "Lazarus, Come Out!" (11:38-57). The tiny gap at 11:36-37 falls inside L3 and is framed with sermon 16's material. This unit plants the new graded anchor, John 11:25, on L2. Quote the ESV in the finished materials.


A word to set the room. John has told us this sign is the hinge. The raising of Lazarus is the last and greatest of the seven signs, and it is the one that sends Jesus to the cross (11:53). John frames the tomb of Lazarus so it rhymes with the tomb of Jesus: a cave, a stone, graveclothes, four days (11:38, 44; 20:1, 7). The Tyndale note names it plainly, that these details make Lazarus's story a sign pointing to the death and resurrection of Jesus. So the whole unit reads as a window (the semeion frame of Unit 3): a real dead man really raised, and through him a longer sight of the empty tomb and the life he gives his people. This is also the unit that carries the hardest pastoral weight in the whole course. Every teenager who has stood at a grave, or who is angry at God for not showing up, meets their own question in Mary and Martha's "if you had been here." Teach it tenderly and truthfully.

## The christotelic center

Jesus arrives four days late by his own design, stands at the grave of a friend he loves, weeps and burns with anger at death, and then calls a decomposing man out by name, and the greatest sign of life he ever gives is the very act that sets his own death in motion. The unit lands on Christ as the resurrection and the life in person (11:25), the one whose voice the dead obey (11:43-44, tied to 5:28-29), the good shepherd of Unit 14 whose laying-down of his life is now decreed in a high priest's unwitting prophecy (11:50-52), the one man who dies for the people and to gather the scattered children of God into one (11:52). The center in one sentence: resurrection is a Person, standing in the present tense at a sealed tomb, and to have him by faith is to have life now and life through death, purchased by the death this sign sets in motion.

- L1 (11:1-16) lands on Christ the Lord who loves and who waits in love, sovereign over the death he means to defeat, walking toward Judea and his own death to give life.
- L2 (11:17-27) lands on Christ the resurrection and the life in person, present now, the fifth predicated "I am."
- L3 (11:28-37) lands on Christ who weeps real tears with the grieving and burns with anger at death, the incarnate Son entering our sorrow.
- L4 (11:38-44) lands on Christ whose voice the dead obey, the raising enacted as a sign that points past itself to his own empty tomb.
- L5 (11:45-57) lands on Christ the one man who dies for the people and gathers the scattered children of God into one, the sign of life driving him to the cross as the Passover draws near.

## The contested points, ruled

**The deliberate delay (11:4-6), the hardest point in the unit.** Rule the delay an act of love aimed at a greater gift. Read the seam slowly. "Now Jesus loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus. So on hearing that Lazarus was sick, He stayed where He was for two days" (11:5-6). The "so" is the offense. The staying flows out of the loving. Sermon 16 refuses to soften it and neither should we: word reached Jesus in time, in fact too soon, because Lazarus needed to be dead and buried before Jesus arrived. Three of Jesus' own statements govern the ruling. "This sickness will not end in death. It is for the glory of God, so that the Son of God may be glorified through it" (11:4). "For your sake I am glad I was not there, so that you may believe" (11:15). "Did I not tell you that if you believed, you would see the glory of God?" (11:40). The love that delays is love aimed at resurrection over recovery. For the grieving or angry teenager, guard it hard. Never let the class hear that God delayed because someone lacked faith, prayed the wrong words, waited too long, or missed the moment. Sermon 16 is the antidote here and should be mined directly. God's timing can feel like absence and be love at the same time. The tears and the anger of 11:33-38 are the proof that the one who delayed is not cold. Cross before crown: Jesus does not spare Lazarus death, he defeats it.

**"This illness is for the glory of God" (11:4), taught without cruelty.** Rule that the glory of God and the good of the sufferer meet in the resurrection, and rule against turning the phrase into a slogan that cancels grief. The glory named here is displayed when Jesus raises the dead, and past that when the Son is himself glorified in his death and rising (11:4 says "so that the Son of God may be glorified"). The same chapter that says "for the glory of God" shows Jesus weeping and indignant at the grave. Hold both. A teenager angry at God for a death is not to be handed "it was for God's glory" as a lid on the pain. The glory is shown in a God who weeps at the tomb and then empties it.

**Death as sleep (11:11-14).** Rule "fallen asleep" (kekoimetai, 11:11) a tender and deliberate metaphor for the believer's death, chosen because for the one Jesus will wake, death is reversible the way sleep is reversible. Jesus tells the disciples plainly, "Lazarus is dead" (11:14), so the metaphor is not soul-sleep, not a doctrine that consciousness is extinguished. It teaches that death has become, under the voice of the Son, a state from which he wakes his own. The top cross-reference on 11:25, 1 Thessalonians 4:14, uses the same "fallen asleep" of dead believers. Teach the comfort in the metaphor and guard it from the annihilation misreading.

**Thomas, "let us also go, so that we may die with Him" (11:16).** Rule Thomas loyal and gloomy in one breath. He means dying with Jesus, since the disciples have just protested that the Jews there tried to stone him (11:8). His courage is real. His hope is thin, with no expectation that anyone is coming out of a tomb. Refuse to make him only a hero or only a doubter. This is the same Thomas of 20:24-29, devoted and unable to hope until he sees.

**Martha's partial faith (11:21-24).** Rule her faith genuine and incomplete. "Lord, if You had been here, my brother would not have died" (11:21) is grief-shaped trust. "Even now I know that God will give You whatever You ask of Him" (11:22) is high and vague. When Jesus says, "Your brother will rise again" (11:23), she reaches for orthodox Jewish end-times doctrine: "I know that he will rise again in the resurrection at the last day" (11:24). She holds the doctrine of a future resurrection and has no category for the Resurrection standing in front of her. The move Jesus makes in the next verse is from a doctrine and a date to a Person and a present tense.

**The plant, "I am the resurrection and the life" (11:25-26).** This is the anchor and it lands on L2. Rule three things. First, ego eimi, the fifth predicated "I am," present tense: "I am the resurrection and the life" (11:25), spoken while the stone is still in place. The resurrection is a Person, here, now, before any miracle. The Tyndale note frames it well: Jesus helped Martha believe in him not only as one who heals but as the one who defeats death, and victory over death comes from union with him. Second, the two clauses hold two promises. "Whoever believes in Me will live, even though he dies. And everyone who lives and believes in Me will never die" (11:25-26). The believer who dies physically will live (life through and beyond death). The living believer will never ultimately die (the second death has no claim). Sermon 16 reads these as two classes visible in Jesus' own day: the Old Testament saints who died in faith and yet shall live, and the living believer who like the thief on the cross closes his eyes here and opens them in paradise. Third, ground it: the cross-references run to 1 Thessalonians 4:14, John 5:21, John 3:36, and 1 John 5:11-12, "God has given us eternal life, and this life is in his Son." Life is in the Person. To have him is to have resurrection and life now.

**Martha's confession (11:27).** Rule it a true and full confession that still has room to grow. "Yes, Lord, I believe that You are the Christ, the Son of God, who was to come into the world" (11:27). It states the Gospel's own aim (20:31) and echoes Peter at 6:68-69 (Unit 9). She confesses more than she yet expects, since at 11:39 she still says her brother stinks after four days. Grace-first: her faith is real while her expectation is small, and Jesus honors it.

**"Deeply moved in spirit and troubled" (11:33, 38), embrimaomai.** Rule the Greek carries anger and indignation. The word study confirms it: G1690 embrimaomai, in the classical sources the snorting of warhorses, in the New Testament "to be moved with anger" (Abbott-Smith), used of stern rebuke at Mark 1:43 and of indignation at Mark 14:5. "Deeply moved" in the smooth translations undersells it. Jamieson-Fausset-Brown reads it as Jesus powerfully checking a surge of emotion. Rule the deepest object of the anger to be death itself and the whole regime of sin and death that has turned God's world into a valley of tombs. He stands at the grave of a friend and is furious at the grave. Sermon 16's observation belongs in the scene: the sincere tears of Mary and Martha are mixed with the tears of some in the crowd who will soon plot his death, and that intermingling troubles him. Keep that as part of what stirs him and do not make the mourners' unbelief the primary trigger. The guard for teenagers is exact: he is not scolding the grievers for crying, he is enraged at the enemy that made them cry.

**"Jesus wept" (11:35).** Rule these real human tears, and rule the Greek load-bearing. The word study settles a detail worth teaching: the crowd weeps with klaio (11:31, 33), the loud wailing of a funeral, forty times in the New Testament. Jesus weeps with dakruo (11:35), the only time that verb appears in the New Testament, quiet tears welling in the eyes. Abbott-Smith lists them as synonyms with dakruo the shedding of tears and klaio the audible cry. So the Son does not join the wailing, he sheds silent tears. The fathers guard both sides. Irenaeus and Chrysostom press the true humanity ("that thou mayest learn that He had of a truth put on our nature"). Hippolytus catches the balance: "He wept, assigning due measure to his sympathy," weeping with those who weep while refusing both tearlessness and theatrical mourning. Augustine: he wept to teach us to weep. Hold the tears and the anger together in one Lord. His tears are real and his tears are controlled, and they sit inside the same passage as his fury at death. He is the God who cries at the grave and shouts it down.

**"Lazarus, come out!" (11:43-44), and raised to die again.** Rule the life-giving word the enacted form of 5:25-29 (Unit 8). Sermon 17's center: the dead obey the voice of the Lord. The voice that said "let there be" at creation speaks to a corpse and it obeys. Ground the tie: 5:25, "the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God, and those who hear will live," with its "now here," and 5:28-29, the tombs emptying at his voice at the last day. Lazarus is 5:28-29 rehearsed on one man in advance. The cross-references on 11:43 gather the biblical company of raisings by God's word (1 Kings 17:21-22, 2 Kings 4:33-36, Luke 7:14-15, Acts 9:40). Now the crucial distinction, and it is the heart of sermon 17. Lazarus is raised to die again. He comes out still bound hand and foot in graveclothes, his face wrapped, a living man dressed as a dead one, and Jesus says "unwrap him and let him go" (11:44). This is a resuscitation, a sign that points past itself to the true and final resurrection. Set it beside Christ, who left his graveclothes folded in an empty tomb (20:6-7) and rose to die no more. Sermon 17 tells it through Adam's four-year-old asking, "did he ever die again?" The answer is yes, and that answer is why the cross matters: Jesus does for us what he did not do for Lazarus, he conquers death itself and gives one unending life before the face of God.

**The glory-of-God bookend and the prayer (11:40-42).** Rule 11:40 a call-back that ties the raising to the whole chapter. "Did I not tell you that if you believed, you would see the glory of God?" reaches back to 11:4 ("for the glory of God") and to the Martha dialogue (11:23-26). The glory of God frames the sign at both ends. Then Jesus prays aloud, "Father, I thank You that You have heard Me. I knew that You always hear Me, but I say this for the benefit of the people standing here" (11:41-42). Sermon 17's first point lives here: the Father always hears the Son, the unbroken communion of the triune God, and the prayer is spoken for the crowd's faith. Teach it as a model of prayer resting on union already secured, and as a claim of unbroken oneness with the Father.

**The council and Caiaphas's unwitting prophecy (11:47-52).** Rule Caiaphas's words true beyond his intent, and rule the theology substitutionary. The council fears losing "both our place and our nation" to Rome (11:48). Caiaphas, cold and political, says "it is better for you that one man die for the people than that the whole nation perish" (11:50), meaning simply that killing one troublemaker will protect the temple and the land. John then tells us what God meant: "Caiaphas did not say this on his own. Instead, as high priest that year, he was prophesying that Jesus would die for the nation, and not only for the nation, but also for the scattered children of God, to gather them together into one" (11:51-52). The whole tradition reads it one way. Origen and Augustine and Cyril and Aquinas all say the Spirit used the office of the high priest, on the Balaam pattern, to speak truth through a wicked mouth. Gill draws the substitution out: Jesus dies "in the room and stead of his people, as their surety." Clarke notes the doctrine of a vicarious death behind the words. The Greek preposition is huper, one man dying on behalf of and in the place of the people. So Caiaphas states the atonement while trying to arrange a murder. And the gathering of 11:52 opens the reach: the cross-references run first to John 10:16 (Unit 14, other sheep, one flock), then Isaiah 49:6, Ephesians 2:14-22, and 1 John 2:2.

**The irony (11:53).** Rule this the structural point of the whole unit. "So from that day on they plotted to kill Him" (11:53). The greatest sign of life sets Jesus' death in motion. The one who gives a dead man his life back is condemned for giving it. And this is providence, not accident: the sign that most clearly reveals him as the resurrection is the very thing that drives him to the cross, where he becomes the resurrection and the life for us. The crown of the seventh sign is the road to Calvary.

**The withdrawal and the Passover frame (11:54-57).** Rule this the opening of the Passion. Jesus withdraws to Ephraim near the wilderness with his disciples (11:54), the last quiet before the end, and the public ministry of signs closes here (sermon 17 marks this seam in Adam's own series). Then, "the Jewish Passover was near" (11:55). The frame that will carry the rest of the Gospel begins. The Lamb of God whom John announced at 1:29 draws toward his feast.

## Christological and covenantal connections

- **Jesus is the resurrection and the life in person (11:25).** The predicated "I am" now reaches the grave. The progression students have followed: bread (6:35, Unit 9), light (8:12, Unit 12), door and good shepherd (10:7, 10:11, Unit 14), and now resurrection and life (11:25). This is the "I am" that answers death, and it is a present tense before a sealed tomb.
- **Tied to 5:25-29 (Unit 8).** The dead hearing the voice of the Son and living is the doctrine of chapter 5 enacted on Lazarus. 11:43-44 is 5:28-29 in a rehearsal, one tomb emptied at his word as a sign of the day all tombs empty at his voice.
- **The good shepherd of Unit 14 about to lay down his life.** The shepherd who lays down his life of his own accord (10:11, 10:17-18) now has that death decreed in Caiaphas's prophecy (11:50-51). The unit shows the shepherd's death being set, and shows the reason: one man dying for the sheep.
- **The one flock, the scattered children gathered (11:52).** The cross-reference to 10:16 is direct. The death gathers Jew and Gentile, the scattered children of God, into one (Ephesians 2:14-22, Isaiah 49:6). The New Covenant people constituted by his blood.
- **The sign as a window (Unit 3 semeion).** The tomb, stone, graveclothes, and four days point past the resuscitation to the cross and the empty tomb (Tyndale note; 11:38, 44 rhyming with 20:1, 7).
- **Covenantally, the substitutionary heart of the New Covenant.** "One man die for the people" (11:50-51) states, from the high priest's own mouth, the death in the place of the people that inaugurates the New Covenant in his blood, and the ingathering of the scattered fulfills the Ezekiel promise of a re-gathered, resurrected people (Ezekiel 34, 37).
- **The Passover frame opening (11:55).** The hour that has hovered over the Gospel (Unit 3) now begins to arrive, and the Lamb of 1:29 moves toward the feast.

## Reaching the three-tier room

The contact points are the strongest in the course: grief and losing someone, being angry at God for not showing up, whether death is the end, a God who weeps with you, and hope that is a Person rather than a someday.

- **Searching student.** The floor is grief and the tears of Jesus. A God who cries at a grave, who hates death as much as you do, who shows up late and weeps anyway. Ask the plain question: when someone you loved died, did God care? John 11:35 is the answer, and it needs no Bible knowledge to feel. Then hand them the claim of 11:25, that hope has a name and is a Person you can know now. The delay is their honest question too, so give them sermon 16's honesty: the wait can feel like absence and still be love aimed at something greater.
- **Churched but coasting.** Their mirror is Martha. She has the doctrine cold, the resurrection at the last day (11:24), and she gives a true confession (11:27), and she still says "he stinks, it's been four days" (11:39). Right beliefs, small expectations. Press it gently: is the resurrection a doctrine you have filed under someday, or a Person standing in front of you now? Their second mirror is the council, wanting God's favor and a safe nation while refusing the lordship of the Christ who would cost them their status (sermon 17's close).
- **Maturing disciple.** Give them the weight. The embrimaomai anger and the dakruo/klaio distinction, held together with the tears. The two clauses and two classes of 11:25-26. The resuscitation-versus-resurrection distinction, Lazarus raised to die again in his graveclothes against Christ raised to die no more in an empty tomb. Caiaphas's unwitting prophecy and the Spirit's use of a wicked mouth through the office of the priest. The gathering of 11:52 tied to 10:16. And the irony of 11:53, that the sign of life triggers the death that makes the sign true for us. Let them trace the whole chain.

Keep faith content invitational and participation assignable. Every student can analyze the delay, the "I am," the Greek of the tears, and Caiaphas's irony. No student is required to profess.

## The memory verse, John 11:25 (this unit's new plant, on L2)

Plant and teach it on L2 (11:17-27), where it heads the Martha dialogue and gets the full period. ESV: "I am the resurrection and the life. Whoever believes in me, though he die, yet shall he live, and everyone who lives and believes in me shall never die" (11:25-26).

The fresh angle is the present tense and the Person. Students hold eight anchors already (20:31, 1:14, 1:12, 3:16, 4:24, 6:35, 8:12, 10:11), and four are "I am" sayings once 11:25 lands: bread, light, shepherd, and now resurrection and life. The earlier "I am" sayings tell what Jesus gives and is for us in life. This is the "I am" that answers the grave, and the verb is present. Jesus does not say "I will raise you at the end." He says "I am the resurrection," now, standing outside a tomb that is still shut. Teach the two promises plainly: the believer who dies will live, and the living believer will never ultimately die. The line for the room: the answer to death is a Person you can know today, and his name is Jesus. Let 11:25 sit next to 10:11 in the cumulative pass. Last unit's anchor said the good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep. This unit's says the one who lays it down is himself the resurrection and the life.

The other four lessons do not plant, teach, or quiz 11:25 as the graded verse. Keep the eight earlier anchors sharp. The next new anchor is 13:34-35 in Unit 18.

## Pitfalls with teenagers

- The delay taught so it sounds like God is indifferent to suffering. The biggest one. Never let a student leave thinking the wait proves God does not care, or that faster faith would have summoned help. Word reached Jesus in time and he chose to stay, and he stayed in love aimed at resurrection. The tears and the anger at the grave are the guard.
- "Jesus wept" turned into a greeting-card Jesus, or the anger of embrimaomai flattened out. Keep the dakruo tears real and the embrimaomai fury real, in the same Lord. He weeps with the grieving and rages at death, and he is about to die himself to end it.
- Resurrection reduced to "we will see people again someday," missing the present Person. The someday is true, and it stands on a present tense. He is the resurrection now, and union with him now is the whole point of 11:25-26.
- Confusing Lazarus's raising with the final resurrection. Lazarus is raised to die again, still in his graveclothes, a sign that points forward. The final resurrection is Christ's own and ours in him, out of the graveclothes forever. Sermon 17 carries this.
- Prosperity misreads, "God will heal or raise if you have enough faith." Jesus deliberately let Lazarus die. The sisters' faith did not prevent the death and did not summon the healing on a schedule. Sermon 16 spends its whole first half dismantling this. Never tell a suffering teenager that their healing waits on the size of their faith.
- Caiaphas's prophecy misused. It teaches God's sovereignty over even wicked speech and the substitutionary heart of the cross. It does not license reading our own words as prophecy, and it does not excuse Caiaphas, who sinned in his intent while the Spirit used the office (the fathers are unanimous here).
- Anti-Jewish reading of the council. The leadership acts out of fear of losing place and nation (11:48). The many who believed (11:45) are also Jews, and John shows Israel divided over Jesus. The same idolatry, wanting God's favor without Christ's lordship, falls on any heart in the room, including the churched student.
- Using grief to shame, "if you had more faith you would not be sad." Jesus wept. Grief is not unbelief. The tears of the Son sanctify the tears of the mourner (Romans 12:15). Never weaponize hope against someone who is grieving.

## Also worth emphasizing

- The dakruo and klaio distinction is the sharpest exegetical detail in the unit, and it is grounded in the word studies. The crowd wails (klaio), and Jesus sheds silent tears (dakruo, the only time in the New Testament). Surface it in L3. It kills the greeting-card reading and the stoic reading at once.
- The four days and "he stinks" (11:17, 39). Lazarus is decisively, publicly dead. Bethany is under two miles from Jerusalem (11:18), so the crowd of mourners is large and the death is beyond dispute. The magnitude of the sign rests on how dead Lazarus is.
- Two sisters, one grief, two temperaments. Martha runs out and argues theology (11:20-27). Mary stays home, then falls at his feet in tears (11:32). Both say the same words, "if You had been here" (11:21, 32). One Lord meets each where she is.
- The glory of God bookends the sign (11:4, 11:40). Do not teach the raising as a bare miracle. Frame it at both ends with the glory of God and the call to believe.
- The withdrawal to Ephraim (11:54) and the Passover (11:55) are the doorway into the Passion. Sermon 17 marks this as the seam in Adam's own series, the end of the public signs. Let L5 open that door.

## The five-lesson split

The seams cut on the chapter's own scenes, which line up with the two sermons three-and-two. Every lesson lands on Christ. L1 through L4 are regular fifty-minute days, and L5 is the tight single-focus Thursday and the last passage. The plant, 11:25, sits cleanly inside L2. The raising (11:38-44) gets its own full lesson in L4. The council and Caiaphas get L5.

- **L1 (i=62, Fri Dec 11, regular 50), John 11:1-16: the report, the delay, sleep and death, Thomas.** The sisters' word, "the one You love is sick" (11:3), the deliberate two-day delay flowing out of Jesus' love (11:4-6), the return to Judea toward the stoning threat (11:7-10), Lazarus asleep and then plainly dead (11:11-15), and Thomas ready to die with him (11:16). Carry the hardest pastoral crux here, the delay and "for the glory of God" and love that waits. Build on sermon 16. Lands on: Christ the Lord who loves and waits in love, sovereign over the death he means to defeat.

- **L2 (i=63, Mon Dec 14, regular 50), John 11:17-27: Martha meets Jesus. Plants and teaches John 11:25.** The four-days-dead setting (11:17-19), Martha's grief and partial faith (11:20-22), "your brother will rise again" and her default to the last day (11:23-24), the plant, "I am the resurrection and the life" with its two clauses (11:25-26), and her true confession (11:27). The memory verse heads its own lesson and gets the full period the plant deserves. Teach the present tense and the Person. Build on sermon 16. Lands on: Christ the resurrection and the life in person, present now.

- **L3 (i=64, Tue Dec 15, regular 50), John 11:28-37: Mary, the tears and the anger.** Mary called and rising quickly (11:28-31), Mary at his feet with the same words as her sister (11:32), the embrimaomai indignation (11:33), the dakruo tears (11:35), and the two responses of the crowd (11:36-37). The tiny gap at 11:36-37 is framed here with sermon 16's material. Hold the tears and the anger together, grounded in the Greek. Build on sermon 16. Lands on: Christ who weeps real tears with the grieving and burns at death, the incarnate Son entering our sorrow.

- **L4 (i=65, Wed Dec 16, regular 50), John 11:38-44: Lazarus, come out.** The second embrimaomai and the stone (11:38-39), the glory-of-God call-back (11:40), the prayer to the Father and the unbroken communion (11:41-42), the loud cry and the dead man obeying (11:43), and the graveclothes and "unwrap him" (11:44). Teach the voice the dead obey (5:25-29) and the resuscitation that points past itself, Lazarus raised to die again against Christ raised to die no more. Build on sermon 17. The raising gets its full lesson. Lands on: Christ whose voice the dead obey, the sign pointing to his own empty tomb.

- **L5 (i=66, Thu Dec 17, Thursday 36, single-focus), John 11:45-57: the council, Caiaphas, the plot, the Passover near.** The divided response (11:45-46), the council's fear of losing place and nation (11:47-48), Caiaphas's cold calculation turned by God into prophecy (11:49-52), the plot to kill him (11:53), the withdrawal to Ephraim (11:54), and the Passover drawing near (11:55-57). One key question for the short day: "Why does raising a dead man to life get Jesus killed?" Work 11:49-53 as the center, with the tail (11:54-57) as the quick turn into the Passion. Give Caiaphas the room he deserves, the substitution stated by a wicked mouth and the scattered children gathered into one (tie to 10:16). Build on sermon 17. Lands on: Christ the one man who dies for the people and gathers the scattered children of God into one, the sign of life setting his death in motion as the Passover opens.
