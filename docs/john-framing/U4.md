# Framing: John 3:1-21, the new birth (Nicodemus)

Adam's theological framing for this fresh-build unit, in his Theologian's voice (Reformed, exegesis-first, christotelic, grace-centered). Follow its rulings on the contested points. Quote the ESV in the finished materials.

## The christotelic center

John 3:1-21 is about a religious insider being told that no amount of religion gets him in: entering God's kingdom takes a birth he cannot perform, worked by the sovereign Spirit, given to those who look in faith to the Son of Man lifted up on the cross. It lands on Christ at verse 14: the crucified Jesus is the bronze serpent raised in the wilderness, and whoever looks to him lives. The passage moves from a problem no one can solve (you must be born again) to a Person who alone solves it (the Son given, lifted up, believed). That is the spine of the whole unit. Do not let it become a lesson about our decision. It is a lesson about God's gift.

## "Born again" vs "born from above" (anothen, 3:3, 7)

Teach both senses, but rank them. The word carries two meanings in John: "from above" (its sense in 3:31, 19:11) and "again/anew." John is doing both on purpose, and the double meaning drives the dialogue: Jesus means "from above" (a birth whose source is God), Nicodemus hears only "again" (back into the womb, 3:4), and the misunderstanding lets Jesus press the point. Primary sense: from above, from God. "Again" is true but secondary. For sophomores: give the primary meaning plainly ("a birth that comes down from God, not one you produce"), then show the wordplay once as the reason Nicodemus is confused. That is the right dose. The payoff is pastoral: "born again" is not a slogan for a certain kind of Christian; it names something God does to a person from outside themselves. Ground it in 3:6 and in 1:13 ("born not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God").

## "Born of water and the Spirit" (3:5)

Teach the Ezekiel 36:25-27 new-covenant reading. That is the ruling. In Ezekiel, God promises in one breath to "sprinkle clean water on you, and you will be clean" and to "put my Spirit within you." Water (cleansing) and Spirit (a new heart of flesh for the heart of stone) are two facets of the one promised work of God. Nicodemus, "the teacher of Israel" (3:10), is rebuked for not knowing "these things," which points to a text he should have known. Ezekiel 36 is that text. Rule out the alternatives briefly and kindly: not primarily Christian baptism (the scene predates it, and Jesus faults Nicodemus for not already understanding); not "water = natural birth, Spirit = second birth" (one preposition governs both nouns, so it is one birth, and that reading makes verse 6 redundant). Keep the surface simple: "God promised long ago to wash people clean and put his own Spirit inside them. Jesus tells Nicodemus that promise is what has to happen to him." Water is a door into the Spirit's work, not the doctrine itself.

## The sovereign Spirit (3:8), taught grace-first

Be explicit, and be glad about it. "The wind blows where it wishes, so it is with everyone born of the Spirit" (3:8). The Greek word is both wind and Spirit: you do not command the wind, you do not manufacture your own birth, and you did not choose to be born the first time. This is a monergism text: God alone does the deciding work of the new birth. Teach it as freedom, not a locked door. Lead with the good news the doctrine is: you are not left to save yourself. If the new birth depended on your willpower or your religious performance, you would be as stuck as Nicodemus. God does what you cannot. Refuse both ditches. Fatalism ("so why bother") is answered by the same passage, which calls people to look, believe, and come to the light (3:15-16, 21). God's sovereignty and the real call to believe sit side by side here. Hold them the way John does. For the anxious teenager ("have I done it right?"), the relief is: your job is to look to Christ, not to inspect the machinery of your own heart. Keep the power in Christ and the Spirit, never in the student's sincerity. This is where the grace-centered aim lives or dies.

## The bronze serpent and "lifted up" (3:14-15)

This is the hinge, the moment it becomes the gospel. Numbers 21:7-9 is the cross-reference behind 3:14; the "lifted up" chain runs into 8:28 and 12:32-34. The typology, plainly: Israel sinned, God sent venomous snakes, people were dying, and the remedy was strange. Look at a bronze snake on a pole and live. No medicine, no work, no climbing. Just a look of faith at what God provided. Jesus says the cross is that pole: "so must the Son of Man be lifted up." "Lifted up" is a double word again: crucified (raised on the wood) and exalted (raised in glory), one event at two heights. One image, three depths:
- New to the Bible: "People were dying from snakebite. God's cure was to look at a snake on a pole and live. Jesus says the cross is that pole. You are the one bitten. You look and live."
- Churched but passive: press why a snake, the emblem of the curse, is what heals. Christ was made the curse we deserved. Looking is not doing; it is trusting.
- Maturing disciple: the type teaches substitution and faith as the sole instrument. The bitten did not contribute; they looked. Grace, not achievement. Build a lesson around the look. It is the best teenage on-ramp in the unit.

## 3:16-21: teach 16 freshly, handle 17-21 pastorally

On 3:16, the problem is familiarity, so make it strange again by reading it slowly. The verb is gave. Land on three words the churched kid has stopped hearing:
- "so loved" is not "so much" (quantity) but "in this way" (manner): God loved like this, by giving his Son. Point up to verse 14. The manner of the love is the cross.
- "world" is not the deserving; it is the dark, hostile world of verse 19 that loved darkness. God loved that world.
- "gave" costs the Giver. A Father hands over a Son.
For the searching student who hears only Christianese, strip it to one sentence: "God looked at people who wanted nothing to do with him and gave up his own Son to rescue them." Read it as news.

On 3:17-21, get the order right. Verse 17 is the headline: God sent the Son to save, not to condemn. Verse 18: unbelief is already condemned, not because God is eager to condemn but because the light has come and people prefer the dark (3:19). Frame the judgment as self-selected: the verdict is that light came and was refused. This keeps God kind (he came to save) and sin serious (refusing the light is culpable). The honest, gentle word for teenagers: the danger is real, and the rescue is freely held out in the same breath. Do not soften the condemnation into vagueness, and do not wield it as a threat. Verse 21 ends in grace: the one who comes to the light is shown to have done his deeds "in God," so even his coming was God's work in him. The passage closes where it opened. God does the saving.

## Nicodemus himself

Real but modest use of his arc. He is a live person, not a foil. He comes at night (3:2), which John means as more than a timestamp given the light/darkness theme coming in 3:19-21: he comes out of the dark, tentatively. He reappears twice, and both times he has moved: he defends Jesus before the Sanhedrin (7:50-51), and he helps bury him with about seventy-five pounds of spices, a costly public act, no longer at night (19:39). Do not overclaim his conversion (John leaves it quiet), but the trajectory preaches: the new birth is often not a fireworks moment but a man edging out of the dark over years. For the slow, unsure, half-in believer, Nicodemus is a gift. Use him once at the start of the unit and once at the end, as bookends. Do not moralize him into "be brave like Nicodemus."

## Pitfalls to avoid

- The decision / "say this prayer" formula. This is the big one, and this text is the antidote. If the new birth is God's sovereign work from above (3:3-8), reducing it to a technique the student performs contradicts the passage. Call for faith; do not sell a method.
- Making it about the intensity of their experience. Redirect from their inner weather to the object of faith: look to Christ (3:14-15), not at yourself.
- Moralizing the light passages (3:19-21) into "stop doing bad things so you can come to the light." The coming to the light is grace, not the prerequisite for grace.
- Preaching fear as the motive. Fear diagnoses; it does not regenerate. Only the Son lifted up draws.
- Turning "born again" into tribal shorthand for a loud kind of Christian. Recover it as what God does to every believer.
- Flattening the bronze serpent into "look up to Jesus when life is hard." It is substitutionary rescue from death, not a coping tip.
- Over-teaching the Greek. One note on "from above" and one on "lifted up" is plenty.

## Also emphasize

- The "Truly, truly" (amen amen) formula, three times here (3:3, 5, 11). Jesus staking his authority on what follows. A road sign that says "this is bedrock."
- Verse 13, descent and ascent: "No one has ascended into heaven except he who descended, the Son of Man." Jesus can speak of heavenly things because he came down from there. This grounds his authority and sets up "gave his Son" in 16. One clear beat for the maturing tier.
- The covenantal frame. Nicodemus expected the kingdom by descent and law-keeping. Jesus says entry is by new birth, the Ezekiel 36 new-covenant work. The new birth is not a private experience invented in the New Testament; it is the ancient promise of God arriving in Christ.
- "You must" (3:7) is plural in Greek. Jesus widens it from Nicodemus to everyone. Not a special requirement for notorious sinners; the human condition. Good for the churched kid who assumes the new birth is for other people.

## The four-lesson split

- L1, 3:1-8: the night visit and the new birth. Nicodemus the insider; born from above; water and Spirit (Ezekiel 36); the wind and the sovereign Spirit. Lands on: you cannot birth yourself, God does it.
- L2, 3:9-15: "How can this be?" and the bronze serpent. Bewilderment, the descent of the Son of Man (13), the pole in the wilderness (14-15, Numbers 21). Lands on: look and live. The gospel-crescendo lesson.
- L3, 3:16-17: God so loved the world. Slow, fresh reading of the memory verse, planted here by design. "So loved" as manner, "world" as the hostile dark, "gave" as costly. Lands on: the love is the cross.
- L4, 3:18-21: light and darkness, and the verdict. Belief and unbelief, condemnation handled pastorally, coming to the light, and Nicodemus's later arc (7:50-51; 19:39) as the closing bookend. Lands on: the one who comes to the light was drawn there by God.
