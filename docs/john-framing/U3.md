# Framing: John 2:1-25, "Sign 1 and the temple" (Unit 3)

Adam's theological framing for this unit, in his Theologian's voice. Quote the ESV in the finished materials. Build the temple lessons (L2-L4) on Adam's sermon 03, "Zeal for the Lord."

## The christotelic center

Jesus walks into the two centers of Jewish religion, a purification-wedding and the temple at Passover, and shows that he is what both were pointing at all along. At Cana he takes the water of the old rites and gives the good wine of the new covenant, revealing his glory. In Jerusalem he clears his Father's house and then names himself its replacement, the true temple, destroyed and raised in three days. The center is Christ as the reality every ritual and building was a shadow of. He is the abundance the feast promised and the meeting place with God the temple could only point to. The unit lands on 2:19-21: the temple of his body, torn down at the cross and raised on the third day, is now where God and sinners meet. It closes at 2:23-25 with the warning that guards the whole thing: a man can be dazzled by the signs and still be a stranger to the Christ they point to.

## "Sign" (semeion) and what Cana reveals (2:11)

John never calls Jesus' miracles "wonders." He calls them signs (semeion, "a mark by which a thing is known," an indication of something beyond itself). A sign points past the deed to the identity of the doer. John says so at the end: these are "written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ" (20:31), and the cross-reference data ties 2:11 straight to 20:30-31 and to 1:14, "we have seen his glory." For the room: a sign is a window. You look at what Jesus does and you see who Jesus is. Water to wine is the first of seven, and the number matters. Seven is fullness in John, and the signs run from this wedding to the raising of Lazarus, each one opening a window on his glory until the last window is the cross itself.

What Cana reveals: covenant abundance arriving in person. The detail John plants is the six stone jars "for the Jewish rites of purification" (2:6), each holding twenty to thirty gallons. Jesus gives new meaning to the purification water, showing that something greater than ritual washing is now present. The water of the old washings becomes the best wine, served last and in flood quantity, roughly a hundred and fifty gallons. This is the wedding feast the prophets promised, when the Lord makes "a feast of rich food, a feast of well-aged wine" (Isaiah 25:6) and the mountains "drip sweet wine" (Amos 9:13; Joel 3:18). The Bridegroom has come, and the party the old order kept gesturing toward has started. One caution on the six jars: read them as the old purification giving way to the new wine, and stop there. Do not preach "six means incomplete." The jars and the wine carry the freight.

## "My hour has not yet come" (2:4)

The word is hora, "a definite appointed time." When Jesus tells his mother "my hour has not yet come," he names something the whole Gospel circles back to. Teach the chain as one thread: 7:6, 7:30, and 8:20 ("his hour had not yet come"), then the turn at 12:23, "the hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified," then 13:1 and 17:1. In John the hour is when Jesus is glorified, and that happens when he is lifted up on the cross. From his mother's request for wine, Jesus is already looking at the cross. His whole life runs on the Father's clock, and the appointment at the end of the clock is Calvary. For a fifteen-year-old the payoff is concrete: Jesus is never improvising and never running late. He moves toward the cross on purpose, on time. Cana is a small sign of the great hour still ahead, a wedding pointing to a death that makes the real wedding possible.

## The temple cleansing and its early placement (2:13-17)

What Jesus does: he acts as a prophet reclaiming his Father's house for its purpose. The market had colonized the one court a Gentile could stand in to seek God, so the outer ring where the nations came near had become a stockyard and a currency exchange. Jesus drives it out. The disciples remember Psalm 69:9, "zeal for your house will consume me." Build the zeal directly on Adam's sermon 03, because his reading is right and the class should inherit it. The verb "consume" means to eat up, to devour. Psalm 69 is a psalm of a righteous sufferer, and the line the disciples recall is a line about being eaten alive by devotion to God's house. Jesus' zeal for true worship is the zeal that will get him killed. He cleanses the stone temple today so that tomorrow he can give up his body and open access to God forever. The whip is a sign of a love willing to be consumed.

On placement, rule decisively. John puts this at the start of the ministry; Matthew, Mark, and Luke put a temple cleansing in the final week. Set the "two cleansings" theory aside as the load-bearing answer and teach John's own logic, which Adam's sermon already gives the class: John arranges by theology, and he told us so by opening at creation rather than at Bethlehem. John moves the temple scene to the front because it is programmatic. It announces the theme of the whole book on page two: the old temple is ending, and a new one is standing in the court clearing out the traders. Whether a similar act happened twice is a fair question for a sharp student, and the honest answer is that Scripture does not settle it. John's point does not hang on it. He placed it here to tell you at the outset who Jesus is and what he came to replace.

## "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up" (2:19-22)

This is the hinge of the unit, and the Thursday lesson lands on it. It is the christotelic peak of chapter 2. They ask for a sign to justify his authority. He gives them the deepest sign in the book and veils it: tear down this temple and in three days I will raise it. They hear a claim about Herod's forty-six-year building project (2:20). John tells us the meaning in his own voice: "he was speaking about the temple of his body" (2:21). The saying comes back at his trial (Mark 14:58; Matthew 26:60-61) and connects to the sign of Jonah (Matthew 12:40). Teach three moves. First, Jesus is the true temple, the place where God dwells with people, which the Prologue already said when the Word "tabernacled among us" (1:14). Second, the sign he offers is his own death and resurrection, the whole gospel folded into one sentence on day one. Third, the disciples understood none of it until later: "when he was raised from the dead, his disciples remembered" (2:22). Rule that last point pastorally. Understanding came after the resurrection, in hindsight, by the Spirit. Following Jesus with real questions still unanswered is the normal shape of discipleship, and the disciples model it.

## 2:23-25: sign-chasing belief and the heart Jesus reads

Rule this as the deliberate sting in the tail, and protect its grace. Many "believed in his name when they saw the signs" (2:23), and in the very next breath "Jesus did not entrust himself to them" (2:24). John uses the same verb (pisteuo) for their believing and for his not-entrusting, a deliberate play: they trusted him, he did not trust them. Why: "he knew what was in man" (2:25). A faith that is only impressed by the miracles has not yet grown into a faith that rests on the Savior. Adam's sermon 03 preaches this with force, and it carries straight into the lesson: the question is whether Jesus, looking into your heart, entrusts himself to you, or whether you are still bartering, bringing your own righteousness to the exchange. Guard the grace-first frame hard here so it does not curdle into "believe harder or you are faking." The relief is that Jesus knows every heart in that Passover crowd and every heart in the classroom, and he still holds out himself to be received. The cure for shallow faith is coming to him empty-handed. Being fully known by him is safe for the one who comes with nothing to offer.

This bridges straight to Nicodemus, and the seam is exact. Chapter 2 ends "he knew what was in man"; chapter 3 opens "there was a man of the Pharisees named Nicodemus" (3:1). John set it up on purpose. Here is a real man, a religious insider, a night-time believer impressed by signs ("no one can do these signs that you do unless God is with him," 3:2), and Jesus does not need anyone to tell him what is in this man. He tells the man what he needs: you must be born again. Unit 4 opens on the person 2:25 just described. Teach 2:23-25 as the doorway into Nicodemus, and the U4 framing carries it from there.

## Christological and covenantal connections

- True temple (2:19-21). The clearest type in the chapter. The building where God met Israel gives way to the body of Christ, and the New Testament runs with it: the risen Christ is the temple, and by the Spirit his people become one too (Adam's sermon walks this to the church as Christ's body, sound and worth keeping for the disciple tier). This is the Progressive-Covenantal spine: Christ is the antitype the temple was a shadow of.
- The wedding feast (2:1-11). The messianic banquet of Isaiah 25:6 and the new-covenant abundance of Amos 9 and Joel 3, arriving in a Galilean village. Jesus is the Bridegroom, a thread John the Baptist picks up next unit ("the friend of the bridegroom," 3:29) and Revelation closes with (the marriage supper of the Lamb).
- Passover (2:13). John dates the scene to Passover and dates the cross to Passover too (19:14). Unit 2 planted the Lamb of God who takes away sin; here the Lamb walks into the temple at the feast that remembers the first lamb's blood. Let the U2 Lamb and this Passover setting shake hands.
- "On the third day" (2:1) and "in three days" (2:19). John frames the chapter with resurrection language front and back. One beat for the disciple tier.

## Reaching the three-tier room

- (S) New to the Bible. Keep the floor at pictures they can hold. Cana: Jesus shows up at a wedding, the wine runs out, and he makes about a hundred and fifty gallons of the best wine anyone tasted. That is the first "sign," a clue to who he is. The temple: Jesus walks into church and finds a flea market blocking the door where outsiders came to pray, and he clears it, because his Father's house is for meeting God. "Destroy this temple": Jesus points at himself and says the real meeting place with God is standing right here, and he will prove it by dying and rising in three days. Make sure every (S) student can say one sentence: a sign points to who Jesus is, and Jesus is where you meet God. Make sure they can find John 2 in the book.
- (B) Churched but coasting. Their risk is that "water into wine" and "cleansing the temple" are flannelgraph reruns. Slow down on 2:23-25 and let it land on them by name. It is possible to believe the miracles happened, to like Jesus, to have grown up on him, and never to have handed him your empty hands. Ask Adam's question: has Jesus entrusted himself to you, or are you still impressed from a distance. Press the six stone jars until they see the old religion of washing-up running dry and the new wine of Christ filling the room.
- (D) Maturing disciple. Give them the weight: semeion as window rather than spectacle, the hora chain from 2:4 to 17:1, John's theological arrangement against the Synoptic placement, Psalm 69 as the suffering-sufferer psalm, the true-temple typology into the resurrection and the church, and the pisteuo wordplay in 2:23-24. Let them trace "the hour" across the whole Gospel.

Keep faith content invitational and participation assignable. Every student can analyze what the signs reveal and how John argues. No student is required to make the confession the signs call for.

## The memory verse: review only, no new verse

Unit 3 plants nothing and quizzes nothing new. Students keep reviewing the three anchors they hold: John 20:31 (U0), 1:14 (U1), 1:12 (U2). Assign no verse from chapter 2 to memory, and quiz none. The next new anchor is John 3:16 in Unit 4.

Two notes for the builder. First, 2:11 is on the Build Spec's secondary list (taught, optional stretch, never required or scored). Teach it as the key verse of L1 because it is the unit's thesis ("the first of his signs... manifested his glory... and his disciples believed"), and offer it as an optional stretch for the (D) students who want it. Keep it off the graded memory line. Second, this review-only week is a gift of margin. Use a few minutes to let the three anchors breathe next to chapter 2. John 1:14, "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us," is the same tabernacle language that makes "the temple of his body" land, so a backward glance at 1:14 during the L3 Thursday does real teaching work with zero new memory load.

## Pitfalls with teenagers

- Turning the temple cleansing into a permission slip for anger. This is the big one, and Adam's sermon dismantles it. The whip is not a model for your temper. Jesus made the whip deliberately, out of zeal that would consume him to death. Teach the zeal, not the tantrum.
- Reading Cana as an endorsement of getting drunk, or dodging the wine into grape juice. Handle it plainly and without panic. Jesus made real wine, a lot of it, for a celebration, and the Bible both blesses wine as a gift and warns against drunkenness. Do not let the miracle become a debate about alcohol. The sign is about the Bridegroom and the feast. Name the question honestly if a student raises it, then steer back to the glory revealed.
- Draining "sign" into "magic trick." For the churched kid the miracles can become a highlight reel. Keep pointing through the deed to the person. A sign that only amazes has done half its work.
- Making 2:23-25 a fear test. "Jesus did not entrust himself to them" can land on an anxious teenager as "you are probably a fake." Lead with grace. Jesus knows every heart and still holds himself out to be received. The cure for shallow faith is coming to him empty-handed. Keep the power in Christ received, never in the intensity of the student's sincerity.
- Over-teaching the Synoptic problem. One clear beat that John arranges by theology is plenty. Do not let the class turn into a harmonization seminar.
- Over-teaching the Greek. One note on semeion (a sign points past itself), one on hora (the appointed hour, aimed at the cross). That is the whole ration across four lessons.
- Six-jars numerology. Resist "six is the number of incompleteness." The text gives purification water becoming wine. Preach that.

## Also worth emphasizing

- The Gentile court. The market sat in the one place a non-Jew could come near to God, so blocking it was blocking the nations. Jesus clears the outsider's access. For a mixed-faith room this is pointed good news: Jesus fights for the outsider's way in. Adam's sermon draws this out.
- Mary's line, "Do whatever he tells you" (2:5). The last recorded words of Jesus' mother in this Gospel, and a clean summary of discipleship. One beat, aimed at everyone.
- Belief that grows. The disciples "believed in him" at Cana (2:11), then believed more fully after the resurrection (2:22). Faith in John has a floor you can enter at and a ceiling it grows toward. Encouraging for the student who feels his faith is small.
- The two rememberings. The disciples remember Psalm 69 in the moment (2:17) and remember Jesus' words after the resurrection (2:22). John teaches how Scripture and the risen Christ light each other up in hindsight. A quiet model for how these students will one day read their own lives.

## The four-lesson split (keep it)

The split cuts on the two scenes and on real thought-seams inside the temple scene, and each lesson lands on Christ. Calendar: i=11 Tue regular, i=12 Wed regular, i=13 Thu 36-min single-focus, i=14 Fri regular.

- L1 (i=11, Tue, regular 50): 2:1-11, the wedding at Cana. The first sign, water to wine, the six purification jars giving way to the best wine, "he manifested his glory, and his disciples believed." Teach semeion here and "my hour has not yet come" (2:4) as the first glance toward the cross. Lands on: the Bridegroom has come and brought the feast, and the sign is a window on his glory. No sermon of Adam's covers Cana, so L1 leans on this brief.
- L2 (i=12, Wed, regular 50): 2:12-17, the temple cleansing. Attach the travel note 2:12 and the Passover setup 2:13 as the hinge into Jerusalem. The market in the Gentile court, the whip, "zeal for your house will consume me" (Psalm 69:9). First part of Adam's sermon 03. Lands on: a zeal for true worship that will be consumed at the cross.
- L3 (i=13, Thu, 36-min single-focus): 2:18-22, "destroy this temple." One saying, the peak of the unit, exactly what a single-focus Thursday wants (the same shape U2 gave the Lamb of God). Jesus the true temple, his death and resurrection as the sign, the disciples understanding only later (2:22). Middle of Adam's sermon 03. Lands on: the risen body of Jesus is where God and sinners now meet.
- L4 (i=14, Fri, regular 50): 2:23-25, sign-chasing belief and the heart Jesus reads. Three verses on a full period, so build it as unit capstone plus bridge. Spend the first half gathering the whole chapter (the two scenes, what the signs revealed, the belief John commends in 20:31 measured against the belief here), then land on 2:23-25 and open the door to Nicodemus. Last part of Adam's sermon 03. Lands on: Jesus knows every heart and still holds himself out to be received, and the man he is about to meet in 3:1 is the man 2:25 just named.

One note for the builder on L4. Its three verses would starve a 50-minute regular day on their own, so the capstone-and-bridge shape is load-bearing, not optional. Given how naturally 2:24-25 flows into 3:1, keep L4 as its own day rather than folding it into L3 or pushing it into Unit 4. The seam John built between "he knew what was in man" and "there was a man named Nicodemus" is worth its own day, and it hands Unit 4 a running start.
