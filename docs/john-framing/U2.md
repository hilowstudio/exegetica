# Framing: John 1:19-51, "Witness and first disciples" (Unit 2)

Adam's theological framing for this unit, in his Theologian's voice. Quote the ESV in the finished materials.

## The christotelic center

Unit 1 told you who Jesus is before he did anything. Unit 2 is the first week people meet him, and every voice in it points the same direction, away from the speaker and toward Christ. John the Baptist says "I am not the Christ" and then "Behold, the Lamb of God." Andrew finds Peter. Philip finds Nathanael. The chapter is a relay of witnesses handing off to Jesus, and it closes with Jesus himself opening heaven over the Son of Man. The center is Christ received through witness: God brings people to his Son by the testimony of people who found him first, and the One they find is the Lamb who takes away sin and the ladder between heaven and earth. Where the Prologue announced "to all who received him" (1:12), this unit shows the first receiving happen, one name at a time.

## The Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world (1:29)

Teach the title as deliberately full. John the Baptist reaches for a word every Jewish hearer knew from several directions at once, and the Gospel cashes it out at the cross. Rank the backgrounds.

Primary: the suffering servant of Isaiah 53. "Who takes away the sin of the world" is the tell. Isaiah 53 is the one Old Testament text where a lamb-figure carries sin for others: "like a lamb that is led to the slaughter" (53:7), "he bore the sin of many" (53:11-12). The cross-reference data ranks Isaiah 53:7 first for this verse, and the New Testament reads the servant as a lamb (Acts 8:32 quotes Isaiah 53:7; 1 Peter 1:19 calls Christ "a lamb without blemish"). The verb behind "takes away," Greek airo, means to lift and carry off. Jesus picks sin up and removes it. This is the sin-bearing lamb.

Second, and John's own frame: the Passover lamb. John dates the crucifixion to the day the Passover lambs were killed (19:14), and at the cross he alone notes that no bone of Jesus was broken (19:36), quoting the Passover rule of Exodus 12:46. John teaches the Passover reading by how he ends the book. The Passover lamb marks the blood that turns death aside and frees a people from bondage. Lead with these two together: the Lamb who bears sin (Isaiah 53) and the Lamb whose blood delivers (Passover).

For the disciple tier only, name the wider world the title lives in: the daily temple lamb (the tamid, Exodus 29:38-46, sin needing constant covering) and the ram God provided in place of Isaac (Genesis 22:8, "God will provide for himself the lamb"). Do not build a lesson on these. The Genesis 22 line is worth one sentence for a sharp student, because it answers who provides this lamb. God does. The "of God" means God's own provision.

What the title predicts: the cross. On day one of Jesus' public life, before a single sign, the direction of the whole Gospel is spoken over him. This man dies as a sacrifice, and his death removes sin. And the reach is "the world," the hostile, dark world of the Prologue (1:10-11). Plant the cross here, so that when the course reaches chapter 19 students have carried the Lamb in their pocket since chapter 1.

## The chain of witness and John's theme of testimony

This is the structural spine of the unit, so teach it as the thing that ties the four lessons together. The words "testimony" and "testify" (Greek martyria, martyreo) run through the passage: 1:19 opens "this is the testimony of John," and 1:32-34 ends "I have seen and I bear witness that this is the Son of God." Then the witness passes to ordinary people. Andrew hears John, follows Jesus, and the first thing he does is find Simon: "We have found the Messiah" (1:41). Philip finds Nathanael with the same words: "We have found him" (1:45). John points to Jesus. Andrew points Peter to Jesus. Philip points Nathanael to Jesus.

Rule the theme this way: a witness is someone who says what he saw and gets out of the way. John the Baptist is the model, and he is the model for the teacher too (the posture Unit 1 named, "point at Christ and get out of the way," which Unit 5 seals with "He must increase, but I must decrease"). For the room, the payoff is direct and gentle: you do not need a degree to bring someone to Jesus. Andrew brought his brother. Philip brought a friend. The whole method of the church is one person saying "come and see" to one other person.

## The pile-up of titles

Count them: Lamb of God (1:29, 36), Rabbi (1:38, 49), Messiah/Christ (1:41), the One Moses and the prophets wrote about (1:45), Son of God (1:34, 49), King of Israel (1:49), Son of Man (1:51). Seven names in thirty verses. The danger is a vocabulary quiz. Govern it with one move: these are seven camera angles on one person, stacked on purpose to say what Thomas will say at the end, "My Lord and my God" (20:28).

Give the room a simple frame. Two questions answer most of the titles: what is he here to do, and who is he. Lamb of God answers what he does, he takes away sin. Messiah, King of Israel, and the One Moses wrote about answer who he is in Israel's story, the promised rescuer the whole Old Testament was waiting for. Son of God and Son of Man answer who he is in himself, God's own Son and the heavenly man of Daniel 7. Rabbi is the first small step, the disciples start by calling him Teacher and grow into calling him Lord. Teach two or three titles well per lesson: let Lamb carry L2, Messiah and Cephas carry L3, Son of God, King of Israel, and Son of Man carry L4. A sophomore who leaves able to say "all these names point at one person, and the person is Jesus" has the lesson.

## "Come and see" (1:39, 46)

This is the invitation of the unit, aimed straight at the searching student. Two disciples ask Jesus where he is staying, and he says "Come and see" (1:39). Nathanael scoffs, "Can anything good come out of Nazareth?" and Philip answers with the same two words (1:46). Rule it as the honest on-ramp for doubt. Jesus hands the first disciples an invitation to spend the day with him and find out. Philip invites Nathanael to look.

For the room this is the most usable line in the chapter. To the student who has questions and is not sure he believes any of this, the passage says come and see. Christianity opens with an invitation to investigate a person. Tie it to the course's aim: we spend nine months coming and seeing. Nathanael came to scoff and left confessing. The invitation is safe to accept.

## The Jacob's-ladder saying and "Son of Man" (1:51)

Jesus tells Nathanael, "Truly, truly, I say to you, you will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man." The cross-reference is Genesis 28:12, the top-ranked link: Jacob, running for his life, sleeps at a place he will name Bethel ("house of God") and dreams of a stairway between earth and heaven with angels going up and down and the Lord standing above it. Jesus puts himself where the ladder was. Rule it plainly: Jesus is claiming to be the ladder, the one place where heaven and earth are joined and where God comes down to be with people. Jacob found a house of God in the desert. Jesus says the house of God is now a person. What the Prologue said about the Word "tabernacling" among us (1:14) lands here again.

"Son of Man" is the title he chooses for himself. Rule it as more than "a human being." It reaches back to Daniel 7:13-14, where "one like a son of man" comes on the clouds to the Ancient of Days and receives everlasting dominion over all peoples. Stephen sees him there (Acts 7:56). So Jesus is claiming the heavenly figure of Daniel who is given the kingdom, and he is saying that this figure is the meeting point of heaven and earth. For the room: Jesus is the door between God and us. To reach heaven you do not climb. You come to him.

## Jesus already knowing Nathanael (1:47-48)

Do not rush past this, because it is the moment Nathanael's scoffing breaks. Jesus says of a man he has never met, "Behold, an Israelite indeed, in whom there is no deceit" (1:47), and when Nathanael asks how, Jesus says, "Before Philip called you, when you were under the fig tree, I saw you." Rule it two ways.

First, it shows Jesus knowing a heart he has no natural way to know. This is the Prologue's Word who made all things (1:3) seeing a man before the man sees him. The course meets the same power with the Samaritan woman ("he told me all that I ever did," 4:29). Jesus knows you before you know him. For a teenager that cuts two directions, and both are good news. He sees what you hide, and he still calls you.

Second, catch the Jacob echo, because John built it in. Jesus calls Nathanael "an Israelite in whom there is no deceit." Israel is Jacob's second name, and Jacob was the deceiver (Genesis 27, he steals the blessing by lying). Jesus greets Nathanael as a true son of Israel without Jacob's old fault, and two verses later gives him Jacob's own dream. The man with no deceit gets the vision the deceiver got. For the disciple tier this is a beautiful thread. For the searching student, keep it simple: Jesus saw Nathanael honestly, named something true and good in him, and Nathanael went from "can anything good come from Nazareth" to "you are the Son of God, you are the King of Israel" in one exchange. Being known by Jesus is what turned him.

## Reaching the three-tier room

- (S) New to the Bible. Keep the floor at people meeting a person. The Lamb of God is God's way of taking your sin and carrying it off, the way you carry out the trash and it is gone. "Come and see" means you are invited to check Jesus out for yourself before you decide. Andrew and Philip are two guys who told a brother and a friend, "you have to meet this man." Make sure every (S) student can say one sentence: witnesses kept pointing at Jesus, and the ones who came and saw ended up following him. Make sure they can find John 1:19 in the book.
- (B) Churched but coasting. Their risk is that "Lamb of God" is a song lyric they stopped hearing. Slow it down. Ask why a lamb, and sit them in Isaiah 53 and the Passover until the word has blood in it again. Press "come and see" personally: the first disciples spent a whole day with Jesus (1:39), and coasting is possible precisely because you have never actually come and looked.
- (D) Maturing disciple. Give them the weight: the airo verb and Isaiah 53's sin-bearing, John's Passover framing at 19:36, the Daniel 7 Son of Man, the Bethel ladder typology, and the Jacob-Nathanael reversal. Let them chase the witness structure across all four "next day" scenes.

Keep faith content invitational and participation assignable. Every student can analyze who the witnesses say Jesus is. No student is required to make Nathanael's confession.

## The memory verse: John 1:12

"But to all who did receive him, who believed in his name, he gave the right to become children of God." Its text sits back in the Prologue, and the course plants it here on purpose, because Unit 2 is where receiving him stops being an idea and becomes a scene. Teach it as the caption over the whole unit. Andrew received him and followed. Peter, Philip, and Nathanael received him and followed. John 1:12 is what just happened to four men in one week.

Plant it in L3 (1:35-42), the lesson where the first disciples actually receive and follow, and seal it again in L4 when Nathanael believes. Teach its three moves:
- "received him" is what the disciples physically do in this chapter. They come, they stay, they follow.
- "believed in his name" is the confession that grows across the unit, from "Rabbi" to "Son of God, King of Israel."
- "he gave the right to become children of God" is the gift on the far side of receiving. You do not earn your way into the family. He gives the right.

Guard the grace, because 1:13 stands right next to it and Unit 1 already taught it: this birth is "not of blood nor of the will of the flesh nor of the will of man, but of God." Call students to receive him, and give God the credit for the new birth. This protects the payoff in Unit 4, where Jesus says the same thing to Nicodemus. Keep the memorization on the graded-content side and the meaning coached, not scored. Connect it back to Unit 1's verse (1:14).

## Pitfalls with teenagers

- Turning the titles into a quiz. The seven names are angles on one person. If students leave reciting titles but unable to say they all mean Jesus, the lesson missed.
- Draining "Lamb of God" of blood. For the churched kid it is a familiar phrase. Make it a real animal on a real altar until "takes away sin" costs something.
- Making "come and see" a slogan. It is an actual invitation to investigate, aimed at the doubter. Let the searching student hear it as permission to bring questions.
- Over-teaching the Greek and typology. One note on airo ("carries off"), one on the Bethel ladder, one on Son of Man from Daniel 7. That is the whole ration for a 36-minute Thursday and two regular days.
- Weaponizing "Jesus knows you" (1:48). For an anxious teenager, "he sees everything you hide" can land as threat. Lead with the grace: he saw Nathanael honestly and called him anyway. Being fully known by Jesus is safe.
- Making discipleship a personality test. Andrew, Peter, Philip, and Nathanael are different people, a scoffer among them. The lesson is that Jesus called ordinary, mixed people, and they came. Avoid "be bold like Andrew."
- Reverent flatness on L1. The Baptist saying "I am not" four times can read as a dull interview. It is a man refusing the spotlight so he can aim it at someone greater. Read it as drama.

## Also worth emphasizing

- John the Baptist's humility as the model for the whole course. "Among you stands one you do not know" (1:26), "I am not worthy to untie his sandal" (1:27). This is the witness's posture and the teacher's posture in one man. Unit 1 flagged it; this unit shows it in full and hands it to Unit 5's memory line (3:30).
- "I myself did not know him" (1:31, 33). John says twice that he did not recognize Jesus until God showed him at the baptism (the Spirit descending like a dove, 1:32). Even the greatest witness needed God to reveal the Son to him. Recognizing Jesus is a gift, not a talent. Good for the student who thinks faith is for people who are naturally spiritual.
- The Spirit descending and remaining (1:32-33). The Spirit rests on Jesus, and this Jesus "will baptize with the Holy Spirit." Plant it lightly. The course picks up the Spirit in earnest at chapters 3, 7, and 14-16.
- The first question Jesus asks in the Gospel, "What are you seeking?" (1:38). It is the question the course asks every student. Worth one beat, because it turns the passage on the reader.

## The four-lesson split (keep it)

The split on the "next day" seams is right. John marked these scenes with "the next day" (1:29, 1:35, 1:43), so the seams are the text's own, each scene has one center, and each lands on Christ.

- L1 (i=7, Wed, regular 50): 1:19-28, the Baptist's testimony. John says who he is not (not the Christ, not Elijah, not the Prophet) so he can point past himself. Land it on Christ at 1:26-27, "among you stands one you do not know," the greater One already here. Set the witness theme. Note for the builder: this is the least Christ-dense scene on its face, so make L1 land on 1:26-27 and do not let it end as a lesson about John.
- L2 (i=8, Thu, 36-min single-focus): 1:29-34, "Behold, the Lamb of God." One image, the heart of the unit, the cross planted on day one. The Spirit descends and remains, and John testifies "this is the Son of God." A single-focus 36 minutes on the Lamb of God is exactly what a Thursday wants. Lands on: the Lamb who takes away the sin of the world.
- L3 (i=9, Fri, regular 50): 1:35-42, the first disciples follow. John's two disciples follow Jesus, "come and see," Andrew brings Peter, "you shall be called Cephas." Plant John 1:12 here, where receiving him becomes a scene. This is the first half of Adam's sermon 02. Lands on: witnesses bring people to Jesus, and he gives them a new name and a new family.
- L4 (i=10, Mon, regular 50): 1:43-51, Philip and Nathanael. "Follow me," "come and see" again, Jesus knowing Nathanael, the titles resolving in Nathanael's confession, and the Jacob's-ladder saying over the Son of Man. This is the second half of sermon 02 and the climax of the unit. Seal John 1:12. Lands on: Jesus is the ladder between heaven and earth, and being known by him turns a scoffer into a worshiper.

Two notes for the builder. Adam's sermon 02 covers L3 and L4 together, so let its material flow across both full periods rather than compress into one. And L1 and L2 have no sermon of his, so they lean harder on this brief's exegesis, L2's Thursday in particular being a single-focus day on the Lamb.
