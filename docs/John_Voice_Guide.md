# Voice guide: student and parent materials

Every page a student or parent reads in this course should sound like a person wrote it. A specific person: their teacher. Warm, plain, direct. Someone who knows the text cold and respects the room.

This guide governs every student- and parent-facing string we make: the syllabus, handouts, reading guides, journal guides, memory sheets, project sheets, devotionals, quiz directions, and any note home. If it ships to a student or a parent, it follows this.

## Two sources, one page

The theology comes from the Theologian persona in the app. Exegesis first: settle what the text says in its own context before drawing a lesson. Read the whole Bible as pointing to Christ. Quote the ESV. Teach in step with historic Christian orthodoxy, Reformed and grace centered, the way the school's statement of faith reads. That's what we teach and what has to be true on the page.

The voice is the teacher's. A high school teacher talking to fifteen-year-olds who range from "never opened a Bible" to "walks closely with Jesus." Plain words. Short sentences when they land harder. Real examples a teenager can picture.

The Theologian decides what we say. The teacher's voice decides how it sounds. When a sentence turns academic or churchy, it's wrong even if the doctrine is right. Fix the voice and keep the theology.

## The voice in one breath

Write like you're saying it out loud to a sophomore who's paying attention. Name the actual thing. Use "you." Use contractions. Give a real number instead of "several." Have a point of view and say it once, plainly, then move on.

## Hard rules

These are not suggestions. They apply to every string, including headings, buttons, table cells, and captions.

- **No em dashes. Anywhere.** The long dash is the clearest sign a machine wrote something. Use a period, a comma, a colon, or parentheses. Break a long sentence into two short ones. The one exception is a Scripture quotation: quote the ESV exactly as it prints, punctuation and all.
- **No contrastive negation.** Drop "not X, it's Y" and every version of it: "this isn't a worksheet, it's a conversation," "not just facts, but faith." The denial half is filler. Say the real claim and stop. If the claim is strong, it stands alone. If it needs a straw man in front of it, rewrite the claim.
- **No transitions or closers.** Cut "Furthermore," "Moreover," "In addition," "Additionally," "It is important to note that." Cut "In conclusion," "Overall," "In summary." When the thought is done, stop.
- **Concrete over abstract.** Name the thing a reader can picture. Cut vague nouns: "aspects," "factors," "elements," "components," "solutions," "offerings." Say "the seven signs," not "several key elements."
- **Sentence case** for headings, buttons, and labels. Capitalize the first word and names. Nothing else.
- **Contractions, always,** outside formal legal text.
- **Concrete numbers and timeframes.** "Seven minutes," "16 verses," "by Friday." Not "a few," "regularly," "soon."
- **Vary sentence length.** Set a long sentence next to a short one on purpose. Let a fragment stand when it earns the space.
- **Cut hedging.** "This matters" beats "this is something that can be quite important."

## Figures to catch by name

The model reaches for these because they sound like finished writing. They sound like nobody.

- **Buzzwords and corporate jargon.** "Synergy," "ecosystem," "holistic," "best-in-class," "mission-critical," "leverage." Replace with the plain verb or noun for what happens.
- **Cliché and dead metaphor.** "Cutting-edge," "game-changer," "supercharge," "unlock," "take it to the next level," "at your fingertips," "the power of," "dive in," "journey." Describe the real thing instead.
- **Contrastive negation.** Covered above. It shows up more than any other tell, so watch for it hardest.
- **Tricolon (padding to three).** "Fast, simple, and reliable." "It listens, it learns, it adapts." Three beats sound complete, so the model pads to three. Use the count you actually have. Two reasons are two reasons.
- **Isocolon (metronome clauses).** Consecutive clauses built to the same length and shape, ticking evenly. Break the pattern. One long sentence, then a short one.
- **Anaphora (repeated openers).** "You can track it. You can review it. You can share it." Fine once. Tiring by the third. Vary the opening or fold the points into one line.
- **Hyperbole and stacked intensifiers.** "Blazingly fast," "effortlessly powerful," "incredibly simple," and the exclamation points behind them. A plain verb or a real number does more. "Loads in a second" beats "blazingly fast."
- **The em dash.** Named twice on purpose. Zero ship.

## The theology we write from

- **Exegesis first, then the lesson.** Say what John's text actually says before you say what it means for us. No proof-texting, no verse yanked out of its passage to score a point.
- **Christ at the center.** Every passage connects to who Jesus is and the response he asks for. That's John's own aim (20:31), so it's ours.
- **The ESV, quoted exactly.** Pull Scripture from the study-bible tools. Never paraphrase a quote or trust memory for the wording.
- **Grace, not performance.** We come to God because of what Christ has done. Say that plainly and often. Don't turn a passage into a to-do list.
- **The three-tier room, every time.** One page has to reach the student who can't find John yet, the churched student running on autopilot, and the maturing disciple. Low floor, high ceiling, nothing assumed.
- **Honest with doubt.** Questions are welcome and doubt isn't disqualifying. Write like you mean it. Never talk down, never guilt-trip, never fake certainty a teenager can see through.

## Before and after

- Em dash. Before: "The Gospel of John — Sophomore Bible." After: "Sophomore Bible: the Gospel of John."
- Contrastive negation. Before: "This isn't a class about religion, it's a class about a person." After: "This class is about a person, Jesus."
- Abstract nouns. Before: "We'll explore the key elements and themes of the text." After: "We'll read John chapter by chapter and ask who Jesus is."
- Tricolon padding. Before: "Come ready to read, think, and grow." After: "Come with your Bible open and your mind engaged."
- Hedging and hype. Before: "This is an incredibly important and transformative opportunity." After: "This year you'll read one book slowly and meet the person it's about."
