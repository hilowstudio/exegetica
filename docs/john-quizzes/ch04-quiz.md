# John 4 quiz: living water and the word that gives life

> **Course:** Sophomore Bible, the Gospel of John
> **Covers:** John 4:1-54 (Unit 6, "Living water," Lessons 1-5, with the Well Story slides and reading and the Long Feud article, and Unit 7, "Sign 2," Lessons 1-2)
> **Format:** open Bible (ESV). 21 multiple-choice questions at 2 points each, 2 short written answers at 4 points each.
> **Total:** 50 points, about 40 minutes

Review copy with the answer key inline. The correct answer is marked ✓, and the line under each question is the feedback students see once grades are released.

## Instructions (students see this)

This is an open-Bible quiz, so keep your ESV open. Many of the answer choices are things people often say about this chapter. Your job is to find the answer the text itself gives. The choices use plain words, with no quotation marks or verse references, so find the verse named in each question and read it before you choose. The multiple-choice questions are worth 2 points each. The two written answers at the end are worth 4 points each and are graded by hand.

## Part 1: Jacob's well, the long feud, and living water (John 4:1-15)

**1. The woman asks, "How is it that you, a Jew, ask for a drink from me, a woman of Samaria?" John adds, "For Jews have no dealings with Samaritans" (4:9). Think about the history we studied. Why is she surprised?**
- A. People in her town were rude to her, so she didn't expect a stranger to be kind.
- B. The trouble between Jews and Samaritans had started only recently, so she didn't know what to expect from him.
- C. Jews and Samaritans had been enemies for centuries, through rival temples and violence on both sides, and a Jewish man was asking a Samaritan woman for a drink. ✓
- D. She thought a Jewish teacher who spoke to her would be breaking one of the Ten Commandments.

*Feedback: John 4:4, 4:9, and 4:27, with 2 Kings 17:24 and Luke 9:51-56. The hostility went back about seven centuries, to the Assyrian conquest of the north, when the king of Assyria "brought people from Babylon, Cuthah... and placed them in the cities of Samaria" (2 Kings 17:24). After that came rival temples, a Jewish ruler destroying the Samaritan temple on Gerizim, and Samaritans scattering human bones in Jerusalem's temple at Passover while Jesus was a boy. During Jesus' ministry a Samaritan village turned him away, and James and John wanted to call down fire on it (Luke 9:53-54). Even his disciples "marveled that he was talking with a woman" (4:27). Jesus crosses every one of those lines on purpose. John says "he had to pass through Samaria" (4:4). He went to find her.*

**2. The woman asks, "Are you greater than our father Jacob? He gave us the well and drank from it himself" (4:12). John says the well stood near "the field that Jacob had given to his son Joseph" (4:5). Why could a Samaritan call Jacob "our father"?**
- A. The Samaritans traced their family line to Jacob through Joseph's sons, Ephraim and Manasseh, and the well stood on land Jacob gave to Joseph. ✓
- B. Jacob had been born in Samaria and lived there before his family moved to Egypt.
- C. The Samaritans were entirely foreign settlers, so she was claiming a family tie she didn't have.
- D. Every Samaritan had converted to Judaism, so they counted Jacob as their father too.

*Feedback: John 4:5-6 and 4:12, with Genesis 48:5, 22 and 2 Kings 17:24. Jacob adopted Joseph's sons: "Ephraim and Manasseh shall be mine" (Genesis 48:5), and he gave Joseph a portion of land (48:22). When Assyria conquered the north, it brought in foreign settlers, but not everyone was deported. The north became a mix of Israelites who had stayed and newcomers. Jews saw the Samaritans as a mixed people with a corrupted faith, and the Samaritans saw themselves as Joseph's children. Her question is a challenge, and Jesus answers it with the water itself: Jacob's well leaves you thirsty, and the water Jesus gives becomes "a spring of water welling up to eternal life" (4:14). He's greater than the well and the man who dug it.*

**3. Read 4:13-14 side by side. What's the difference between the water in Jacob's well and the water Jesus gives?**
- A. Jesus' water comes from a deeper, cleaner spring, so it's better to drink.
- B. Both kinds satisfy for a while, but Jesus' water lasts a little longer.
- C. Jesus' water makes you stop needing or wanting anything ever again in this life.
- D. The well's water leaves you thirsty again, and the water Jesus gives becomes a spring inside you, welling up to eternal life. ✓

*Feedback: John 4:11-15. "Everyone who drinks of this water will be thirsty again, but whoever drinks of the water that I will give him will never be thirsty again." It becomes "a spring of water welling up to eternal life," inside the person. Option A is what the woman thought he meant: "Where do you get that living water?" (4:11), and then "give me this water, so that I will not... have to come here to draw water" (4:15). She was still thinking about the well. Option C misses that Jesus himself sat down tired and thirsty (4:6). What he promises is a spring that never runs dry, and he doesn't promise a life without needs.*

**4. Jesus calls what he offers "living water" (4:10). Turn to John 7:37-39, where John tells you what the living water is. What is it?**
- A. Happiness and fulfillment in this life, the feeling of finally having what you need.
- B. A life free from problems and suffering for the person who follows Jesus.
- C. The Holy Spirit, and the eternal life he brings to those who believe. ✓
- D. The goodness already inside you, which flows out once you discover it.

*Feedback: John 4:10, 14 and 7:37-39, with Jeremiah 2:13. John explains it himself: "Now this he said about the Spirit, whom those who believed in him were to receive" (7:39). The Spirit is the gift, and the life he gives is eternal: "welling up to eternal life" (4:14). Long before, God had called himself "the fountain of living waters," and grieved that his people had turned to "broken cisterns that can hold no water" (Jeremiah 2:13). Jesus sits at a well and offers himself as that fountain. The water comes from him, so option D has the source backward.*

## Part 2: the well story (John 4 and the Old Testament well scenes)

**5. In class we read three Old Testament well stories: Isaac and Rebekah (Genesis 24), Jacob and Rachel (Genesis 29), and Moses and Zipporah (Exodus 2). When a man meets a woman at a well in those stories, what usually comes next?**
- A. The man fights the local shepherds for the right to the water, and the woman's family drives him away.
- B. Someone draws water, the woman hurries home with the news, the visitor stays and eats, and the two are joined in marriage. ✓
- C. The man receives a vision from God at the well and becomes a prophet to the woman's people.
- D. The woman tests the man with a riddle, and he must answer it before she will give him water.

*Feedback: Genesis 24:28, 67; Genesis 29:10-12; Exodus 2:21. The writers told these stories the same way on purpose, so readers would recognize the pattern: journey, well, water, news, hospitality, joining. Rebekah "ran and told her mother's household" (Genesis 24:28), and "she became his wife" (24:67). Rachel "ran and told her father" (Genesis 29:12). Moses stayed, and the father "gave Moses his daughter Zipporah" (Exodus 2:21). So when Jesus sits down at Jacob's well and a woman comes to draw water (John 4:6-7), a reader who knew Genesis would expect a wedding.*

**6. In the old well stories, one man marries one woman. John has just called Jesus "the bridegroom" (3:29), and John 4 ends with a whole town believing in him (4:39-42). In this well story, who is the bride Jesus comes to win?**
- A. The Samaritan woman herself, whom Jesus would later marry.
- B. Nobody. Jesus never married, so the wedding pattern doesn't apply to him.
- C. Only the Jewish people, since salvation is from the Jews.
- D. A people: Jews, Samaritans, and in time people from every nation who come to him in faith. ✓

*Feedback: John 3:29 and 4:39-42, with Ephesians 5:25 and Revelation 19:7. "The one who has the bride is the bridegroom" (3:29). John follows the old steps, and each one points somewhere bigger. The woman's news isn't "a man is here to marry one of us." It's "Come, see a man who told me all that I ever did" (4:29). The hospitality is two days with the Samaritans (4:40). And the joining is a whole town confessing "the Savior of the world" (4:42). The bride is a people, the one the New Testament calls the church: "Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25), and at the end, "the marriage of the Lamb has come" (Revelation 19:7).*

## Part 3: the woman fully known (John 4:16-19)

**7. In 4:16-18, what does Jesus know about the woman, and how does she respond (4:19)?**
- A. He knows her whole history, that she has had five husbands and the man she has now is not her husband, though she never told him. She says she perceives that he's a prophet. ✓
- B. He guesses from her coming to the well alone at noon that her life is troubled. She says he's a good judge of people.
- C. His disciples had heard about her in town and told him. She's upset that people have been talking about her.
- D. She had told him about her life earlier in the conversation. She says he's a kind listener.

*Feedback: John 4:16-19, with 4:8. She says only, "I have no husband." Jesus tells her the rest: "you have had five husbands, and the one you now have is not your husband." Nobody told him. His disciples were in town buying food (4:8). She answers, "Sir, I perceive that you are a prophet." Later she sums it up for the whole town: "He told me all that I ever did" (4:29).*

**8. Jesus has just named the most painful part of her life out loud, and she leans in and keeps talking with him. Why does being fully known by Jesus draw her toward him?**
- A. She was impressed by his supernatural powers and wanted to see what else he could do.
- B. He named her life without contempt and kept offering her living water. Being completely known by him was safe. ✓
- C. He shamed her enough that she finally felt bad enough to change her life.
- D. She realized she had been a good person underneath it all, and Jesus could see that.

*Feedback: John 4:10, 4:16-19, and 4:28-29. There's no sneer in what Jesus says. He even tells her, "What you have said is true" (4:18). He knows the worst about her, and he's still talking with her and still holding out the living water he offered in 4:10. Being seen all the way through and not thrown away is what she'd been missing. Jesus brings the buried thing into daylight to heal it, and he never uses her shame as a lever. It's the same knowing that saw Nathanael: "Before Philip called you, when you were under the fig tree, I saw you" (1:48). Soon the thing that could have shamed her becomes her invitation to the whole town: "Come, see a man who told me all that I ever did" (4:29).*

## Part 4: worship in spirit and truth (John 4:20-26)

**9. The woman raises the old argument: "Our fathers worshiped on this mountain, but you say that in Jerusalem is the place where people ought to worship" (4:20). The mountain is Gerizim, right above the well, where the Samaritans had their own temple until a Jewish ruler destroyed it, and where they kept worshiping without one. Read 4:21-24. Where does Jesus say true worship happens now?**
- A. No longer bound to a mountain or a city. True worshipers worship the Father in spirit and truth, made possible by the Holy Spirit and shaped by the truth, finally Jesus himself. ✓
- B. In Jerusalem's temple, since the Jews had been right all along about where God lived.
- C. Wherever you feel sincere, since the place and what you believe about God don't really matter.
- D. In church buildings, which would take the place of the temple and the mountain.

*Feedback: John 4:20-24, with 2:19-21, 14:6, and Matthew 15:8-9. Each side could open its own Bible and find its own mountain there: the Samaritans' version of the books of Moses even read "Gerizim" where the Hebrew text has "Ebal." Jesus takes the Jewish side on what God had revealed (4:22), and then he ends the argument: "The hour is coming when neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem will you worship the Father." Jesus won't score the fight. He moves worship from a place to the Spirit and the truth, and to himself, the true temple (2:19-21). "In spirit" means the Holy Spirit makes true worship possible. "In truth" means it's shaped by what's real, and Jesus is the truth (14:6). So option C doesn't work: what you believe about God matters, and a person can have the right words and still have a heart "far from me" (Matthew 15:8). Lead with the good news in 4:23: "the Father is seeking such people to worship him."*

**10. Jesus gives the reason: "God is spirit, and those who worship him must worship in spirit and truth" (4:24). What does "God is spirit" mean for worship?**
- A. God is an impersonal force or energy, so worship means getting in tune with it.
- B. Since God is spirit, what we do with our bodies doesn't matter to him.
- C. God isn't made of matter and isn't stuck in one place, so the worship that fits him isn't locked to one building or mountain. ✓
- D. Since God is spirit, worship is only about feelings and has nothing to do with what you think.

*Feedback: John 4:23-24. "God is spirit" describes who God is, and the description sets worship free. He isn't trapped in a building, a mountain, or your family's way of doing things, so you can worship the Father anywhere the Spirit is at work and the truth, Jesus, is embraced. God is no impersonal force, either: the same verse calls him "the Father," and 4:23 says he "is seeking" worshipers. A force doesn't seek anyone. Worship "in truth" engages what you think, and the Word "became flesh" (1:14), so bodies matter to God.*

**11. Jesus tells the woman, "You worship what you do not know; we worship what we know, for salvation is from the Jews" (4:22). What does he mean that salvation is from the Jews?**
- A. Only Jewish people can be saved.
- B. God gave his covenants, his Scriptures, and the Messiah through Israel, and salvation goes out from them to the whole world. ✓
- C. The Jews were better people than the Samaritans, so God favored them.
- D. The Samaritans could never be saved, because they worshiped at the wrong temple.

*Feedback: John 4:22, with Romans 9:4-5 and 4:39-42. Paul lists what God gave through Israel: "the covenants, the giving of the law, the worship, and the promises," and "from their race, according to the flesh, is the Christ" (Romans 9:4-5). That's why the woman answers with "Messiah" in the next breath (4:25). The verse points outward. Salvation comes from the Jews to the world, and a Samaritan town proves it before the chapter ends: "this is indeed the Savior of the world" (4:42). So options A, C, and D all fail. Jesus doesn't sneer at the Samaritans, and he doesn't erase Israel.*

**12. The woman says, "I know that Messiah is coming... When he comes, he will tell us all things." Jesus answers, "I who speak to you am he" (4:25-26). What is happening here?**
- A. Jesus confirms what she guessed earlier: he's a prophet sent to her people.
- B. Jesus never actually claimed to be the Messiah. His followers said that about him later.
- C. Jesus tells her plainly that he is the Messiah. It's the first time in John that he says it himself, and he says it to a Samaritan woman. ✓
- D. Jesus tells her he's the Messiah, but only after she has shown him that she believes.

*Feedback: John 4:25-26, with Deuteronomy 18:15, 1:41, and 4:19. The Samaritans accepted only the five books of Moses, and they waited for the prophet Moses promised: "The LORD your God will raise up for you a prophet like me" (Deuteronomy 18:15). That's the hope behind her words, "he will tell us all things." Others had called him Messiah already ("We have found the Messiah," 1:41), but here Jesus says it about himself, plainly: "I who speak to you am he." Option B is something people say today, and this verse is Jesus himself making the claim. She had called him a prophet (4:19). He tells her he's more. And notice who hears it first: a Samaritan woman with a hard past, before Jerusalem ever does. She hasn't proved anything to him. He tells her because he came to find her.*

## Part 5: the woman's testimony and the Savior of the world (John 4:27-42)

**13. Read 4:28-30 and 4:39. What does the woman do after talking with Jesus, and what happens?**
- A. She leaves her water jar, goes into town, and tells people, "Come, see a man who told me all that I ever did." Many Samaritans believe in him because of her testimony. ✓
- B. She warns the town to repent quickly, because the Messiah had come to judge them.
- C. She keeps quiet about the conversation, because she's ashamed of what Jesus knew about her.
- D. She preaches a long sermon explaining the prophecies about the Messiah, and the town is persuaded by her arguments.

*Feedback: John 4:28-30 and 4:39, with 1:39 and 1:46. "So the woman left her water jar." She came to the well for water and went away without it, because she'd found the living water. Her whole message is an invitation: "Come, see a man who told me all that I ever did. Can this be the Christ?" It's the same "come and see" we heard in chapter 1 (1:39, 46). The thing that could have shamed her became her testimony, and "many Samaritans from that town believed in him because of the woman's testimony" (4:39).*

**14. At first many believed "because of the woman's testimony" (4:39). Then the townspeople say, "It is no longer because of what you said that we believe, for we have heard for ourselves, and we know that this is indeed the Savior of the world" (4:42). What changed, and what are they claiming?**
- A. They stopped trusting the woman once they found out about her past, so they decided to check Jesus out for themselves.
- B. Their borrowed faith became firsthand faith once they heard Jesus themselves, and they called him the Savior of the whole world. ✓
- C. They believed he was a prophet for the Samaritans, who would save their people from the Jews.
- D. They believed only after he did a great miracle in their town during his two-day stay.

*Feedback: John 4:39-42, with 1:29 and 3:16. Her word was the doorway, and they walked all the way through it to Jesus himself: "many more believed because of his word" (4:41). John records no sign in Samaria at all, so option D is wrong. They believed his word. Then the outsiders name him rightly: "the Savior of the world." That reaches back to "the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world" (1:29) and "God so loved the world" (3:16), and it reaches past Samaria to everyone. The emperor in Rome was called savior of the world. John gives the title to Jesus.*

**15. A Samaritan village once refused to receive Jesus, and James and John asked, "Lord, do you want us to tell fire to come down from heaven and consume them?" (Luke 9:54). Read Acts 8:14-17. What happened later between the apostles and Samaria?**
- A. The apostles stayed away from Samaria to keep peace with the leaders in Jerusalem.
- B. Samaria rejected the apostles' message, just as that village had rejected Jesus.
- C. The Samaritans built a new temple on Mount Gerizim and dedicated it to Jesus.
- D. Samaria received the word of God, and Peter and John came and prayed for the Samaritan believers to receive the Holy Spirit. It was the same John who once wanted to call down fire on them. ✓

*Feedback: Luke 9:51-56, Acts 1:8, and Acts 8:14-17. Jesus rebuked James and John for wanting fire (Luke 9:55). Before he ascended, he sent his apostles to be his witnesses "in Jerusalem and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth" (Acts 1:8). Then "Samaria had received the word of God," and the apostles "sent to them Peter and John, who came down and prayed for them that they might receive the Holy Spirit" (Acts 8:14-15). The living water Jesus offered at Jacob's well was poured out on Samaria. Seven centuries of hostility began to end where Jesus said they would, in the Messiah himself.*

## Part 6: the welcome that wanted a show (John 4:43-48)

**16. "So when he came to Galilee, the Galileans welcomed him, having seen all that he had done in Jerusalem at the feast" (4:45). What was their welcome based on? (Compare 2:23-25.)**
- A. They had finally recognized him as the Son of God and wanted to follow him.
- B. The Samaritans had sent word ahead that he was the Savior of the world.
- C. The signs they had seen him do in Jerusalem. They welcomed the wonder-worker, which isn't the same as trusting him. ✓
- D. They had repented under John the Baptist's preaching and were ready for the Messiah.

*Feedback: John 4:44-45 and 2:23-25. John tells you why they welcomed him: "having seen all that he had done in Jerusalem at the feast." It's the same kind of crowd as 2:23-25, where "many believed in his name when they saw the signs," and "Jesus on his part did not entrust himself to them." A crowd can cheer the miracles and still not hand him their lives. Set it beside the Samaritans one chapter earlier, who believed "because of his word" (4:41) without seeing a single sign.*

**17. A royal official from Capernaum, whose son was at the point of death, begged Jesus to come down and heal him (4:46-47). Jesus answers him, "Unless you see signs and wonders you will not believe" (4:48). In the Greek, "you" is plural. What is Jesus doing?**
- A. Telling the official he shouldn't have asked, because asking God for help shows weak faith.
- B. Refusing to heal the boy, because he was tired of doing miracles for crowds.
- C. Rebuking the official alone for being a rich and powerful man from Herod's court.
- D. Speaking past the man to the whole crowd, naming a faith that needs a show, to lift them toward trusting him. Then he grants the request. ✓

*Feedback: John 4:45-50. The plural "you" reaches the whole sign-hungry crowd of 4:45. Jesus names a faith that only works when there's something to watch. The rebuke is mercy: it's aimed at lifting the father off needing a show and onto trusting Jesus himself. And Jesus grants the request in the very next exchange: "Go; your son will live" (4:50). So option A is wrong. Asking Jesus for help is exactly what this father does, and Jesus answers him.*

## Part 7: take him at his word (John 4:49-54)

**18. The father pleads again, "Sir, come down before my child dies." Jesus says, "Go; your son will live" (4:49-50). What does the father do?**
- A. He insists that Jesus come with him to Capernaum, just to make sure.
- B. He believes the word Jesus spoke to him and starts for home, before he has seen anything. ✓
- C. He stays in Cana with Jesus until a messenger brings news about his son.
- D. He goes home planning to test whether Jesus was right before he decides what to believe.

*Feedback: John 4:49-51. He came asking Jesus to walk to Capernaum. He leaves without him: "The man believed the word that Jesus spoke to him and went on his way" (4:50). He trusts the bare word, with no touch, no journey, and nothing yet to see. That's the turn of the whole story. His servants meet him on the road with the news (4:51).*

**19. Jesus heals a boy he never sees, by a word, from Cana to Capernaum. What does that show about who Jesus is? (Compare 1:1-3 and Psalm 33:9.)**
- A. Jesus is the Word through whom all things were made. His word carries God's own life-giving power, so he speaks and it's done, at any distance. ✓
- B. The father's faith was strong enough to make the healing happen, so the power came from his believing.
- C. Jesus sent his healing power through the father's prayers on the walk home.
- D. Jesus could tell the fever was about to break anyway, and he predicted it.

*Feedback: John 4:50-53 and 1:1-3, with Psalm 33:6, 9 and Isaiah 55:11. "All things were made through him" (1:3). The word that made the world healed the boy. "For he spoke, and it came to be" (Psalm 33:9). The father came with a weak faith that wanted Jesus to come in person, and Jesus healed his son anyway. The power was in Jesus' word, never in the size of anyone's belief, so option B gets it backward. And the timing was exact: the fever left "at the seventh hour," the very hour Jesus said, "Your son will live" (4:52-53).*

**20. Verse 50 says the father "believed the word that Jesus spoke to him." Verse 53 says "he himself believed, and all his household." Why does John show him believing twice?**
- A. Faith is a one-time decision, so John is just repeating the same moment for emphasis.
- B. The father worked hard at believing on the walk home, and his effort earned a stronger faith.
- C. The household believed because the father told them they had to.
- D. His faith grew. He trusted the promise first, then saw it kept at the very hour, and he believed again, with his whole household. ✓

*Feedback: John 4:50-53, with 2:11 and Acts 16:31. He believed, and then he believed again. First he trusted the word with nothing to see. Then he checked the hour, matched it to Jesus' word, and his faith went deeper and spread to his whole house. Christ's word drew that faith out of him; he didn't produce it by effort. That's the normal shape of following Jesus: you trust him, you see him keep his word, and you trust him more. And grace reached a whole household through one man, the way it still does: "Believe in the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved, you and your household" (Acts 16:31).*

**21. "This was now the second sign that Jesus did when he had come from Judea to Galilee" (4:54). John also notes this happened at Cana, "where he had made the water wine" (4:46). Why does John point that out?**
- A. Cana was a holy place, so Jesus' miracles worked better there.
- B. Jesus had done only two miracles in his whole life up to this point.
- C. The two signs at Cana bracket chapters 2-4. Each shows who Jesus is, his glory at the wedding and life given by his word, and each leads people to believe. ✓
- D. Jesus did miracles only in Galilee and never in Judea.

*Feedback: John 2:11, 2:23, and 4:46-54. The first sign "manifested his glory. And his disciples believed in him" (2:11). The second gives life by a word, and "he himself believed, and all his household" (4:53). Both at Cana, they open and close this part of the book. Options B and D can't be right: John says Jesus did signs in Jerusalem at the Passover (2:23), and the Galileans had seen them (4:45). This is the second sign "when he had come from Judea to Galilee." John writes them all for one reason: "so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name" (20:31).*

## Part 8: written answers (4 points each, graded by hand)

**22. John 4 follows the Old Testament well-story pattern and then changes it. Write (1) one Old Testament well story we studied and how it ends, with a reference, (2) two steps of the pattern that John 4 follows, with a verse for each, (3) how John 4 ends differently, with a verse, and (4) what that ending shows you about who Jesus is.**

*Feedback: A full answer names an Old Testament well story and its wedding, matches two steps of the pattern to verses in John 4, shows that John 4 ends with a whole town believing in Jesus instead of one wedding, and says what that shows about who Jesus is.*

Rubric: 1 point for an Old Testament well story and its ending, with a reference (Isaac and Rebekah, Genesis 24:67; Jacob and Rachel, Genesis 29:21-28; Moses and Zipporah, Exodus 2:21). 1 point for two steps of the pattern in John 4, each with a verse (journey, 4:3-4; well, 4:5-7; water, 4:7, 10-14; news, 4:28-29; hospitality, 4:31-34 or 4:40). 1 point for the different ending, with a verse (a whole town believes, 4:39-42). 1 point for a clear statement of who Jesus is (the bridegroom, 3:29, come to win a people for himself; the Savior of the world, 4:42).

Model answer: In Genesis 24, Abraham's servant finds Rebekah at a well, she runs home with the news, and the story ends when "she became his wife" (Genesis 24:67). John 4 follows the same steps. Jesus makes a journey through Samaria (4:3-4) and sits at Jacob's well when a woman comes to draw water (4:6-7). She hurries into town with the news (4:28-29). But instead of a wedding, it ends with a whole town believing: "we know that this is indeed the Savior of the world" (4:42). That shows Jesus is the true bridegroom (3:29), and the bride he came to win is a people, Jews, Samaritans, and everyone who comes to him in faith.

**23. The royal official's faith grows in this story. Write (1) what he first asked Jesus to do, with a verse, (2) what Jesus said and what the man did about it, with a verse, (3) how his faith grew by the end, with a verse, and (4) what the healing shows you about who Jesus is.**

*Feedback: A full answer traces the man from asking Jesus to come in person, to trusting Jesus' word and going home, to believing again with his household once he learned the hour, and says what the healing shows about who Jesus is.*

Rubric: 1 point for the first request, with a verse (to come down and heal his son, 4:47 or 4:49). 1 point for Jesus' word and the man's response, with a verse ("Go; your son will live," and he believed the word and went, 4:50). 1 point for the growth, with a verse (the fever left at the hour Jesus spoke, and "he himself believed, and all his household," 4:52-53). 1 point for a clear statement of who Jesus is (his word has God's power to give life, at any distance; the Word through whom all things were made). Give no credit for "the man's strong faith healed his son."

Model answer: At first the official asked Jesus "to come down and heal his son" (4:47), and he asked again, "Sir, come down before my child dies" (4:49). Jesus didn't go. He said, "Go; your son will live," and "the man believed the word that Jesus spoke to him and went on his way" (4:50). On the road he learned his son got better at the very hour Jesus spoke, and "he himself believed, and all his household" (4:53). The healing shows that Jesus' word has God's own power to give life, even from miles away, because he is the Word through whom everything was made.

## Teacher notes

**Sources.** Along with the seven lesson guides, the quiz draws on the Unit 6 class materials: "The Woman at the Well" reading and "The Well Story" slides (the six-step well pattern, the three Old Testament well stories, and the twist where a whole town is joined to the bridegroom) in Q5, Q6, and Q22, and the article "The Long Feud: Jews and Samaritans" in Q1, Q2, Q9, Q12, and Q15. History from the article sits in stems and feedback. Students only need the history you taught to answer Q1, Q2, and Q15, and each stem points them back to the verse.

**Answer choices and references.** No answer choice carries a verse reference or quotation marks, and the instructions tell students so. Correct answers put John's words in plain language, so students have to read the verse to see which choice says what John says. Quotations live in the stems and the feedback. Right answers vary in length. The key is balanced: A five times, B five, C six, D five. Forms shuffles the options, so the balance matters for the printed copy.

**How the wrong answers work.** Every wrong answer is something people believe or easily assume, and none of them is a defensible reading of the verse in the stem. Several come from the text misread: Q3 A is the woman's own literal reading of the water (4:11, 15). Q2 C is the old Jewish view pushed too far: the article shows the north was a mix of Israelites who stayed and settlers who came, so "entirely foreign" is wrong. Where the beliefs come from:

**Memory-verse alignment.** Unit 6 plants 4:24 on Lesson 4, and Unit 7 is review-only. The quiz doesn't ask anyone to recite 4:24, since the memory work is graded on its own line and the quiz is open Bible. It does lean on 4:24 in Q9 and Q10, and Q21's feedback closes on 20:31, so going over the quiz is a review session for both. John 4:50 is the optional off-line stretch for Unit 7. Q18 teaches it and doesn't ask anyone to recite it.

**Grading.** Grade the written answers for meaning and accurate references, the way you grade memory verses for sense. Content is graded and belief stays invitational: both written questions ask what the text says, so any student can earn full marks without being asked to profess anything. On Q22, accept any of the three Old Testament well stories and any two steps matched correctly to John 4. Q23 gives no credit for "his strong faith healed his son," which is the unit's big pitfall.

**Timing.** Twenty-one open-Bible questions plus two written answers make 40 minutes tight. Q4 sends students to 7:37-39 and Q15 to Acts 8:14-17, Q22 asks them to recall a well story from Genesis or Exodus, and several stems are long (Q1, Q2, Q9, Q15). Watch the time the first time you give it.

**Handle with care when you go over it.** On the five husbands (Q7, Q8): the text tells us her history and nothing about how the marriages ended, and the quiz says no more than the text. No option calls her names, and none should come up in discussion. On the feud (Q1, Q2, Q15): the history has wrongs on both sides, so keep it that way when you go over it. Keep the year's anti-Judaism guard on Q11: salvation comes from the Jews outward to the world, Jesus is a Jew, and the Samaritan town is the proof that it reaches everyone. On Q6 option A, a student may have met the claim that Jesus married; the feedback stays on the text, where the joining is a whole town. On Q17 and Q19, watch the student whose prayer for healing got a no or a not-yet. The sign shows who Jesus is, and it isn't a formula for how every prayer will be answered. Sermon 05's Father-first frame helps here: Jesus serves the Father's will, and our good is wrapped inside it. On Q9 option D, the problem is "take the place of." Gathering as a church matters. The option is wrong because Jesus moves worship from a place to the Spirit and the truth, and to himself.

**For going over the quiz.** On Q1, the article notes that the verb in 4:9 (synchraomai) appears only here in the New Testament, and some scholars read it as "use vessels in common," so she may be surprised that a Jewish man would drink from her jar. Either reading lands on the same answer. Also on Q1, "he had to pass through Samaria" (4:4) uses the same "must" as "so must the Son of Man be lifted up" (3:14). On Q3, point out that the Giver of living water sat down "wearied" from his journey and asked a woman for a drink (4:6), and that he says "I thirst" again at the cross (19:28). On Q5, the reading's Eden connection (the river in Genesis 2:10-14) is good depth for the disciple tier. On Q12, the disciple tier can hear "I am he" (ego eimi) echoing toward 8:24: "unless you believe that I am he you will die in your sins." On Q14, the harvest saying (4:35-38) is worth reading aloud as the town walks out to him: the fields are "white for harvest," and it's God's harvest. On Q15, the enemies' insult in 8:48 ("you are a Samaritan and have a demon") shows how deep the feud ran. On Q17, Chrysostom's read helps: Jesus rebukes the state of mind the man came with, and the rebuke draws him on to belief. On Q20, the student who worries a wobble means their faith was never real can see the father's two believings in a single afternoon.

**Coverage.** Every question maps to a content objective from the seven lessons or to the Unit 6 class materials. Unit 6 Lesson 3 was a recap and memory day, so it has no items of its own. Not quizzed as items of their own: reciting 4:24 (graded on the memory line), the harvest saying (in Q14's notes above), who the official is (in Q17's stem), and the ego eimi resonance (disciple tier).
